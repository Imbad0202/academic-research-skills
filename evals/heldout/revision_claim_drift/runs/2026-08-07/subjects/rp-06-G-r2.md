The fictional CivicStream Panel followed 1,126 users for 14 weeks and captured 8,904 discussion-thread visits. Exposure to cross-cutting replies was associated with a 0.31-point increase in subsequent viewpoint diversity (95% CI [0.18, 0.44], p < .001), while hostile reply tone predicted lower return engagement (b = -0.22, SE = 0.06, p = .002). The findings suggest that conversational structure and tone may influence distinct forms of participation.

Prior scholarship converges on three features of networked discussion that bear on participation: what people are exposed to, how they judge what they see, and the norms that govern interaction. On the exposure side, algorithmic feed concentration can increase selective exposure (Rahmani, 2018), while visible identity cues can increase willingness to share contentious material (Okeke & Vidal, 2020). On the credibility side, correction timing may shape belief revision after misinformation exposure (Sato, 2019), and source familiarity can improve message recall without improving accuracy judgments (Dlamini & Kroll, 2021). Interaction norms complete the account: thread civility has been associated with return participation (Petrescu, 2022), and response diversity may signal that disagreement is socially acceptable (Al-Masri & Ko, 2023). Taken together, these propositions are consistent with a layered account in which exposure, credibility, and interaction norms operate through related but nonidentical pathways, an account that maps onto the present pattern of results: exposure to cross-cutting replies was associated with subsequent viewpoint diversity, whereas hostile tone predicted lower return engagement.
