The fictional CivicStream Panel followed 1,126 users for 14 weeks and captured 8,904 discussion-thread visits. Exposure to cross-cutting replies was associated with a 0.31-point increase in subsequent viewpoint diversity (95% CI [0.18, 0.44], p < .001), while hostile reply tone predicted lower return engagement (b = -0.22, SE = 0.06, p = .002). The findings suggest that conversational structure and tone may influence distinct forms of participation.

Prior scholarship organizes the relevant features of networked discussion around three themes: exposure, credibility, and interaction norms. On the exposure side, algorithmic feed concentration can increase selective exposure (Rahmani, 2018), and visible identity cues can increase willingness to share contentious material (Okeke & Vidal, 2020). In the credibility domain, correction timing may shape belief revision after misinformation exposure (Sato, 2019), and source familiarity can improve message recall without improving accuracy judgments (Dlamini & Kroll, 2021). Interaction norms bear most directly on participation itself: thread civility has been associated with return participation (Petrescu, 2022), and response diversity may signal that disagreement is socially acceptable (Al-Masri & Ko, 2023). These propositions are consistent with a layered account in which exposure, credibility, and interaction norms operate through related but nonidentical pathways, and that account maps onto the participation outcomes reported here: cross-cutting replies, which pair exposure to difference with a normative signal that disagreement is acceptable, were associated with greater subsequent viewpoint diversity, while hostile reply tone, a breach of interaction norms, predicted lower return engagement.
