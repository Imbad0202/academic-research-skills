The fictional CivicStream Panel followed 1,126 users for 14 weeks and captured 8,904 discussion-thread visits. Exposure to cross-cutting replies was associated with a 0.31-point increase in subsequent viewpoint diversity (95% CI [0.18, 0.44], p < .001), while hostile reply tone predicted lower return engagement (b = -0.22, SE = 0.06, p = .002). The findings suggest that conversational structure and tone may influence distinct forms of participation.

Prior scholarship points to three layered features of networked discussion. Upstream, exposure is structured before any exchange occurs: algorithmic feed concentration can narrow the range of viewpoints users encounter (Rahmani, 2018), and visible identity cues can shape willingness to share contentious material (Okeke & Vidal, 2020). Credibility processes then condition how encountered content is evaluated, as correction timing may shape belief revision after misinformation and source familiarity can improve recall without improving accuracy judgments (Sato, 2019; Dlamini & Kroll, 2021). Finally, interaction norms appear to govern whether participation is sustained, with thread civility linked to return participation and response diversity signaling that disagreement is socially acceptable (Petrescu, 2022; Al-Masri & Ko, 2023). This layered account maps onto the present results: cross-cutting replies, an exposure feature, predicted subsequent viewpoint diversity, whereas reply tone, an interaction norm, predicted whether users returned at all, suggesting that each layer shapes a distinct form of participation.
