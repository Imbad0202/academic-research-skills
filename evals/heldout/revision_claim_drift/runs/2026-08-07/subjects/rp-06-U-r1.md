The fictional CivicStream Panel followed 1,126 users for 14 weeks and captured 8,904 discussion-thread visits. Exposure to cross-cutting replies was associated with a 0.31-point increase in subsequent viewpoint diversity (95% CI [0.18, 0.44], p < .001), while hostile reply tone predicted lower return engagement (b = -0.22, SE = 0.06, p = .002). The findings suggest that conversational structure and tone may influence distinct forms of participation.

Prior scholarship points to three mechanisms through which networked discussion shapes participation: exposure, credibility, and interaction norms. On the exposure side, algorithmic feed concentration narrows the range of viewpoints users encounter (Rahmani, 2018), while visible identity cues broaden willingness to share contentious material (Okeke & Vidal, 2020). Credibility processes operate in parallel: correction timing shapes belief revision after misinformation (Sato, 2019), and source familiarity improves recall without improving accuracy judgments (Dlamini & Kroll, 2021). Interaction norms complete the account, as thread civility supports return participation (Petrescu, 2022) and response diversity signals that disagreement is socially acceptable (Al-Masri & Ko, 2023). This layered framework maps directly onto the present results: cross-cutting replies appear to work through the exposure pathway to broaden subsequent viewpoint diversity, whereas hostile tone appears to work through the norms pathway to depress return engagement, indicating that related but nonidentical mechanisms govern distinct forms of participation.
