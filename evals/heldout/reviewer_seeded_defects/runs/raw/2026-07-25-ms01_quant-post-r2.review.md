## Review Strategy Recommendations

**Special characteristics of this paper requiring particular attention**

1. **The manuscript argues against itself, and this is the defining feature of the review.** Section 1 promises to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story"; Section 2 cites Ibarra (2023) specifically on causal language outrunning evidence; Section 3.1 states the design is cross-sectional. Then Section 5 opens with "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and asserts that increasing engagement "raises the probability" of completion, and Section 6 escalates to a worldwide institutional recommendation. Reviewers should not be allowed to treat this as a wording problem. The manuscript demonstrates that the author knows the correct epistemic standard and did not apply it, which changes the character of the required revision from "soften the language" to "reconstruct the argument the data support."

2. **The numeric layer must be checked, not assumed.** The Abstract's r = .42 and the Results' r = .24 cannot both be the study's primary finding. The perceived-control t-test reports df = 156 for a comparison the Methods restrict to the 87 students who answered the item. Table 2's group sizes (66 + 61 = 127) do not match the "all 142 students" the adjacent text claims, nor the df = 140 of the accompanying test. A t of 1.31 at df = 140 is described as not reaching "a comparable level" while carrying p = .008. Reviewer 1 must be instructed to reconstruct rather than restate these, and every downstream reviewer's substantive judgment should be conditioned on what that reconstruction finds. No reviewer should build an interpretive argument on a number that has not survived Reviewer 1's pass.

3. **Two disclosure-level issues sit outside ordinary methodological critique** and should be routed deliberately rather than left to whoever notices first: the un-notified analysis of behavioral trace data with no ethics statement (Reviewer 3), and a reference list in which every DOI carries a placeholder prefix, 9 of 15 entries are never cited, and at least one cited claim appears to invert its source (Reviewer 2). Both are verification requests to the author in the first instance. Reviewers should be instructed to state the specific evidence and the specific clarification sought, and to name what answer would resolve it — not to speculate about cause.

4. **Register**: the manuscript is a competent revised draft with real defects, not an incompetent one. The prose is disciplined, the Limitations section is honest about three genuine constraints, and the Literature Review shows command of the field's methodological critiques. Reviewers should use a developmental register — the study could become publishable as a scoped correlational contribution. Per the standing rule, the register governs wording only; the recommendation itself must follow from the evidence against the criteria, and a developmental tone must not soften a verdict that the numeric and disclosure findings warrant.

**Anticipated complementarity and tension between reviewers**

- **Reviewer 1 ↔ Reviewer 2 (expected divergence in severity).** Reviewer 1 will likely conclude that the analysis as described cannot support inference about retention at all, given the mid-term recruitment window truncating the outcome. Reviewer 2 is more likely to find the study salvageable if rescoped. This is the panel's most productive tension and the synthesizer should preserve it rather than average it: the disagreement is really about whether the selection problem is fatal or bounded, and forcing that question into the open is more useful to the author than a blended verdict.

- **Reviewer 1 ↔ Reviewer 3 (complementary, non-overlapping).** Reviewer 1 owns whether the numbers are internally possible; Reviewer 3 owns whether the study should have been run and whether its recommendation is actionable. Neither should stray into the other's territory. Note the hidden dependency: Reviewer 3's assessment of the worldwide-deployment claim is strengthened considerably if Reviewer 1 confirms the effect size discrepancy, since an institution acting on r = .42 and one acting on r = .24 face materially different cost-benefit arithmetic. The synthesizer should sequence Reviewer 1's findings into Reviewer 3's input where possible.

- **Reviewer 2 ↔ Reviewer 3 (shared ground on equity, different lens).** Both will engage the Ferro & Nakamura / Osei demotivation thread — Reviewer 2 as a question of whether the literature is represented faithfully, Reviewer 3 as a question of whether the deployed peer-comparison band harmed students. Assign the citation-fidelity determination exclusively to Reviewer 2 to prevent duplicate findings; Reviewer 3 should take Reviewer 2's determination as given and reason forward to deployment consequences.

- **EIC ↔ panel (calibration risk).** The EIC's fit-and-framing verdict will be the harshest-sounding but rests on the least independent verification. The synthesizer should require the EIC's judgment to cite at least one confirmed finding from Reviewers 1–3 before it carries weight in the final recommendation, so that "overclaiming" is anchored to the r = .42/.24 discrepancy and the causal-language reversal rather than to editorial impression.

- **Collective blind spot to compensate for.** All four configurations are oriented toward what is wrong. None is configured to identify what is salvageable. The synthesizer should explicitly extract, across the four reports, the components that survive scrutiny — the honest Limitations section, the field-aware Literature Review, the acknowledged median-split tradeoff, the transparent statement that the design is cross-sectional — and ensure the final report tells the author which parts of the manuscript to keep.

## Contract Paraphrase

**D1 — methodology_rigor (mandatory).** From the editor's chair, this dimension asks whether the study's design, data handling, and reporting are strong enough that the paper's headline claim can survive peer scrutiny at this venue. I am not auditing estimator choice or code line-by-line — that is the methodology seat's work. My oversight question is narrower and more strategic: does the design as described support the grade of claim the title makes? A title pairing dashboard engagement with course retention promises a relationship between an optional student-facing tool and an institutional outcome, and my job is to check that the reported design, sample framing, outcome definition, and reproducibility affordances are stated at all and are commensurate with that promise. Silence on the pieces a reader needs to judge the claim is, for me, a rigor problem even when the analysis that is shown looks clean.

**D2 — domain_accuracy (mandatory).** This dimension asks whether the paper is a trustworthy citizen of the learning-analytics literature. Editorially, that means three things: the domain's constructs are used with their accepted meanings rather than redefined silently; prior work — including the substantial existing body on dashboard efficacy and early-alert systems — is represented as that literature actually stands, neither strawmanned nor inflated into a gap that does not exist; and the technical and institutional vocabulary of the field, along with any reported results attributed to others, is factually correct. My particular sensitivity here is the originality claim, because a paper that misreads what is already known has usually also mislocated its own contribution.

**D3 — argumentative_coherence (mandatory).** This is the spine I care most about as EIC: whether the chain running title → abstract → research question → evidence → conclusion holds together without a break. The specific failure I watch for in a paper of this shape is the drift between an associational design and a causal or prescriptive conclusion — the point where "students who used the dashboard persisted more" quietly becomes "the dashboard improved retention" or "institutions should deploy dashboards." Coherence for me also covers whether the conclusion answers the question actually posed, whether stated limitations are honoured in the implications rather than parked and forgotten, and whether the paper over-promises in its framing relative to what it delivers.

**D4 — cross_disciplinary_relevance (high).** The paper sits at an intersection — learning analytics and educational technology on one side, higher education research on the other — and its readership will include people fluent in only one of those. This dimension asks whether the framing, definitions, and implications are legible to an adjacent-field reader: whether field-specific terms are defined on first use, whether the institutional outcome is specified in a way a higher-education researcher would recognise as well-posed, and whether any claim that reaches across the boundary (about learning theory, student behaviour, or institutional policy) is actually substantiated rather than asserted by disciplinary courtesy. This is high priority rather than mandatory, but under the contract a block here still drives a major-revision outcome, so I score it seriously.

**D5 — writing_and_structure (normal).** This dimension covers organisation, clarity of exposition, quality and necessity of figures and tables, and conformity to venue conventions. Its priority is normal and no failure condition in this contract keys on it, so it functions as a quality signal rather than a decision lever for me — I will report what I find and let it inform the overall recommendation without letting presentational polish substitute for, or contaminate, my judgment on D1–D3. At the stated length of 2,487 words the structural question is specifically one of allocation: whether a compressed manuscript spends its words on the load-bearing parts of the argument.

## Scoring Plan

### D1: methodology_rigor
- **what_to_look_for**: Whether the design is named and matched to the claim (observational/correlational vs. quasi-experimental vs. randomised); whether the analytic sample, deployment setting, and time window are stated; how "dashboard engagement" is operationalised into a measurable exposure and how "retention" is defined as an outcome (course completion? re-enrolment? which census point?); whether the obvious confounding structure of a voluntary tool — prior attainment, motivation, self-selection into use — is acknowledged and addressed by some stated means; whether effect estimates carry uncertainty (intervals, standard errors, model specification) rather than appearing as bare point values or percentages; whether reproducibility affordances exist at all (data availability, analysis code, instrument description, ethics/consent handling for student records).
- **what_triggers_block**: The paper reports a relationship between engagement and retention while leaving at least one load-bearing element of the design undefined or unreported — no stated outcome definition, no stated sample or setting, or no analytic approach given — such that a reader cannot in principle judge whether the reported relationship means anything; **or** the design is stated and is structurally incapable of supporting the claim the paper actually draws (e.g., a single-group post-hoc comparison of users vs. non-users presented as evidence that the dashboard produces retention) with no acknowledgement of that gap; **or** student data are analysed with no mention of ethics approval, consent, or institutional data-governance basis where the venue's conventions require it.
- **what_triggers_warn**: The design is adequate for an associational claim and is reported, but material rigor affordances are thin — confounders named in prose yet never handled analytically, uncertainty reported inconsistently or only for some estimates, no data/code availability statement, sample attrition or missing-data handling unexplained, or the exposure measure (what counts as "engagement") defined loosely enough that a replicating team would have to guess. In short: the reader can evaluate the claim, but only by extending the authors more credit than the manuscript earns.

### D2: domain_accuracy
- **what_to_look_for**: Whether the constructs central to this literature — engagement, at-risk identification, retention/persistence, self-regulated learning — are used in their established senses or redefined without notice; whether the review of prior dashboard and early-alert work reflects the field's actual state, including its well-documented mixed and null findings, rather than a uniformly positive or uniformly barren picture; whether cited studies are characterised accurately in what they found and at what scope; whether the claimed gap is real given that prior work; whether technical descriptions of the platform, its data sources, and its indicators are internally accurate and use the field's terminology correctly; whether any comparison to published effect sizes or benchmark rates is stated correctly.
- **what_triggers_block**: A factual misrepresentation of prior work that the paper's contribution depends on — the gap claim rests on asserting that something has not been studied when the cited literature itself shows otherwise, or a cited source is characterised as finding the opposite of what it found; **or** a core domain construct is used incorrectly in a way that changes the meaning of the results (e.g., a retention figure that is actually a course-pass rate, or an engagement metric described as something the underlying data cannot represent).
- **what_triggers_warn**: The domain grounding is broadly correct but dated, thin, or one-sided — a literature base that omits the field's contrary findings, terminology used imprecisely without changing the result's meaning, prior effect magnitudes invoked loosely, or a novelty claim that is defensible but overstated in degree rather than false in kind.

### D3: argumentative_coherence
- **what_to_look_for**: Whether the research question is stated explicitly and whether the conclusion answers *that* question; whether the verb grammar stays consistent across title, abstract, results, and discussion, or escalates from association to effect, causation, or prescription somewhere along the way; whether the implications drawn for institutions or practitioners are proportionate to the evidence class actually presented; whether stated limitations are genuinely honoured downstream or contradicted by the paper's own recommendations; whether every claim in the abstract has a corresponding piece of evidence in the body; whether the paper's own framing of its contribution matches what it delivers.
- **what_triggers_block**: A break in the spine that would mislead a reader who trusted the abstract — the central conclusion, headline framing, or practice recommendation asserts causal impact or institutional efficacy that the paper's own reported evidence class cannot support, and the manuscript does not mark the inference as provisional; **or** the conclusion answers a different question than the one posed; **or** the paper states a limitation that logically voids its main claim and then proceeds to make that claim anyway.
- **what_triggers_warn**: The overall argument holds but leaks at the seams — occasional causal-sounding language in the discussion that the results section does not license, an implications passage that reaches somewhat further than the design allows, an abstract claim that is supported in the body but stated more confidently there, or a limitations section that is present and honest yet perfunctory relative to the threats the design plainly carries.

### D4: cross_disciplinary_relevance
- **what_to_look_for**: Whether learning-analytics vocabulary and platform-specific detail are defined or glossed for a higher-education researcher who does not work with clickstream data, and conversely whether institutional-retention concepts are specified precisely enough for a technology-oriented reader; whether the institutional context is described sufficiently for a reader elsewhere to judge transferability (institution type, course type, cohort, national setting) or is treated as self-evident; whether any claim that crosses disciplinary lines — about learning behaviour, student motivation, or institutional policy — is supported by cited evidence or by the study's own data rather than asserted; whether the stated implications name an audience the venue's readership actually contains.
- **what_triggers_block**: The paper's contribution is unreadable or unusable outside its immediate sub-community — core terms and metrics are never defined, the deployment context is so unspecified that no adjacent-field reader can assess relevance to any other setting, **or** an interdisciplinary claim is load-bearing for the conclusion (a policy or pedagogical recommendation) and rests on no cited or reported evidence at all.
- **what_triggers_warn**: The paper is intelligible across the boundary but not hospitable — jargon defined late or unevenly, contextual detail present but too sparse to gauge transferability, implications addressed generically to "institutions" without engaging what would differ elsewhere, or a cross-boundary claim supported thinly by a single source doing more work than it can carry.

### D5: writing_and_structure
- **what_to_look_for**: Whether the section architecture is conventional and complete for an empirical report, and whether the ~2,487-word budget is allocated to the load-bearing parts (design, measures, results, limitations) or spent disproportionately on framing; whether the abstract is a faithful precis rather than a promotional one; whether tables and figures are necessary, self-contained, and properly captioned, with numbers that agree with the text; whether the title reflects the paper's actual scope; whether citation style and reporting conventions are applied consistently; whether prose is clear enough that ambiguity in the results is the reader's problem and not the sentence's.
- **what_triggers_block**: Presentation failure severe enough to obstruct review itself — an essential section missing outright, figures or tables whose values contradict the text, or exposition so disorganised that the study's design and findings cannot be reconstructed on a careful reading. (Recorded as a quality signal: under this contract a block on a normal-priority dimension fires no failure condition on its own.)
- **what_triggers_warn**: Ordinary presentational shortfalls that a revision would fix — uneven section balance or a methods section starved by the word budget, an abstract that oversells relative to the body, redundant or under-labelled exhibits, inconsistent citation formatting, or passages of avoidable vagueness that force the reader to infer what was meant.

### Summary Assessment

This manuscript reports a single-term, single-course observational study (n = 142, introductory statistics, one institution) relating LMS-logged dashboard sessions to a dichotomous course-completion outcome, supplemented by a single-item perceived-control measure from 87 respondents. The correlational finding is modest (r = .24 in Results) and, scoped honestly, would be a legitimate if minor contribution to a literature this journal publishes.

The manuscript does not scope it honestly, and that is the defining problem. Section 1 commits to distinguishing "the pattern in the data from the causal story"; Section 2 cites Ibarra (2023) precisely on causal language outrunning evidence; Section 3.1 declares the design cross-sectional. Section 5 then opens with "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and asserts that increasing engagement "raises the probability" of completion, and Section 6 recommends dashboards to institutions "worldwide" as a "dependable strategy" and a "generalizable lever." The author demonstrably knows the correct epistemic standard and did not apply it. This is not a wording problem to be fixed by softening verbs; the argument the data support has to be reconstructed.

Compounding this, the Abstract advertises r = .42 where the Results report r = .24, and claims measurement of "self-regulated learning behavior" that the Methods never describe. For an editor, an abstract that misstates its own study's primary effect size is an integrity matter, not a copy-edit. ### Detailed Comments

#### Journal Fit

Topically this is a good fit. *JLA* has a sustained line of work on student-facing dashboards and an editorial culture that welcomes honest correlational work when the claims are scoped to match — this is the journal most likely to give the author field-appropriate correction rather than a flat desk reject. Two things stand between the manuscript and that fit. First, this journal's readership includes the methodological-audit tradition the manuscript itself cites (Ibarra, 2023); publishing a paper that commits the exact error that literature documents would be indefensible. Second, this journal presses hard on the consent question, and the manuscript currently offers nothing on it. Fit is real; readiness is not. If the author is unwilling to strengthen the design beyond its cross-sectional form, *Australasian Journal of Educational Technology* or *Journal of Computing in Higher Education* would be a realistic home for a rescoped version, and *The Internet and Higher Education* would suit a version with covariate adjustment and the causal language removed.

#### Originality

Weak, and weak by the manuscript's own accounting. §2 states that "Much dashboard research relies on cross-sectional or correlational designs" — and then delivers a cross-sectional correlational study. The Introduction's gap claim ("more enthusiasm than evidence") is defensible in the abstract but is not what this study addresses: it adds another single-site correlation to a pile the manuscript has just described as already large. The genuinely novel move available here — linking dashboard behavior to a downstream academic outcome with the selection problem taken seriously — is precisely the move not made. What would make this publishable is not a bigger claim but a smaller, cleaner one: an honest report of what a single gateway course's trace data can and cannot tell an institution, with the selection structure characterized rather than ignored.

#### Significance

Low as currently framed, and potentially negative. If the conclusions held, institutions would have a cheap retention lever; that is why the framing is attractive and why it must be resisted. On the evidence presented, a system administrator is authorized to do nothing. The recommendation asks institutions to expand peer-comparison dashboards on the strength of an r of either .24 or .42 (the manuscript does not say which), measured in one section of one course over one term, on a sample that structurally excludes the students who left. A rescoped version would have modest but real significance as a data point in a literature that needs more honest ones.

#### Structural Coherence

This is where the manuscript fails hardest. The spine breaks between §3 and §5, and the break is not a slip of the pen — §1 states the correct standard, §2 cites the paper that enforces it, §3.1 declares the design that constrains it, and §5 violates all three. The Limitations section makes the break sharper rather than softer: it names three real constraints and omits the two (self-selection, causal identification) that would have blocked the conclusion. A reader who trusted the Abstract and Conclusion would come away with a materially false understanding of what was shown. This is the finding that governs my recommendation, and it is why the required revision is a reconstruction of the argument rather than a softening of verbs.

#### Title & Abstract

The title is the manuscript's most accurate sentence: "Dashboard Engagement and Course Retention" is correctly associational, and "Evidence from an Undergraduate Learning Analytics Deployment" correctly signals scope. Keep it. The Abstract does not live up to it. It reports an effect size the Results contradict, claims a measure that does not exist, and closes on "a promising lever for improving retention" — a prescriptive register the study cannot fund. At ~155 words it is also unstructured where this venue expects structure. The Abstract needs rebuilding around the numbers the Results actually report.

#### Conclusion

The Conclusion is the least defensible section in the manuscript and needs to be rewritten from nothing. "Associated with, and raises" is a causal claim wearing an associational prefix. "Dependable strategy," "worldwide," "across programs and disciplines," "practical and generalizable lever" — each of these is contradicted by §5.1 on the preceding page. There is a good conclusion available in the material already present: §5, ¶3's reading that dashboards may "help at the margin, nudging some students toward completion without transforming the underlying determinants of academic success." That paragraph, extended and stated as the study's actual finding, would be a conclusion an editor could stand behind.

### Questions for Authors

1. Which is the study's primary effect size — the r = .42 reported in the Abstract or the r = .24 reported in §4.2 — and how did the discrepancy arise?
2. What body approved this study, under what protocol, and on what basis was student notification of the trace-data analysis waived? If no approval or waiver was obtained, please state so directly.
3. §3.2 describes both "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" and a voluntary response to a mid-term LMS announcement. Which governs? What was the response rate against total enrollment?
4. How many students withdrew before the mid-term recruitment window, and how does their exclusion bear on an outcome defined as completing the final assessment?
5. Which specific finding in Ferro & Nakamura (2021) supports the sentence "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," given the source's title?
6. Please supply resolvable DOIs for all 15 references, and either cite or remove the 9 entries that never appear in the text.
7. Please reconcile Table 2 (n = 66 + 61 = 127) with the adjacent claim that all 142 students were classified, and t(156) with the 87 respondents who answered the perceived-control item.
8. Did you examine whether the peer-comparison band was associated with disengagement among lower-achieving students? If not, can the data support that analysis?

### Minor Issues

- Table 1 reports "Final exam score (0-100)" as a primary measure, but §3.3 never defines it among the study's measures.
- "The course enrolled several hundred students" — give the exact enrollment; it is the denominator for the response rate.
- Perceived control is reported as 3.847 (three decimals) from a 1-5 integer scale while its SD is given to two; align precision and reduce to one or two decimals.
- The Abstract is a single unstructured paragraph; this venue expects a structured abstract.
- §1, ¶3 describes "combining behavioral log data and self-report measures" in language that may read as a mixed-methods design. Both strands are quantitative and there is no integration design; consider wording that forecloses the misreading.
- Funding statement, conflict-of-interest declaration, and data-availability statement are all absent and are required at submission.
- §3.3 cites no source for "single-item overall ratings are common in dashboard studies to limit survey burden."

### Summary Assessment

The manuscript reports a single-term, single-course observational study relating dashboard session counts to end-of-term course completion, and it declares that scope honestly in Section 3.1. Three problems then make the reported analysis unassessable as printed.

First, the statistics are not mutually compatible. Part of the engagement–retention association is therefore definitional rather than empirical.

Third, the Discussion and Conclusion assert causation and worldwide generalisability that the design cannot license, contradicting the paper's own Section 3.1 and Section 5.1. ### Detailed Comments

#### Research Questions & Hypotheses

The central question is stated clearly and in an answerable form (§1: "whether students who engage more with a learning analytics dashboard are more likely to persist in and complete their course"). No formal hypotheses are posed, and none are needed for a descriptive association study. I found no HARKing signature: the reported analyses are few, they map onto the stated question, and there is no post-hoc subgroup that the framing appears fitted to. The problem is not the question — it is that the Discussion answers a different, causal question than the one asked.

#### Research Design

The design is named and, with the caveat in W17, honestly characterised. Its fitness for the stated question is the issue. The question as written ("more likely to persist") is answerable by an observational design; the question as answered in §5–§6 ("increasing engagement raises the probability") is not. A within-term design that measures exposure in an early window and retention thereafter, with prior attainment adjusted, would answer a directional question at modest additional cost using the same data. That is the single highest-value change available to the authors and it does not require new data collection.

#### Sampling Strategy

Two incompatible sampling accounts are given (W6), and neither is accompanied by a denominator. The consequence is not merely that representativeness is unknown — it is that the *direction* of the likely bias cannot be signed. If the sample is voluntary-response, students who answer a mid-term survey about study habits are plausibly the more self-regulating students, which inflates the engagement–retention association through a common cause. No power analysis appears; with a reconstructed event count near 15, the study is underpowered for any adjusted model, which the authors should state rather than have a reviewer infer.

#### Data Collection

The behavioural measure is well specified and portable (S3). The self-report measure is a single global item with no reliability estimate available in principle, defended on the grounds that "single-item overall ratings are common in dashboard studies to limit survey burden" (§3.3). Prevalence is not validity. More consequentially, a single global perceived-control rating does not index the adjustment phase that the self-regulated learning account requires, yet the Abstract claims the study "measured dashboard engagement, self-regulated learning behavior, and course persistence." No behavioural measure of regulation appears anywhere in §3.3. The Abstract should be corrected to state what was measured.

#### Analysis Methods

The analysis plan in §3.4 is three lines long and specifies no model, no covariates, no assumption checks, and no treatment of the skew the Results themselves report. Pearson *r* on a binary outcome, two independent-samples *t*-tests, and a median split constitute the whole of it. Multiple comparisons are not a concern here — three tests do not require correction — so I raise no alpha-inflation objection. The objections are that the estimator does not match the outcome (W10), that nothing is adjusted (W9), and that the reported statistics do not reconcile (W1–W4).

## Review Body

This manuscript's defining methodological problem is that the analysis it describes cannot be shown to exist as described. Three printed statistics are impossible: an Abstract effect size (*r* = .42) incompatible with the Results' own *p*-value, where *r* = .24 reproduces it exactly; a degrees-of-freedom value (156) larger than any sample the study reports, including the full analytic sample of 142 and the 87 who answered the item being tested; and a *t*/*p* pair (*t* = 1.31, *p* = .008) that no *df* in this study can produce, attached to a sentence describing the result as non-significant. A fourth set is mutually inconsistent: Table 2's 66 and 61 contradict both the adjacent claim that all 142 students were classified and the reported *df* of 140. Reconstruction resolves the last of these in the table's favour — the printed *t* of 1.31 is exactly what Table 2's means, SDs, and group sizes generate, whereas a 71/71 split would give 1.39 — which implies *df* = 125 and, most economically, that 15 students had no final-exam score because they did not sit the final. If that is right, the exam comparison is conditioned on the study's own outcome variable, and the headline association rests on roughly 15 events with no interval reported anywhere. My core question in this seat is not whether the effect is large; it is whether the analysis described exists, and on the manuscript's own numbers it does not yet.

Underneath the arithmetic sits a design problem the Limitations section does not reach. Recruitment occurred mid-term while retention is measured at term end, so students who withdrew earliest could never enter a study of withdrawal. Session counts accumulate across enrolled time without normalisation, so a student who leaves in week 8 has roughly half the opportunity window to generate the exposure. Some portion of *r* = .24 is therefore definitional rather than behavioural, and no amount of careful language repairs it. This is also where I record my disagreement in advance with a softer reading: the problem is not that the design is observational — observational work on this question is legitimate and useful — but that this particular exposure–outcome timing makes the association partly circular. The repair is available in the existing data: measure engagement in an early-term window that closes before any withdrawal, adjust for prior attainment, model the binary outcome with logistic regression, and report intervals. That produces a modest, defensible, publishable correlational finding.

The third failure is that the paper does not believe its own Methods. Section 3.1 declares the design cross-sectional; Section 1 promises to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story"; Section 2 cites Ibarra (2023) precisely on causal language outrunning evidence. Section 5 then states that "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and that increasing it "raises the probability" of completion, and Section 6 recommends dashboard investment to institutions worldwide as "a dependable strategy" and a "generalizable lever" — negating Section 5.1's own concession that the interface studied differs from those deployed elsewhere. Because the author demonstrably knows the standard, the required revision is not to soften verbs but to rebuild the argument on what the data support.

What should be kept is real and worth naming: the design is declared rather than disguised; the median-split tradeoff is disclosed by the authors themselves and for the right reason; the sessionization rule makes the exposure measure portable to other platforms; the Limitations section names three genuine constraints; and the unfavourable exam result is reported rather than dropped. Those five elements are the foundation of a scoped correlational contribution. They are not sufficient to carry the manuscript in its present form, because the statistics that would carry it do not reconcile, and no downstream judgment — mine or any other seat's — should be built on a number that has not survived reconstruction.

Two matters I do not adjudicate. Citation-claim fidelity, including the tension between the Literature Review's "reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students" and the listed title of the source it attributes that to, is Reviewer 2's determination. The consent question raised by the statement that students were not informed their dashboard activity would be analysed is Reviewer 3's; I score only its reproducibility surface — no ethics record, no data-availability statement, no software version, no code — which is independently inadequate. I found no instruction-injection content in the manuscript.

### Detailed Comments

#### Literature Review

- **Coverage**: Structurally competent within the dashboard literature; effectively absent on the retention side. No persistence theory, no primary SRL source, and no engagement with null or negative dashboard trial results — the field's mixed record is described in §2 ¶1 but never evidenced. At ~2,500 words I do not fault the *number* of citations; six substantive works can carry a short empirical paper. I fault that the six do not include the tradition the outcome belongs to, and that nine on-point listed works go unused.
- **Integration quality**: Genuinely synthetic. §2 moves through four themes with connective argument rather than serial summary, and §2 ¶4 explicitly folds the present study into the critique it reports. This is the manuscript's strongest section and should survive revision largely intact once W1 and W8 are corrected.
- **Research gap argument**: Asserted rather than argued. §1 ¶2 claims the mechanism "has attracted more enthusiasm than evidence" and that outcome studies "vary widely in design and rigor," but neither claim is supported by a citation, and §2 ¶5 then concedes that the field is already saturated with cross-sectional correlational work — which is exactly what this study adds. The gap statement and the design are in tension: as framed, the paper's contribution is another instance of the thing it identifies as the problem.

#### Theoretical Framework

- **Appropriateness**: SRL is a defensible frame for a dashboard study; the objection is not to the choice. The problem is boundary fit — SRL explains why a dashboard *might* change study behaviour, and this study has no measure of study behaviour, only of dashboard viewing and a global self-perception.
- **Application depth**: Decorative. The frame is introduced in §1 ¶2, restated in §2 ¶3, invoked once in §5 ¶1 as post-hoc consistency, and never used to generate a prediction, shape a measure, or structure an analysis. Critically, the framework's own logic supplies a competing prediction — engagement without adjustment (§2 ¶3) — which the paper states and then does not test. A framework that generates a rival hypothesis you decline to examine is not doing interpretive work. The conclusions do not feed back to theory in any form: no extension, no revision, no challenge.
- **Alternative frameworks**: For an outcome defined at the course level and a mechanism of visible progress feedback, a feedback-and-goal-setting frame or an expectancy-value frame would fit the single perceived-control item better than a cyclical SRL model, and would not commit the paper to measuring adjustment. If the SRL commitment is retained, the monitoring phase must be measured, not assumed.

#### Academic Argument Quality

- **Factual accuracy**: One apparent effect-direction reversal (W1), one apparently misplaced attribution (W8), one strength inflation of the evidence base (W5), and one construct misuse (W2). The general background claims — attrition as a persistent institutional concern, the gateway course as elevated-risk — are accurate but uncited, which is a missed opportunity given that Halloran (2020) sits in the list.
- **Argument logic**: The break is at §5 ¶1 and is total, not gradual. Between §3.1 ("observational, cross-sectional") and §5 ¶1 ("dashboard engagement improved course retention… therefore raises the probability") there is no intervening evidence, no covariate adjustment, no temporal ordering, and no argument. §6 then converts an unearned within-course causal claim into a worldwide institutional recommendation with the word "dependable." §5 ¶3's counsel "against overstatement" is a hedge about *magnitude* that leaves the causal claim untouched — it concedes the effect is small while continuing to assume it is an effect.
- **Terminology precision**: "Retention," "persistence," "attrition," and "completion" are used interchangeably across §1, §3.3, §5, and §6 while denoting a single course-level binary. "Engagement" floats between the behavioural sense the study measures and the cognitive sense the theory implicates — the manuscript diagnoses this conflation at §2 ¶4 and then commits it at §5 ¶1. The Abstract's "self-regulated learning behavior" names a construct that does not exist in the study.

#### Contribution to the Field

- **Incremental contribution**: As currently scoped, the empirical increment is a single-course, single-term correlation between dashboard session frequency and final-assessment participation, in a literature the manuscript itself describes as dominated by exactly that design. That is not nothing — deployment evidence from a specific interface with a peer-comparison band has value — but it is descriptive, and it is smaller than the paper claims.
- **Positioning**: The paper positions itself as answering the field's causal question ("whether it translates into measurable improvements in retention") while delivering a design that cannot address it. Its stated similarity to prior work is left implicit; its stated difference from prior work (combining logs with self-report) is real but modest, since the self-report component is one item.
- **Overclaiming**: Present at three escalating tiers — the Abstract's "promising lever," the Discussion's "improved" and "raises the probability," and the Conclusion's "dependable strategy," "worldwide," and "generalizable." Each tier is stronger than the one before and none is supported by an added piece of evidence. The Conclusion also contains an unexamined paradox for the persistence literature: if the students who engage are the students already positioned to persist, then raising engagement changes the proxy, not the outcome. The paper never distinguishes engagement-as-cause from engagement-as-symptom, and its entire recommendation depends on that distinction.

### Summary Assessment

This is a competent, honestly written manuscript that has a governance problem at its foundation and an actionability problem at its conclusion, and the two are connected. Section 3.2 states plainly that students were not informed their dashboard activity would be analysed; survey respondents consented only to the survey. No ethics approval, no lawful basis, no waiver, and no data-governance discussion appears anywhere. Section 6 then recommends that institutions worldwide expand precisely this class of deployment. A paper that monitors students without telling them, and then tells the sector to do more of it, owes its readers an account of why the first was permissible before the second can be assessed — and this one offers none.

The actionability problem is separable and equally serious. The manuscript's recommendation is to increase dashboard engagement. It offers no account of whether engagement is a cause of persistence, a proxy for conscientiousness, or a symptom of an intention to persist that was already formed. Those three possibilities imply radically different policies, and only the first implies any policy at all. An administrator acting on Section 6 could spend real money and change nothing.

Underneath both sits a monitoring blind spot. The deployment included a peer-comparison band. The manuscript's own literature review documents that relative-standing feedback discourages some students. No one appears to have looked for that harm, and the students most likely to have suffered it — those who left — are the ones the study is least able to see.

The study is salvageable as a scoped, honestly framed correlational contribution. The recommendation is not salvageable in its current form.

### Detailed Comments

#### Assumption Audit

**Explicit assumptions.** Four are stated and load-bearing. (i) Visibility supports self-regulation (§1) — presented as "the theoretical appeal," which is appropriately hedged. (ii) Session counts index engagement (§3.3) — the manuscript concedes the coarseness, which I credit. (iii) Course retention equals sitting the final assessment (§3.3) — defensible at course level, not at the level §6 spends it. (iv) Dashboards reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students (§2) — I do not make the citation-fidelity determination, which is Reviewer 2's exclusively. What I can attest from my own practice is that the deployment evidence base for differential benefit is mixed and contested, and that "reliably" overstates it as a description of the field. Since this assumption is what §2 says "underpins much of the equity-oriented rationale for institutional dashboard deployment," and since §6's recommendation inherits that rationale, the overstatement is not decorative. If Reviewer 2 confirms that the attribution reverses its source, my D2 score would escalate to block; on my own evidence alone it warrants warn.

**Implicit assumptions.** Five, none stated anywhere, all relied upon.

*(a) Engagement is institutionally manipulable.* Every sentence of §6 requires it. Nothing in the study establishes it. This is the single most consequential unstated premise in the manuscript and it is the one a practitioner will inherit without noticing.

*(b) LMS telemetry is institutional property available for research use without notification.* This is assumed by conduct rather than argued. It is a contestable position in my field and an unlawful one in some jurisdictions.

*(c) The dashboard is a neutral instrument with no downside.* Section 2 disproves this in the manuscript's own words and Sections 3–6 proceed as though it did not.

*(d) More engagement is monotonically better.* Table 1 reports a maximum of 48 dashboard sessions across 15 weeks — roughly one every other day — in a right-skewed distribution. The manuscript's framework scores that student as maximally healthy. From an adjacent-field reading, repeatedly checking a peer-comparison band may equally index anxiety, and the same behaviour would be scored as a success by this study and as a warning sign by a student-wellbeing service. The manuscript has no dose ceiling and no way to tell the two apart. I would flag this as the most interesting unexamined pattern in the paper.

*(e) What benefits the individual scales to the institution.* §6 makes this leap explicitly ("at scale") with no argument. Interventions that work by giving attentive students better information routinely fail to scale, because the constraint was never information.

**Paradigmatic assumptions.** The manuscript inherits its field's trace-data positivism — that logged behaviour is behaviour, and that behaviour is the thing worth measuring — and its linear-causality frame, in which a signal produces a response. It also inherits the "actionable signal" paradigm's default that the actor is the student. In institutional practice the actor who moves retention is usually an advisor with a caseload, and a dashboard that routes the signal only to the student assigns the labour of intervention to the person least equipped to perform it. Finally, the field's working assumption that observation is costless to the observed is doing silent work throughout this manuscript, and is precisely the assumption my discipline exists to question.

#### Cross-Disciplinary Connections

**Parallel research.** The closest parallel I can attest is the Purdue Course Signals case, where an early and widely publicised dashboard-retention claim was subsequently challenged on the grounds that the retention association was substantially produced by a selection artefact — students who took more Signals-enabled courses were mechanically more likely to have persisted. That episode is the field's canonical cautionary tale about exactly the inference this manuscript's §5 and §6 make, and it is a striking absence from a literature review that otherwise engages the field's critics well. I would also point the authors toward clinical research's adverse-event reporting model, which exists precisely because interventions with real average benefits harm identifiable subgroups, and toward the behavioural public policy literature's treatment of proxies-turned-targets.

**Borrowing opportunities.** One concept would materially strengthen this paper and costs a paragraph: Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The manuscript recommends turning its measure into a target and has no defence against this. Engaging it honestly would convert §6 from an unsupportable recommendation into a genuinely useful caution, and would make the paper more citable rather than less. Second, the data protection impact assessment as a framing device gives the authors a structured way to write the governance section the manuscript needs. Third, the Belmont Report's distinction between beneficence and justice gives a vocabulary for the §2-to-§5 equity gap: a deployment can raise the average and still fail the students it disadvantages, and those are separate obligations rather than one.

**Methodological borrowing.** A randomised encouragement design — randomising a prompt to use the dashboard rather than access to it — is feasible inside a single course, raises fewer consent problems than the present arrangement, and would give the authors the causal claim they currently assert without warrant. I offer this as a cross-disciplinary suggestion; design adequacy is Reviewer 1's determination. From my own side of the fence: short exit interviews with withdrawn students would tell the authors more about whether the dashboard helped or hurt than any additional log analysis, and participatory design sessions with students would answer the question of what the peer-comparison band felt like — a question this manuscript's data cannot reach.

#### Practical Impact

**Real-world application.** I read §6 as the prospective buyer it addresses. It tells me that investing in dashboards and encouraging engagement is a dependable strategy. Working out what I am authorised to do with that: I cannot procure on it, because the evidence is one course, one term, one institution, with an effect size the manuscript itself reports at two different values. I cannot mandate engagement, because the manuscript gives me no reason to think mandated engagement carries the association, and its own literature review gives me reason to fear forced exposure to peer comparison for the students who are behind. I cannot target the intervention, because no disaggregation was reported. I cannot even scope a pilot confidently, because I do not know whether the dashboard was vendor-supplied or locally built, and therefore whether the artefact studied is purchasable at all. The honest answer is that §6's recommendation authorises nothing, while sounding as though it authorises a great deal. That gap is the practical harm this manuscript risks.

**Implementation feasibility.** Dashboards are procurement items with licence costs, integration costs, and ongoing governance costs, and they compete for budget against advising headcount — the intervention with the strongest evidence base in this space. A paper recommending the former over the latter, on this evidence, is asking institutions to substitute cheap observation for expensive support. The unintended consequence I would flag for the authors is that dashboards are politically attractive precisely because they are cheap and visible, and a published claim of "dependable" retention benefit will be used in exactly that argument.

**Stakeholders.** Missing: students as people rather than rows; the withdrawn students, who are the study's own outcome variable and are entirely voiceless; instructors, who in practice notice disengagement first; advisors, who are the only actor with the capacity to convert a signal into an intervention; and the institution's data-governance function. Power asymmetry goes unremarked throughout: the students were observed, not asked, and the paper recommends extending that arrangement.

#### Broader Implications

**Ethical dimensions.** The central issue is not that a rule was broken — I cannot establish that and do not assert it. It is that a manuscript which observed students without telling them recommends that the sector observe more students, and offers no account of why the first was acceptable. That combination is what makes the missing ethics statement load-bearing rather than clerical. Secondary use of behavioural data for research is a genuinely contested area with defensible positions on several sides; the manuscript takes one without noticing that a position was available to take.

**Social impact.** Two risks. First, differential effect: if the dashboard helps confident students and discourages struggling ones, an average benefit conceals a widening gap, and this manuscript is structurally unable to detect that while actively recommending scale-up. Second, displacement: publishing "dependable" retention benefit for a low-cost technological intervention supplies ammunition to budget arguments that trade human support for software. Neither risk is the authors' fault; both are foreseeable consequences of §6's wording, and both are avoidable by scoping it.

**Future directions.** The most valuable follow-up is not a bigger correlational study. It is a harm-monitored deployment: a randomised encouragement design with pre-specified adverse outcomes, disaggregation by achievement band, and a student-facing consent and opt-out mechanism designed in from the start. A cross-jurisdiction governance study — what consent arrangements actually govern dashboard telemetry across regulatory regimes — would be a genuine contribution and one this author is unusually well positioned to make, having encountered the question the hard way.

**D1 — methodology_rigor.** The contract asks whether design, data handling, statistical reporting, and reproducibility affordances clear the field's peer-review bar; as Devil's Advocate I read this dimension as the question of whether the reported procedure could have produced the reported conclusion *at all*, or whether an equally-fitting procedural story explains the numbers better. My job is not to redesign the study (that is R1's territory) but to ask whether the manuscript's own reported procedure logically licenses what it concludes, and whether reproducibility claims are stated at a level the reader could actually check. Under the #215 gate, any severity I assign that rests on "learning analytics deployments should report X" must name an external accepted-practice boundary in this field, not my prior about what a rigorous study looks like in general.

**D2 — domain_accuracy.** The contract asks whether claims match current domain evidence, whether prior work is represented correctly, and whether domain terminology and results are factually stated. My adversarial angle is not coverage completeness (R2 owns that) but *directional* selection: whether the literature is marshalled as a one-sided brief. Learning analytics has a well-known split between studies reporting dashboard benefits and studies reporting null, mixed, or harmful effects — particularly around at-risk students' reactions to negative comparative feedback. ### Strongest Counter-Argument

If I held the opposite view, I would not argue that the effect is small, or that one course cannot generalise. I would argue that **the reported correlation is a definitional artifact of how the two variables were constructed, and that no behavioural mechanism is needed to produce it.**

Dashboard engagement is "the number of distinct sessions in which a student opened the dashboard view **during the term**" (§3.3). Retention is "whether the student remained enrolled and completed the final assessment" (§3.3). These are not two independent quantities. A student who withdrew in week 6 had six weeks in which sessions could accrue; a retained student had fifteen. The exposure variable's ceiling is a mechanical function of the outcome variable. Any positive association between a cumulative activity count and an indicator of remaining present to accumulate it is expected *a priori*, at some magnitude, in the complete absence of any effect of the dashboard on anything. The authors report no time-normalised measure (sessions per enrolled week), no engagement window closed before the withdrawal decision, and no survival framing. r = .24 is precisely the size one would expect from unequal time-at-risk alone.

The recruitment design compounds this rather than relieving it. The survey went out "midway through the term" (§3.2), and the analytic sample is composed of students who responded to it. Students who withdrew before that announcement could not enter the sample at any price. So the "not retained" group is not the attrition population — it is the residue of students who survived to mid-term and then left. The paper's headline claim is about attrition; its sample structurally excludes the majority of it.

Now take the counter-argument to its destination. Section 5 asserts that "increasing dashboard engagement therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course," and Section 6 converts this into worldwide deployment advice. Under the time-at-risk reading, that recommendation is not merely unsupported — it is incoherent. Session counts, on this account, are a downstream readout of weeks enrolled. Instructing institutions to raise session counts in order to raise completion is instructing them to move the thermometer in order to move the temperature. The rival explanation is more parsimonious than the authors' (it requires no self-regulatory mechanism, no perceived control, no theory at all), it fits every number the paper reports, and it is never named, tested, or excluded anywhere in the manuscript.

A competent rebuttal exists — the authors could restrict engagement counting to a fixed early window common to all students, or model time-to-withdrawal directly. They report neither. Until they do, the paper's central empirical claim has an equally-fitting, more economical rival, and the rival wins on parsimony.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths

1. **Unequal time-at-risk (the strongest rival).** Cumulative sessions are bounded by weeks enrolled; retention is defined as remaining enrolled. The correlation is expected under the null. More parsimonious than the authors' account — it requires no dashboard, no theory, no mechanism — and it fits every reported number. Never named.
2. **Reverse direction.** Persisting students keep opening the dashboard *because* they are still enrolled and still have assignments to check. The paper's directional language ("increasing engagement raises the probability") presumes an ordering the cross-sectional design cannot establish.
3. **Common cause: prior attainment and conscientiousness.** In an introductory statistics course, prior quantitative preparation plausibly drives both dashboard use and completion. The study reports zero covariates — no prior GPA, no prior-term persistence, no entry cohort, no major. The authors' mechanism and the common-cause account are observationally identical here.
4. **Restriction of range from mid-term volunteer recruitment.** The sample's "low-engagement" group consists of students engaged enough to answer a mid-term survey about dashboard use. This is not the low-engagement population; it is the low-engagement tail of an already-selected group. Any effect estimated across this range is uninformative about the students the retention recommendation targets.
5. **Reverse ordering for perceived control.** A student who already feels in command of the course may consult the dashboard more, rather than the dashboard producing the sense of control. The survey was administered at a single mid-term point; nothing in the design breaks the tie, yet §5 reads the association in one direction only.

### Unexamined Premise

**The paper assumes throughout that dashboard engagement is a manipulable input rather than a readout, and never states the assumption, so never defends it.**

Every layer of the manuscript is built on this: the theory section treats visibility as a cause of regulation; the measure treats session counts as a quantity a student "has"; the analysis treats it as an independent variable; and §6 treats it as a dial an institution can turn ("encouraging students to engage with them"). If session counts instead index weeks enrolled (C1), or index a stable disposition toward course engagement (alternative 3), then the recommendation does not merely lack support — it names an intervention target that cannot transmit an effect.

The paper observed students who chose to open a dashboard, and recommends that institutions prompt students to open it. §2's own Vandermeer (2023) and Rutledge & Berange (2022) material predicts that these are precisely the cases most likely to diverge — clicks without cognitive engagement, information "noted and then set aside." ## Review Body

The defining feature of this manuscript, from the adversarial seat, is that it argues against itself — and does so with full knowledge of the standard it is breaking. Section 1 promises to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story." Section 2 cites Ibarra (2023) specifically on causal language outrunning evidence in correlational learning analytics. Section 3.1 states plainly that the design is cross-sectional. Section 5 then opens with "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and asserts that increasing engagement "raises the probability" of completion, and Section 6 escalates to telling institutions worldwide that dashboard investment is a "dependable strategy" and a "generalizable lever." This cannot be treated as a wording problem. The manuscript demonstrates that its author knows the correct epistemic standard, cites the paper that enforces it, concedes the design that requires it, and then does not apply it. The required revision is therefore a reconstruction of the argument the data support, not a softening of the argument the paper currently makes.

Beneath the causal escalation sits a deeper problem that revision alone may not reach. The two variables in the primary analysis are not independently constructed. Dashboard sessions accumulate across the term; retention is defined as remaining enrolled through the final assessment. A student who withdraws in week six cannot accrue sessions in weeks seven through fifteen. The reported r = .24 is therefore expected at some magnitude under the null hypothesis of no dashboard effect whatsoever, purely from unequal time-at-risk. This rival account is strictly more parsimonious than the authors' — it needs no self-regulated learning mechanism, no perceived control, no theory of feedback — and it fits every number in the paper. It is never named. Compounding it, recruitment occurred "midway through the term," so students who withdrew before that point could not enter the sample at all: the study's non-retained group is not the attrition population, but the residue of students who survived to mid-term and then left. A study of attrition that structurally excludes most attrition is not a study of attrition.

The numeric layer will not bear the weight the argument places on it. Four of the manuscript's reported statistics cannot have been computed from the data it describes. The Abstract's r = .42 and the Results' r = .24 cannot both be the primary finding, and the reported p = .004 reconciles only with .24 — meaning the Abstract, the surface most readers will encounter, nearly doubles the effect. The perceived-control t-test carries df = 156 for a comparison the Methods restrict to 87 respondents, implying a sample larger than the entire study. Section 4.3 asserts that all 142 students were classified into engagement groups and reports df = 140, while Table 2 immediately below sums to 127. And t = 1.31 at df = 140 corresponds to p ≈ .19, not the p = .008 printed beside it — a discrepancy that matters doubly, because p = .008 would be significant at the paper's own α = .05, directly contradicting the prose that the comparison "did not reach a comparable level" and the Discussion's inference that it was "weaker still." The Discussion, in other words, interprets the sentence rather than the statistic. Even the descriptive layer fails: a mean of 3.847 is arithmetically unattainable from 87 integer responses on a 1–5 scale, since the two nearest possible values are 3.8391 and 3.8506.

The literature handling contains one specific and serious defect inside an otherwise creditable review. Section 2 attributes to Ferro & Nakamura (2021) the claim that dashboards have been "shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," while the reference list gives that source's title as "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student." The characterisation appears to invert the source, and the qualifier "reliably" converts a literature the same paragraph calls "far from uniform" into settled fact. This is load-bearing, not incidental: Section 2 states that this position "underpins much of the equity-oriented rationale for institutional dashboard deployment," and the equity rationale is what Sections 5 and 6 draw on to address institutions worldwide. I record explicitly that the citation set is *not* one-sided — Osei, Vandermeer, and Ibarra all appear, carrying the contrary strand — so the charge here is narrow and specific: a misattribution inside a balanced review, not a selective bibliography. The Domain reviewer owns the final determination, particularly given that all fifteen DOIs share a placeholder prefix and no source is independently locatable.

Two omissions complete the picture, and both are self-inflicted. Section 2 introduces the demotivation risk at length and Section 3.1 confirms that the deployed dashboard carried "a peer-comparison band" — the exact feature that literature implicates. The paper then never disaggregates by achievement level, never examines who disengaged, and proceeds to recommend worldwide expansion of that feature. The one deployment where the harm hypothesis could have been tested is the one where it was raised and dropped. Meanwhile Section 5.1 lists three limitations, all genuine, none of which threatens the causal claim, while omitting the three that do — selection, causal identification, and outcome-truncated exposure. The section performs candour without constraining a single claim made anywhere else in the manuscript.

One further premise underlies all of this and is never stated, so never defended: that dashboard engagement is a manipulable input rather than a readout. Theory, measurement, analysis, and recommendation all treat session counts as a dial an institution can turn. If session counts instead index weeks enrolled, or index a stable disposition toward course engagement, then the Section 6 recommendation does not merely lack support — it names an intervention target incapable of transmitting an effect. A second premise rides on the first: that engagement induced by institutional prompting is the same construct as engagement students chose. The paper's own cited material (Vandermeer, 2023; Rutledge & Berange, 2022) predicts these will diverge, producing "engagement without adjustment." Nothing in the manuscript addresses the substitution.

Finally, and separately from the argument: Section 3.2 discloses that "students were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study," and no ethics approval, data-availability, funding, or conflict-of-interest statement appears anywhere. I hold this at MINOR and label it `[FIELD-NORM UNVERIFIED]`, because assigning higher severity would rest on a disclosure norm I cannot ground against an external checkable venue standard from within this review, and the ethics determination belongs to the Perspective seat. The argumentative point that is mine to make stands regardless of that determination: a paper that analysed student behavioural traces without notifying the students recommends, in its Conclusion, that institutions worldwide expand precisely that practice — and treats the expansion as costless.

Registering what should be kept: Section 2's field awareness, Section 3.1's plain statement of the design, Section 3.3's unprompted disclosure of the median split's cost, and the third paragraph of Section 5 are all sound. That last paragraph — "the modest size of the engagement-retention association counsels against overstatement… dashboards help at the margin" — is a correctly calibrated reading of this study's own evidence, and it sits between a causal claim and a global deployment directive. The scoped correlational paper this manuscript should be is partly already written inside the manuscript that overclaims.

I file no Phase 1 dissent. Every dimension was scored against the triggers committed before I saw the paper, and each block trigger fired on manuscript-internal evidence rather than on an external standard I would have had to supply myself.
