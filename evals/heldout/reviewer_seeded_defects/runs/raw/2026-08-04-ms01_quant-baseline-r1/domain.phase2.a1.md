## Review Body

I read this manuscript in the way I read every dashboard study: with the reference list open beside the text, checking each in-text attribution against the source it points to, and checking each field term against the referent the persistence literature actually assigns it. On that reading the manuscript has a scholarship problem rather than a writing problem, and the scholarship problem is concentrated in three places.

The first is citation fidelity. Five of the six sources this paper cites in text are characterised in a way consistent with their own stated scope: Calloway on reflective prompts, Osei on discouragement from relative-standing feedback, Rutledge and Berange on forethought and monitoring, Vandermeer on the limits of click counts, and Ibarra on causal language in correlational work are all invoked for something their titles and framing plausibly support. The sixth is not. Section 2 states that dashboards "have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students" and attributes this to Ferro and Nakamura (2021), a source the reference list titles "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student." That is a reversal of the cited finding's direction, not a loose paraphrase, and the paper does not treat it as incidental: it says the position "underpins much of the equity-oriented rationale for institutional dashboard deployment, and we return to it in the Discussion." The equity rationale for deployment is therefore built on the inversion of what the cited study found. That the surrounding citations are handled correctly makes this worse rather than better, because it removes the excuse of uniform carelessness and leaves a specific, load-bearing misattribution.

The second is the construct. What this study measured is whether a student remained enrolled and sat the final assessment in one 15-week introductory statistics course. That is a defensible operationalisation of course completion, and it is a familiar and useful outcome. It is not retention as the higher education persistence literature uses the term. In the Tinto and Bean lineage and in the contemporary work that contests it, retention denotes institutional or programme persistence measured across enrolment periods: term-to-term re-enrolment, first-to-second-year continuation, degree completion. Course completion and institutional persistence are empirically related but operationally distinct, and the relationship between them is itself a research question, not an identity. The manuscript's own design section concedes the point without drawing the consequence: "no student was observed across multiple courses or terms." Yet the Introduction opens on "undergraduate attrition" and the gateway course as "a point of elevated risk," and the Conclusion promises "improving retention across programs and disciplines" for "higher education institutions worldwide." My judgement on the question of repairability is that this is not fixable by substituting words. The problem statement, the policy implication, and the claimed scope of contribution all depend on the institutional reading; the data support only the course reading. Repair means reframing the paper as a course-completion study, with a title, abstract, framing, and conclusion built for that narrower claim, and with the persistence literature engaged honestly on the question of what course completion does and does not tell you about attrition.

The third is the theory. Self-regulated learning is introduced as the explanatory mechanism, with the standard phase vocabulary and a correct citation to a source that frames dashboards through forethought and monitoring. But the only regulatory measure in the study is a single global item, "Overall, I feel in control of my learning in this course." A global control belief is not a measure of strategy use, not a measure of metacognitive monitoring, and not a measure of the adjustment the theory predicts; it sits closer to academic self-efficacy or perceived academic control than to any phase of the Zimmerman cycle. The Discussion nonetheless reads the group difference on that item as "consistent with a self-regulated learning account in which dashboards scaffold monitoring and adjustment," and the Abstract goes further, listing "self-regulated learning behavior" among the things the study measured. No behaviour of that kind was measured. On my reading the theory is decorative: it supplies vocabulary for the introduction and an interpretive frame for the discussion, and does no work that the data could have contradicted.

Related to the theory problem is a reconciliation the paper owes and does not pay. Section 2 raises the goal-orientation branch of this literature and states plainly that performance-avoidance students "may interpret an unfavorable comparison as a threat to be avoided rather than a problem to be solved, disengaging from the very interface intended to re-engage them." That is a direct rival account of the reported association: students on a trajectory toward non-completion stop opening the peer-comparison band and stop completing the course, which produces a positive engagement-completion correlation with no facilitative mechanism at all. The rival account is sharpened, not weakened, by the recruitment design, since a mid-term volunteer survey systematically under-samples exactly the disengaging students the counter-literature describes. The Discussion never returns to this; it reads the association as aligning "with the view that externalized progress cues can support persistence" and stops. Berange (2021) on goal orientation and dashboard response sits in the reference list, uncited.

Two further patterns bear on literature integrity. Nine of the fifteen listed references are never cited anywhere in the text, and the uncited set is not random: it contains all three sources in *Higher Education Persistence Quarterly*, including a review of gateway-course retention interventions and a cautionary study of retention modelling with LMS trace data, plus the SRL synthesis and the goal-orientation study. A paper framed on undergraduate attrition engages no persistence source in text while listing three. Separately, the manuscript cites Ibarra's audit of causal language in correlational learning analytics, declares "we designed the present study to be transparent about its correlational scope," and then writes that "dashboard engagement improved course retention," that engagement "raises the probability that a student completes the course," and that dashboards are "a dependable strategy for improving retention." Committing the documented error immediately after citing the audit of it is the clearest signal in the manuscript that its sources were assembled rather than consulted.

On reported quantities I record what I found and defer the inferential audit to the methodology seat, whose dimension owns it. The headline association appears at two incompatible values (Abstract r = .42; Results r = .24). The perceived-control comparison reports df = 156, which implies roughly 158 cases when only 87 students answered the item and the whole analytic sample is 142. The exam comparison reports a table whose group sizes sum to 127 immediately after the text states that all 142 students were classified, alongside a t of 1.31 on 140 df paired with p = .008 and then described as not reaching a comparable level. The secondary clarity item reports a sample SD of 0.10 for ten integer responses with a mean of exactly 3.00, which is arithmetically unavailable: integer deviations from an integer mean give either 0 or at least 0.33. I have set my dimension score to block rather than fatal because every defect above has an identifiable repair that does not require new data, and because whether the impossible test statistics indicate transcription failure or analyses that were never run is a determination for the methodology reviewer rather than for me. If that seat concludes the reported statistics cannot be reconciled with the described sample at all, the fatality question should reopen on their axis, not on mine.

Two items I flag as referrals outside my dimension. The statement that "Students were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study" raises a consent and data-governance question I am not scoring here but which I do not regard as a mere compliance matter, since it bears on whether the behavioural half of the dataset was lawfully constituted. And the causal escalation between Section 2's declared scope and the Discussion and Conclusion is also an internal-consistency failure squarely within the devil's advocate seat's dimension; I report it below only in its citation-fidelity aspect.

### S1: Section 2's measurement critique is domain-accurate and self-applied

The paper characterises the click-count problem correctly, matching the scope of the source it cites, and explicitly includes itself in the criticism rather than exempting itself. The illustration it gives, that a student who opens the dashboard once and studies it carefully is scored below one who opens it repeatedly without reflection, is the right illustration and is stated in the terms the measurement literature uses.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 "Most dashboard studies, including the present one, infer engagement from coarse behavioral proxies such as page views or session counts"`

### S2: Limitations names the correct three constraints for this design

The limitations subsection identifies the session-count proxy, the self-report basis of the control measure, and the single-course, single-interface setting. Those are the three constraints a competent referee in this area would expect to see acknowledged, and each is stated accurately rather than deflected.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5.1 "dashboard engagement was operationalized narrowly as session counts, which does not capture the depth or quality of engagement"`

### S3: Operational definitions are specific enough to be checked

The sessionization rule, the dichotomous retention coding, the exact wording and scale of the control item, and the median split are all stated with enough precision that a reader can test them against the reported numbers. That transparency is what made the inconsistencies below detectable, and it is a real virtue in a subfield where measurement details are often withheld.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.3 "A session was defined as a dashboard view preceded by at least thirty minutes of inactivity"`

### W1: A cited source's central finding is reversed and the reversal carries the paper's equity rationale

Section 2 attributes to Ferro and Nakamura (2021) the claim that dashboards reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students, with those students gaining most from externalised progress cues. The source as listed reports the opposite: peer comparison demotivating the lower-achieving student. The paper then designates this reversed finding as the foundation of the equity-oriented deployment rationale and announces that it will return to it in the Discussion, which it does when reading its own result as aligning with the view that progress cues support persistence. The defect is not a bibliographic slip; the inverted claim is doing structural work in both the literature review and the interpretation. Uncorrected, the manuscript asserts as established the negation of what its own cited evidence found, which no editor can accept and which alone justifies a block on this dimension.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomely for lower-achieving students" against reference title "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student"`
**Confidence**: 5 — direct comparison of an in-text attribution against the reference entry printed in the same manuscript.

### W2: Course completion is presented as retention in the institutional sense the persistence literature reserves

The outcome is remaining enrolled and sitting the final assessment in one course over one term. The framing is undergraduate attrition, gateway-course risk, and improving retention across programmes and disciplines for institutions worldwide. In the persistence literature retention denotes continuation across enrolment periods, and the design section concedes that no student was observed across multiple courses or terms. Because the problem statement and the policy implication both require the institutional reading while the data support only the course reading, correction requires reframing the study as a course-completion study rather than adjusting vocabulary. The empirical finding survives that reframing; the claimed contribution does not survive unchanged.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.3 "coded dichotomously as whether the student remained enrolled and completed the final assessment" with §1 "Undergraduate attrition remains a persistent concern for institutions and instructors alike"`
**Confidence**: 5 — standard construct boundary in student persistence research, checked against the paper's own stated coding rule.

### W3: The Discussion and Conclusion commit the exact error of the critique the paper cites and disclaims

Section 2 cites an audit of causal language in correlational learning analytics and states that the present study was designed to be transparent about its correlational scope. The Discussion then opens by asserting that dashboard engagement improved course retention and that increasing engagement raises the probability of completion, and the Conclusion calls dashboard investment a dependable strategy. A cross-sectional volunteer-sample association licenses none of these. The scholarship failure is specific: the paper invokes a source as authority for restraint and then supplies that source with a fresh example.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5 "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and §6 "a dependable strategy for improving retention across programs and disciplines"`
**Confidence**: 5 — the paper's own stated scope commitment and its concluding claims are directly comparable.

### W4: The SRL framework is decorative, and the Abstract claims a measurement the study did not take

Self-regulated learning supplies the explanatory mechanism throughout, but the only regulatory measure is a single global perceived-control item, which indexes neither strategy use nor monitoring behaviour and therefore cannot discriminate the SRL account from any competing account of the association. The Discussion nonetheless reads the group difference as consistent with dashboards scaffolding monitoring and adjustment, neither of which was observed, and the Abstract states that self-regulated learning behaviour was measured. Repair means either removing the mechanism claim, which thins the contribution substantially, or adding genuine strategy-use and monitoring measures.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Abstract "we measured dashboard engagement, self-regulated learning behavior, and course persistence" with §3.3 "measured with a single-item overall rating" and §5 "consistent with a self-regulated learning account in which dashboards scaffold monitoring and adjustment"`
**Confidence**: 5 — the mismatch between the invoked theoretical constructs and the single item administered is on the page.

### W5: The goal-orientation counter-literature the paper itself raises is never reconciled with its uniformly positive reading

Section 2 states that performance-avoidance students may disengage from the interface intended to re-engage them and that the direction of the effect depends on framing and individual differences. That mechanism generates the reported positive association with no facilitative effect, and the mid-term volunteer recruitment window under-samples precisely those students. The Discussion does not address the alternative it raised, and the goal-orientation source in the reference list is never cited. Because the rival account explains the headline result equally well, the interpretation requires substantial rewriting and, ideally, a goal-orientation measure or a comparison of respondents with non-respondents.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 "Performance-avoidance oriented students, in particular, may interpret an unfavorable comparison as a threat to be avoided" with §5 "aligns with the view that externalized progress cues can support persistence"`
**Confidence**: 4 — the omission and the rival mechanism are clear; the magnitude of the resulting bias cannot be estimated from what is reported.

### W6: The sample is described with a technical term the recruitment procedure contradicts

Section 3.2 states that participants were drawn using a random sample of students enrolled in the section, then describes an LMS announcement to which students chose to respond, with non-respondents excluded. Those are incompatible descriptions, and "random sample" carries a specific meaning that governs what inferences the data support. Whether or not the methodology seat treats this as a sampling defect, it is a misuse of a field term with direct consequences for the generalisation claims in the Conclusion, and it must be corrected before any statement about the population can be assessed.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.2 "using a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" against "Students who chose to respond, and who consented to the survey, formed the study sample"`
**Confidence**: 5 — the two sampling descriptions are adjacent and mutually exclusive.

### W7: The headline association is reported at two incompatible values

The Abstract reports r = .42 for the engagement-retention association; the Results report r = .24. Both cannot be correct, and the two support materially different readings of the paper's contribution, since one is a moderate association and the other is the modest effect the Discussion goes on to describe. A reader cannot determine which number the study produced.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Abstract "Dashboard engagement correlated positively with retention (r = .42)" against §4.2 "positively associated with course retention (r = .24, p = .004)"`
**Confidence**: 5 — two stated values for the same statistic in the same manuscript.

### W8: The perceived-control comparison reports degrees of freedom incompatible with every sample the paper describes

The reported t(156) implies about 158 cases. The perceived-control item was answered by 87 students, and the entire analytic sample is 142. The reported statistic therefore cannot have been computed on the perceived-control respondents, on the analytic sample, or on any subset of either. This is the result the Discussion uses to connect engagement to regulatory experience, so its provenance needs to be established rather than corrected in passing.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4.3 "t(156) = 3.02, p = .003" against §4.1 "across the 87 survey respondents who answered the item"`
**Confidence**: 4 — the arithmetic is unambiguous; whether the cause is transcription or analysis is for the methodology seat.

### W9: The exam comparison is internally inconsistent in sample composition and in its reported p value

The text states that all 142 students were classified into engagement groups and reports t(140), which is consistent with 142. The accompanying table gives group sizes of 66 and 61, totalling 127. Separately, t = 1.31 on 140 df does not correspond to p = .008, and the text simultaneously describes the comparison as not reaching a comparable level while reporting a p value below the stated alpha. The reader cannot determine who was analysed or whether the comparison was significant.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `table: Table 2 (n = 66 plus 61 = 127) against §4.3 statement that all 142 students were classified, with t(140) = 1.31 reported as p = .008`
**Confidence**: 4 — the table-to-text contradiction is certain; the t-to-p mismatch is a straightforward check.

### W10: The secondary item's reported dispersion is unavailable for integer responses

For ten integer responses on a 1-5 scale with a mean of exactly 3.00, deviations from the mean are integers, so the sample SD is either 0 or at least about 0.33. The reported 0.10 lies in neither range. Because the item is summarised descriptively only and supports no claim, correcting or removing it does not alter any conclusion; it belongs in the class of reporting errors that a revision should sweep up rather than one that reshapes the paper.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4.1 "the reported secondary-item values were N=10; M=3.00; sample SD=0.10; integer scale=1-5"`
**Confidence**: 4 — elementary arithmetic on the stated integer scale and sample size.

### W11: Nine of fifteen references are never cited, including the whole persistence literature listed

The uncited set comprises the three persistence-journal sources, the SRL synthesis, the goal-orientation study, the survey-methods source, and three further dashboard papers. Standing alone this is a hygiene defect: deleting the uncited entries would leave every claim in the paper unchanged, so on its own decision impact it is Minor rather than inherited from the misattribution in W1. Its significance is diagnostic. A manuscript framed on attrition that lists a gateway-course retention review and a cautionary study of retention modelling with trace data, and engages neither in text, has assembled a bibliography rather than read into a literature, which is the same failure mode that produced W1 and W5.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: References section — expected in-text citation of the nine listed sources (Ainsworth & Devi 2018, Berange 2021, Delacroix & Ohno 2022, Halloran 2020, Kessler & Amadou 2019, Montez 2022, Prakash & Tolliver 2021, Solberg & Whitfield 2018, Wexler & Ojo 2020); checked Introduction, Literature Review, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion`
**Confidence**: 5 — exhaustive comparison of in-text citations against the reference list.

### W12: An uncited empirical claim about field practice is used to justify a measurement choice

Section 3.3 asserts that single-item overall ratings are common in dashboard studies to limit survey burden, and offers this as the warrant for measuring the study's only regulatory construct with one item. That is a claim about the state of practice in the field, and it needs a citation, particularly since the paper's own cited measurement critique points the other way on the adequacy of coarse proxies. Adding support or deleting the sentence changes no conclusion, but as written the methodological justification rests on an unevidenced assertion about the literature.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.3 "single-item overall ratings are common in dashboard studies to limit survey burden"`
**Confidence**: 4 — the claim is uncited on its face; my knowledge of prevailing practice suggests it is at best contested.
