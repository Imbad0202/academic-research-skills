## Review Body

Let me first construct the strongest version of this paper's argument, because that is the version worth attacking. Steelmanned, the claim is: in a large gateway statistics course, students who voluntarily opened a progress dashboard more often were more likely to sit the final assessment, and the same students reported a higher sense of control over their learning; taken together, this pattern is what a self-regulated learning account of dashboards predicts, and it is therefore weak but non-trivial evidence that making progress visible supports persistence. Framed that way — an association, reported honestly, with a mechanism-consistent correlate — the paper would be publishable in some form. The Introduction even announces exactly that discipline: the authors promise to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story that might explain it." The manuscript then breaks that promise in its own Discussion and Conclusion, and the breakage is not a stylistic slip. It is the load-bearing inferential step.

The Discussion asserts that engagement "improved" retention and that increasing engagement "therefore raises the probability" of completion; the Conclusion escalates to a "dependable strategy" that is "generalizable" for institutions "worldwide." Nothing in the design licenses any of this. The study is explicitly observational and cross-sectional, dashboard access was universal and unprompted, no covariate is adjusted for, and no rival explanation is even enumerated, let alone discriminated against. Two rivals are obvious enough that their absence is itself an argumentative defect. The first is reverse and common causation: a student who is already on track to finish a course has both more reason and more opportunity to check a progress display, and prior achievement, conscientiousness, and course commitment plausibly drive both variables. The second is more damaging, because it is partly mechanical rather than merely confounded.

Consider what the two variables actually are. Retention is defined as remaining enrolled through the final assessment. Engagement is defined as the count of dashboard sessions accumulated "during the term." A student who withdraws in week five has, by construction, roughly a third of the exposure window available to a student who persists to week fifteen. The predictor's magnitude is therefore partly a function of the outcome's realisation. Some non-zero association between these two operationalisations is guaranteed by their definitions before any behavioural or psychological process is invoked, and the manuscript offers no analysis that separates the definitional component from the substantive one. This is the circularity case in its clearest form: an observed r cannot be read as evidence for a mechanism when the measurement window of the cause is truncated by the effect. The fix is available in principle — count sessions only within a fixed early window (say weeks one to four) that every eventual withdrawer also inhabited, then predict later persistence — but that analysis is not in the paper, and until it exists the headline association carries no interpretable meaning.

The sampling architecture compounds this. Recruitment happened "midway through the term" via an LMS announcement, and the sample is those who chose to respond. Students who had already withdrawn could not respond; students who were disengaging were least likely to. The analytic sample is thus conditioned on survival past the recruitment window, which is precisely the region where retention variance lives. The manuscript never reports how many of the 142 were coded not retained, so a reader cannot tell whether the retention outcome had ten failures or fifty — and with a dichotomous outcome and no marginal, the reported correlation is uninterpretable in either magnitude or stability. The same section simultaneously describes the sample as "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section," a description that cannot both be true and coexist with self-selected volunteer response. One of those two sentences is false, and which one it is changes the paper's entire inferential standing.

Beyond the design, the reported numbers do not cohere with each other, and this is where charity runs out. The Abstract's r = .42 and the Results' r = .24 are not two views of one finding; they are different findings, differing by roughly a factor of three in shared variance, and the Abstract's version is the one that supports the word "promising." The perceived-control comparison is reported as t(156) when only 87 respondents answered the item, which admits no reading consistent with any sample described in the manuscript. The exam comparison reports t(140) = 1.31 with p = .008 and then characterises the result as a difference that "did not reach a comparable level" — a statistic that cannot produce that p-value, paired with an interpretation that cannot follow from that p-value. Table 2's subgroups sum to 127 against a stated 142 classified into groups. The secondary clarity item reports M = 3.00 with SD = 0.10 for ten integer responses on a 1–5 scale, a combination that is arithmetically unreachable (ten identical responses give SD = 0; a single one-point deviation gives SD ≈ 0.32). Individually these are correctable; jointly they mean that no reported inference in Section 4 can currently be taken at face value, which is why the coherence dimension blocks rather than warns.

I also want to press on a subtler move the paper makes and then buries. Section 2 concedes, citing Vandermeer, that click-based counts "should be treated as rough indicators rather than as faithful measures of the cognitive engagement the theory implicates," and Section 5.1 repeats the concession. Section 5 then reads a session-count association as "consistent with a self-regulated learning account in which dashboards scaffold monitoring and adjustment." The concession is quarantined and the proxy is silently promoted to the construct. Related: perceived control is invoked as a "mediating construct" with no mediation model, no temporal ordering (the item was collected mid-term, concurrently with the behaviour it supposedly mediates), and a single item standing in for a multidimensional regulatory construct. A cross-sectional correlation between two self-selected variables is not evidence of mediation, and the sentence should either become a hypothesis or disappear.

Two matters outside my scoring remit deserve flagging for the seats that own them. First, ethics: the manuscript states that students "were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study," and no ethics approval, waiver, or oversight statement appears anywhere in the paper. That is a substantive consent problem, not a documentation gap, and I have logged it as a Critical for the panel's adjudication rather than leaving it to inference. Second, literature representation: Section 2 attributes to Ferro and Nakamura (2021) the claim that dashboards "reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," while the same source appears in the reference list as "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student." The attributed claim appears to invert the cited work, and the Discussion's equity framing rests on it. The domain seat should verify this and also check that Osei (2020) and Ibarra (2023) are represented as their authors would recognise. I note separately that nine of the sixteen listed references (Ainsworth and Devi, Berange 2021, Delacroix and Ohno, Halloran, Kessler and Amadou, Montez, Prakash and Tolliver, Solberg and Whitfield, Wexler and Ojo) are never cited in the text, which the editorial seat may wish to treat as reference padding.

Minor items that do not rise to the tables but should be fixed: the perceived-control mean is reported to three decimals (3.847) from a single-item integer scale with SD = 0.62, a precision the measure cannot carry; Table 1 reports final exam score, which Section 3.3 never defines as a measure; the Abstract implies survey data from all 142 students when only 87 answered the focal item; Section 3.1 claims the single course gives the sample "disciplinary breadth," a claim about student majors that Section 6 then silently converts into generalisability "across programs and disciplines"; no effect sizes or confidence intervals accompany either group comparison; and the median split of a right-skewed count variable, together with a Pearson coefficient computed against a dichotomous outcome without labelling it point-biserial, are analytic choices the methodology seat should evaluate on its own terms.

What would change my assessment: a re-analysis in which engagement is counted only within a window every student could complete, a reported retention marginal, a corrected and internally reconciled statistics section, and a Discussion and Conclusion whose strongest verb is "was associated with." That is a materially narrowed paper, and it may be a defensible one. The paper as submitted argues for something its data cannot reach.
