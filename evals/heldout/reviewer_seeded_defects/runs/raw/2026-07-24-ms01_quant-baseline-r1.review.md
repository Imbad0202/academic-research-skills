## Review Strategy Recommendations

- **The defining characteristic of this paper is a systematic gap between what the Results section reports and what the rest of the paper claims — the reviewers should be steered to converge on this from four independent angles rather than each treating it as an isolated nitpick.** Concretely, the synthesizer should expect strong, mutually reinforcing signals on: (a) **numerical inconsistency** — the abstract's r = .42 versus the Results' r = .24; degrees of freedom (t(156), t(140)) that do not match the stated sample sizes (n = 142 primary; Table 2 n = 66 + 61 = 127; 87 survey respondents); and at least one impossible statistic/p-value pairing (t(140) = 1.31 does not give p = .008); and (b) **causal overreach** — a design the Methods correctly call "observational, cross-sectional," yet a Discussion/Conclusion asserting dashboards "improved," "raises," and provide a "dependable" retention "lever." R1 owns (a); the EIC and R3 own (b); R2 links the two by showing the paper contradicts its own cautious Literature Review.

- **Deliberate complementarity, minimal overlap:** R1 (statistics/design), R2 (literature fidelity + construct validity), and R3 (ethics/consent + deployment scope) attack genuinely different failure classes and should not tread on one another. The one intended point of *productive* convergence is causal language: it will surface in R1's design critique, the EIC's fit/significance judgment, and R3's practical-recommendation critique — that triangulation is a feature, and the synthesizer should treat three independent flags on the same sentence as a strength-of-signal indicator, not as redundant findings to be merged away.

- **Watch for a specific citation-integrity tension the domain reviewer (R2) is uniquely equipped to catch:** Ferro & Nakamura (2021) is invoked in one place (§2, ¶2) as showing dashboards "reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," and in another (the References gloss and §2's demotivation thread) as being about dashboards *demotivating* lower achievers through peer comparison. Whether this is a genuine misrepresentation of a source or two different findings from one paper is exactly the kind of question a peer-review panel should flag for author clarification rather than resolve unilaterally — it should be raised as a query, not asserted as fabrication.

- **Ethics is a first-class axis here, not a footnote.** The explicit statement that students were not informed their dashboard-activity data would be analyzed, combined with the absence of any IRB/ethics-approval statement, is the kind of issue that can block publication independently of the paper's statistical merits. R3 is configured specifically so this does not get lost among the methodology findings; the synthesizer should preserve it as a distinct, potentially decision-driving concern.

- **Tone calibration:** despite the polish, this manuscript's problems are substantive and partly integrity-adjacent (internal inconsistency, causal overclaiming, consent). Reviewers should be rigorous and specific rather than "developmental/gentle" — this is not a struggling first draft to be nurtured, but a fluent draft whose confident claims outrun its evidence. Feedback should be direct about the mismatch while remaining professional and actionable, giving the authors a clear route to a defensible, appropriately-scoped correlational contribution.

### Detailed Comments

#### Journal Fit
The topic fits the *Journal of Learning Analytics* well — LMS-trace dashboard work with a retention outcome is core scope. But fit is conditional on this journal's editorial line of distinguishing correlation from causation, and the manuscript as written violates that line in its Discussion and Conclusion. It fits us *as an associational note*, not as the causal, generalizable claim it currently advances. If the authors cannot retreat from the causal framing, BJET or *The Internet and Higher Education* may be a more forgiving home; I would rather keep it here in scoped-down form.

#### Originality
Modest but real. A single-site correlational engagement–retention association is not novel in kind — the literature is crowded with such studies — but the paired perceived-control channel and the candid measurement discussion give it enough to earn space *if* the contribution is honestly delimited. The originality does not come from the effect (small, expected); it comes from the transparency the paper could offer about the limits of click-based engagement proxies. Ironically, the Discussion abandons exactly the caution that would have made the paper distinctive.

#### Significance
At the reported effect sizes (r = .24, and an exam-performance comparison the authors themselves call weak), the significance is at-the-margin: dashboards may nudge some students toward completion. That is a legitimate, modest finding. The Conclusion's leap to a "dependable, generalizable lever... at scale" is not supported and inflates significance far past what the data deliver. Correctly scoped, this is a useful brick; as claimed, it is a promise the evidence cannot keep.

#### Structural Coherence
This is where the paper fails most visibly at my altitude. There are three coherence breaks: (1) the Methods say correlational, the Discussion/Conclusion say causal — the paper tells two different stories about what it did; (2) the Abstract's r = .42 contradicts the body's r = .24 — the headline and the evidence disagree numerically; (3) §1 and §2 are commendably cautious, then §5–§6 abandon that caution entirely. A reader who accepts the Abstract and Conclusion would come away believing something the Results section does not support. That title→conclusion mismatch is the paper's central structural defect.

#### Title & Abstract
The title ("Dashboard Engagement and Course Retention: Evidence from an Undergraduate Learning Analytics Deployment") is appropriately associational and I would keep it. The Abstract, however, both reports the wrong correlation (r = .42) and slides into "increasing dashboard engagement is a promising lever for improving retention," pre-committing the reader to the causal reading before any evidence is seen. The Abstract should carry the correct r and the associational framing.

#### Conclusion
The Conclusion (§6) is the least defensible section. "This study provides evidence that engagement... raises course retention"; "a dependable strategy"; "a practical and generalizable lever... at scale"; "institutions worldwide." Every one of these overshoots the design. The Conclusion must be rebuilt from the Results, not from the aspiration.

#### Research Questions & Hypotheses
The RQ in §1 — whether students who engage more with the dashboard are "more likely to persist in and complete their course" — is clear and, as phrased, associational. §1 even promises the paper will "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story." That promise is broken by §5–§6. The RQ is answerable by the design; the conclusions drawn are not.

#### Research Design
Correctly self-labeled observational and cross-sectional (§3.1). Internally contradictory. §3.2 asserts a "random sample" and then, in the same subsection, describes a mid-term LMS announcement inviting volunteers, with non-responders "excluded." #### Assumption Audit

- **Explicit assumptions**: The paper explicitly frames dashboards through a self-regulated learning lens — visibility supports self-monitoring, which supports persistence (§1, §5). - **Implicit assumptions**: The load-bearing implicit premise is that *the students who engage are exchangeable with the students who don't* — i.e., that engagement, not the disposition behind it, is what moves retention. - **Parallel research**: The behavioral-nudge and program-evaluation traditions have long treated exactly this pattern — voluntary uptake of a tool correlating with a good outcome — as the paradigm case of selection bias, and have developed language (selection on the dependent variable, motivated uptake) the paper could borrow to characterize its own limit honestly.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths

1. **Common prior cause (baseline motivation / prior attainment).** Students who enter the course more motivated or better prepared both persist at higher rates *and* check the dashboard more. This single unmeasured variable produces the observed r = .24 with no dashboard mechanism at all, and is more parsimonious than the proposed "dashboards scaffold monitoring" story because it requires no causal action by the interface. The paper adjusts for nothing (§3.4), so it cannot rule this out.
2. **Reverse causation.** Persistence causes engagement rather than the reverse: students who have already decided to complete the course keep opening the course tools, including the dashboard. The cross-sectional design (§3.1) gives no temporal ordering to distinguish this from the paper's preferred direction.
3. **Selection into the analytic sample.** Because the sample is volunteers who answered a mid-term announcement (§3.2), the students captured are plausibly the more engaged and more committed to begin with; the association may be an artifact of who opted in, not a property of dashboard use.

### Missing Stakeholder Perspectives

- **Students whose activity data were analyzed without being informed** (§3.2 states they "were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed"). *(Consent/governance stakeholder flagged as absent; adjudication belongs to R3/ethics.)*

### Unexamined Premise

The entire paper assumes that *opening* the dashboard is the causally active ingredient — that the click is doing the work. But every finding is equally consistent with dashboard-opening being a *marker* of a pre-existing disposition (commitment, conscientiousness, prior achievement) rather than a lever on it. If the click is a symptom rather than a cause, the central recommendation — get more students to click — has no reason to move retention at all. None of the eight challenge dimensions above fully isolates this premise; the paper never states it, and its conclusion cannot survive without it.

- The Introduction (§1 ¶4) and Methods (§3.1) show genuine methodological self-awareness ("careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story").
