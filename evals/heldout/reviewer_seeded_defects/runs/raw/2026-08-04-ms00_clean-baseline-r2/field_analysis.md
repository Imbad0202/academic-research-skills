## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Research in Learning Technology** (ALT, open access) — Explicitly hospitable to well-bounded single-institution empirical work and short-format reports; editorial culture rewards transparent limitation-setting, which is this paper's strongest asset. Best current-form match.
2. **Interactive Technology and Smart Education** or **Electronic Journal of e-Learning** (Q3) — Single-site LMS survey studies are a standing genre in both; the incremental-data-point framing is acceptable there in a way it is not at Q1 outlets. Requires adding response rate and situating r = .42 against published meta-analytic ranges.
3. **Journal of Applied Learning and Teaching** or an institutional-practice outlet (practitioner tier) — If the authors prefer to lead with the onboarding implication rather than the correlation. This reframing trades scholarly novelty for practical utility and would require the platform-context detail the paper currently omits.

*Note:* the study becomes Q1/Q2-competitive only with log-data validation, multiple sites, or a longitudinal wave. That is a new study, not a revision.

## Reviewer Configuration Cards

### Reviewer Configuration Card #1

**Role**: EIC
**Display role**: Journal-Fit Reviewer
**Identity Description**: Senior Associate Editor of *Research in Learning Technology*, 15 years handling LMS/VLE adoption submissions and running first-pass scope triage; has personally desk-rejected several hundred single-site technology-acceptance surveys and maintains the journal's reference-verification checklist.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **Contribution sufficiency vs. scope fit** — Does one bivariate correlation, honestly reported, clear the journal's bar for a research article, or is this a Research Note / practice piece? Judge the *category*, not just accept/reject.
  2. **Reader interest test** — Would this journal's readership (learning technologists, academic developers, e-learning researchers) change any decision on the basis of r = .42 from one unnamed mid-sized university? What is the actionable takeaway line an editor would put in the issue summary?
  3. **Evidence-base verifiability** — Run the reference check. All six DOIs sit under the `10.5555` CrossRef *test/reserved* prefix in a sequential block (2050001–2050006), and none of the six journal titles is a recognisable indexed outlet. Establish whether these are real, unverifiable, or placeholder before any substantive verdict is issued.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether well-calibrated hedging is being used as a substitute for contribution. A paper that correctly says "this shows very little" has told the truth, but truthfulness is not novelty, and the verdict must track the criteria rather than the modesty of the register.
**Possible blind spots**: Likely to under-credit the paper's genuinely exemplary statistical reporting (CI, sensitivity analysis, robustness check, refusal of causal language) because novelty gatekeeping dominates the editorial reading. May also skip past the missing response rate, which is a methods matter rather than a scope matter.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Identity Description**: Quantitative survey methodologist and psychometrician in a school of education; research programme on measurement error in single-item self-report measures and on nonresponse bias in institutional student surveys; regularly reviews for *Educational and Psychological Measurement* and teaches the graduate correlational-design sequence.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **Single-item ordinal outcome** — The outcome is one 5-point frequency item with unknown reliability. Its measurement error attenuates r downward, while Pearson treatment of a 5-category ordinal variable with unequal psychological intervals ("rarely or never" to "several times daily") pulls in the other direction. The Spearman check (ρ = .40) addresses monotonicity but not reliability. Ask for the verbatim item wording, the full category frequency distribution, and an explicit statement of the attenuation direction.
  2. **Missing denominator and nonresponse** — "All enrolled undergraduates were eligible" with 233 responses, but the sampling frame size is never given, so no response rate can be computed and Limitation 4 (voluntary-response bias) is asserted without magnitude. Also required: whether the 214 respondents' year-level and discipline distribution matches the institutional population. "Spanned all four year levels" is not a distribution.
  3. **Common method variance** — Both constructs were captured in the same instrument, at the same moment, from the same respondents, with no marker variable or procedural separation. A material portion of r = .42 is plausibly method-shared. This is not a limitation the paper currently names.
  4. **Instrument adaptation and analysis provenance** — The scale was "adapted" from a prior instrument but only α = .88 is reported: no dimensionality check, no evidence the adaptation preserved the original factor structure. Separately, the power statement is a *sensitivity* analysis on an availability sample, which is legitimate but should be labelled as such rather than read as a priori design.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether the headline coefficient is reported with enough surrounding information (item wording, distributions, response rate, r² stated numerically rather than described as "modest") for a reader to independently appraise or replicate it.
**Possible blind spots**: Will engage almost nothing with the paper's theoretical positioning or practical value. Risks generating a revision list that cannot be executed on already-collected anonymous data, and may treat "add a second use item" as a revision request when it is in fact a new data collection.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #3

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Identity Description**: Higher education researcher specialising in technology acceptance in tertiary learning environments; author of a synthesis of TAM-derived studies in e-learning and fluent in the Davis → Venkatesh → Bhattacherjee lineage and its meta-analytic record; editorial board member of a Q2 educational technology journal.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **Literature base adequacy** — Six references cannot support a technology-acceptance paper. Absent are the foundational sources (Davis 1989; Venkatesh & Davis 2000; Venkatesh et al. 2003 UTAUT; Bhattacherjee's expectation-confirmation extension) and, more damagingly for this paper's own logic, the existing meta-analyses of perceived usefulness in e-learning contexts.
  2. **The incrementality claim is self-undermining without a comparator** — The paper explicitly positions itself as "one point in a distribution" (echoing its own Song, 2018 caution). That framing *requires* the distribution be specified. Where do published pooled PU–use estimates sit, and is r = .42 inside, above, or below that interval? Without this, the increment is asserted rather than demonstrated, and the paper's central rhetorical move fails on its own terms.
  3. **Construct slippage** — "Use," "engagement," "adoption," and "continued use" are used interchangeably across the abstract, literature review, and discussion; these are distinct constructs with distinct predictors in this literature. Additionally, TAM specifies perceived usefulness → behavioural *intention* → behaviour. The paper skips the intention mediator without acknowledging that it is doing so, which matters when the outcome is self-reported rather than observed.
  4. **Whether the disclaimers are load-bearing or decorative** — The paper cites Delgado (2020) on reverse causality and Vasquez (2020) on self-report divergence, then proceeds with a cross-sectional self-report design. Citing a caution is not the same as responding to it. Assess whether any design choice was actually made differently because of these cited warnings.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether the authors demonstrate command of the field they claim to add an increment to. Conflating perception-of-use with use, and adoption with continuance, signals otherwise.
**Possible blind spots**: Strong pull toward scope creep — demanding a full TAM/UTAUT specification, mediation model, or additional constructs that 214 responses on two measures cannot support. The paper's narrow question is a legitimate design choice; this reviewer may not concede that.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #4

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Identity Description**: Learning analytics researcher embedded in a university's institutional research office; builds LMS trace-data pipelines across Moodle and Canvas, co-chairs the institutional data-ethics working group, and previously ran a campus-wide LMS onboarding redesign. Reads adoption papers as an operator deciding whether to fund something.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **The cheapest available validation was foreclosed by design, not by circumstance** — Every LMS in production emits per-user access logs. The study's second-largest limitation (self-report vs. behavioural traces, which the paper itself cites Vasquez, 2020 about) was solvable *at the same institution* via consented log linkage. Because no identifiers were collected, linkage is now permanently impossible. That is a defensible privacy choice, but it is a *choice with a stated cost* and the paper presents it as an unavoidable constraint. Ask the authors to discuss the tradeoff explicitly, including the consent and ethics pathway that would have permitted linkage.
  2. **LMS access is bursty and calendar-driven, and the design ignores the calendar** — Real access patterns spike at assessment deadlines and collapse in mid-term troughs. A single "typical week" retrospective self-estimate is a poor summary statistic for a cyclical behaviour, and the three-week collection window is never located in the semester. If collection sat near an assessment cluster, both perceived usefulness and reported frequency shift together for structural reasons unrelated to acceptance.
  3. **Compelled use vs. chosen use** — The paper never states which LMS, nor which functions are mandatory. If assignment submission, grade release, or attendance runs exclusively through the platform, a large share of reported frequency is *compelled*, and perceived usefulness cannot explain it in the way the discussion implies. This single omitted paragraph changes how the entire correlation should be read.
  4. **Actionability of the onboarding implication** — "Help students see concrete usefulness" is not an implementable specification. Which usefulness facets? Which student segments? At what point in the enrolment cycle? An institution cannot cost or schedule an intervention from r = .42, and the paper should either specify what it would take to get there or drop the recommendation.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether the study describes its platform and temporal context in enough detail for anyone at another institution to interpret, replicate, or act on the finding. Currently it does not.
**Possible blind spots**: May over-privilege behavioural logs and undervalue perceived use as a legitimate construct in its own right (student *perception* of engagement matters independently of clickstream truth). Also inclined to push toward data-linkage solutions that carry real consent and surveillance costs this reviewer is professionally comfortable with but the students may not be.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

**1. This paper's defining feature is calibration, which changes the reviewers' job.** Claims are hedged appropriately, causal language is refused throughout, internal arithmetic reconciles, and the limitations section pre-empts most standard objections. The usual reviewing reflex — catch the overclaim — has little purchase here. The real question is harder: does a well-hedged minimal contribution clear the bar? Reviewers must resolve that against the criteria, and the paper's modest register must not move the verdict in either direction. Rewarding hedging *as if it were contribution* is the specific failure mode to guard against; so is penalising the paper for candour.

**2. Mandatory anti-phantom-criticism instruction.** Do not criticise this paper for causal overclaiming, for ignoring reverse causality, for treating self-report as behaviour, or for over-generalising from one site. It explicitly disclaims all four. Every criticism must quote the text it targets. Reviewers who attack the standard version of this paper rather than *this* paper should have those points struck at synthesis.

**3. Reference integrity is a gating item that outranks methodology.** All six references carry sequential DOIs under the `10.5555` reserved test prefix, and none of the six journal titles corresponds to a verifiable indexed outlet. This must be reported as an **observation requiring verification, not an accusation** — placeholder DOIs in a working draft are a plausible innocent explanation. But if the citations cannot be resolved, no methodological commentary matters, because the paper's entire framing (situating itself against prior findings, citing cautions from Delgado and Vasquez, adapting a validated instrument from Costa & Wren) rests on sources that cannot be checked. Assign primary responsibility to the Journal-Fit Reviewer, with Reviewer 2 corroborating.

**4. Anticipated convergence, and the trap inside it.** All three peer reviewers will independently conclude that the design cannot be fixed post hoc: R1 wants better measurement, R2 wants a comparator distribution, R3 wants log data. Summed naively, that is a demand for a different study. The synthesiser must sort every request into two bins:
   - *Executable in revision*: report the sampling-frame denominator and response rate; report the use-item verbatim wording and category distribution; state r² numerically; name the LMS and its mandatory functions; locate the collection window in the academic calendar; add the foundational and meta-analytic literature; position r = .42 against published pooled estimates; add a common-method-variance limitation; reframe the log-data limitation as a design tradeoff with its cost stated; add funding, conflict-of-interest, and data-availability statements.
   - *Requires new data*: log validation, multi-institution sampling, longitudinal waves, multi-item use measure, intention mediator.
   Only the first bin belongs in a revision request. The second belongs in a future-research paragraph, which the paper already partly has.

**5. Managed overlap between Journal-Fit Reviewer and Reviewer 2.** Both will land on "insufficient contribution." Keep them at different altitudes: the Journal-Fit Reviewer adjudicates *scope, article category, and readership* (is this a Research Note?); Reviewer 2 adjudicates *literature command and construct precision*. If both simply write "not novel enough," one of the four slots has been wasted.

**6. Coverage note.** The paper is moderately cross-disciplinary (education + information systems + measurement + analytics). Reviewer 2 holds the core discipline; Reviewer 1 holds measurement; Reviewer 3 holds the analytics/operations angle, which is the perspective the authors most visibly have not considered — the observation that the paper's headline limitation was created by its own recruitment design rather than imposed on it is unlikely to arise from any other seat at the table.

**7. Register.** Developmental-constructive is warranted: the reporting discipline here is above the median for this genre and the authors clearly know what a defensible claim looks like. Tone accordingly. But tone adjusts wording only. On current evidence the verdict lands at Major Revision at a Q3 outlet, conditional on reference verification; if the references cannot be resolved, the recommendation is Reject with invitation to resubmit once the evidence base is verifiable.
