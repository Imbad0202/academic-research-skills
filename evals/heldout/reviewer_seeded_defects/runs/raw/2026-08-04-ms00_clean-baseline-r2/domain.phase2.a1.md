## Review Body

Let me start by conceding what this reviewer is most tempted to demand and will not: a full TAM or UTAUT specification, a behavioural-intention mediator, or additional exogenous constructs. Two measures and 214 responses cannot carry a structural model, and the authors' decision to ask one narrow correlational question is a legitimate design choice, not a deficiency. The paper's hedging discipline is also genuine rather than performative in most places — the causal language is correctly withheld throughout, the reverse pathway is stated with the right symmetry, and the practical implication is explicitly marked as suggested rather than demonstrated. Those are not small things; a great many submissions in this literature fail exactly there.

My block rests on a different problem, and it is one the paper creates for itself. The manuscript disclaims theory testing and disclaims causal inference, which leaves it with exactly one contribution: positional. It claims to be "an incremental data point, comparable with prior work," and asserts three separate times — abstract, Discussion, Conclusion — that r = .42 is "consistent with prior technology-acceptance research." It then borrows Song (2018) for precisely the right framing, that a single-site estimate is "one point in a distribution rather than as a fixed value." That framing is a commitment. If the estimate is a point in a distribution, the distribution has to be specified, and it is not specified anywhere in the manuscript. There is no pooled coefficient, no interval, no range, not even Song's own reported spread across the multiple campuses the paper says Song surveyed. "Consistent with prior research" against an unstated reference distribution is unfalsifiable: r = .18 and r = .61 would both have earned the same sentence. The paper's sole claim to contribution therefore cannot stand as written.

This is not a hard defect to repair, and the repair needs no new data. The meta-analytic record on perceived usefulness in e-learning and LMS contexts exists and is not thin; the authors should locate their coefficient inside it explicitly and say whether .42 sits inside, above, or below the published interval. I deliberately will not supply a pooled figure from memory for the authors to cite — that is their obligation and it should be done against the actual sources — but I will note that the reference list contains none of that record, and contains none of the lineage the paper's own framing invokes: Davis (1989), from whom the paper's PU definition is drawn almost verbatim with "job performance" generalised; Venkatesh and Davis (2000); Venkatesh et al. (2003); Bhattacherjee (2001) on the expectation-confirmation extension, which matters here because the instrument the authors adapt is described in its own title as a *continued use* instrument.

That last point opens the second substantive problem: the paper does not hold its outcome construct still. In §2 the authors state their construct decision plainly — the self-report item is "an indicator of perceived use rather than a behavioral count" — and then the abstract's closing sentence reports that perceived usefulness "tracks with LMS engagement." Perceived use and engagement are not the same object; engagement in this literature is multidimensional and is not measured by a single typical-week access-frequency item. The same paragraph that makes the careful construct decision also folds "adoption and continued use" into one clause, and adoption and continuance are distinct outcomes with distinct predictor profiles — that distinction is the whole reason Bhattacherjee's extension exists. The authors' actual outcome is neither: it is habitual access frequency among currently enrolled students. Once the construct is stabilised, a further consequence follows that the paper never draws. If both variables are perceptions, the coefficient is a perception–perception association, and the authors' own cited source on self-report divergence implies the reported .42 is more plausibly an upper bound on the perceived-usefulness–actual-use relationship than an unbiased estimate of it. The paper cites Vasquez (2020), repeats the caution in Limitations, and then never lets it touch the number's interpretation or its comparability to log-based estimates. Citing a caution is not the same as responding to one.

Finally, a verification matter I can flag but not adjudicate. None of the six cited sources is recognisable to me as part of the technology-acceptance record I work in, all six DOIs share the 10.5555 test prefix and run in sequence, and three of the six journal titles are near-variants of real journals rather than the journals themselves. I am not asserting fabrication and I do not have the standing to; I am reporting that I could not verify a single one of the paper's characterisations of prior work, which is the layer this dimension exists to check.

### S1: Complete and discipline-appropriate reporting of the focal association
The authors report the point estimate with its confidence interval, p value, and n, and pre-commit to a rank-based robustness check appropriate to an ordinal outcome. This is better inferential hygiene than the median paper in this literature, and the Spearman coefficient's proximity to the Pearson value is reported rather than merely claimed.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.4 — "we also computed a Spearman correlation as a robustness check" and "we report the correlation coefficient alongside its 95% confidence interval, p value, and sample size"

### S2: Correct symmetry in the reverse-causality statement
Many cross-sectional acceptance papers nod at reverse causality and then quietly retain a directional reading. This one states the reverse path as *equally* consistent with the data, which is the correct characterisation of a bivariate cross-sectional correlation and is the strongest single sentence in the manuscript.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 Discussion — "the reverse pathway, in which more frequent use raises perceived usefulness, is equally consistent with the data"

### S3: Scope is declared narrowly and honestly, with no smuggled model test
The Introduction states the question descriptively and correlationally and explicitly declines to test an acceptance model. The paper then keeps that promise: no path language, no mediation implied, no model-fit rhetoric. Reviewers should not punish this restraint, and I am not.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 Introduction — "It asks a deliberately narrow question" and "We frame the question descriptively and correlationally."

### S4: The practical implication is correctly demoted
The onboarding recommendation is presented as a possibility and expressly flagged as not established by the correlation. This is the standard failure point in applied LMS papers and the authors avoided it.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 Discussion — "this implication is suggested by, not proven by, the present correlation"

### W1: The incrementality claim has no comparator, so the paper's only stated contribution is unevaluable
The manuscript disclaims theory testing and causal inference, leaving positional comparability as its sole contribution, and asserts consistency with prior technology-acceptance research three times without ever specifying the distribution it claims membership in. No pooled estimate, interval, or numeric range from any cited study appears anywhere — including from Song (2018), whose multi-campus spread the paper invokes conceptually but never reports. A secondary ambiguity compounds this: the paper does not say *which* prior quantity it claims consistency with. TAM specifies perceived usefulness acting on behaviour through behavioural intention, so an unmediated bivariate perceived-usefulness–use correlation is not the same estimand as a TAM path coefficient. I am not asking the authors to estimate the mediated model; I am asking them to name the quantity their .42 is being compared against. The fix is literature work in §2 and §5, not new data.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §2 Literature Review and §5 Discussion — expected a pooled or interval estimate for the perceived-usefulness/use association from prior work against which r = .42 is positioned; checked abstract, §1, §2, §4, §5, §7, reference list
**Confidence**: 5 — author of a synthesis of TAM-derived e-learning studies; the absence is verifiable by exhaustive reading of the manuscript

### W2: The outcome construct is not held constant, and the abstract contradicts the paper's own construct decision
§2 commits to treating the self-report item as an indicator of *perceived* use rather than a behavioural count. The abstract's headline sentence then reports that perceived usefulness tracks with LMS *engagement*, a broader and multidimensional construct that a single typical-week access-frequency item does not measure. The same §2 paragraph also collapses "adoption and continued use" into one clause; these are distinct outcomes with distinct predictors, and the instrument being adapted is itself described as a continued-use instrument, while the sample is a general undergraduate population rather than an established-user cohort. Repair requires the authors to fix one construct label, align abstract, §2, §4, §5, and §7 to it, and state which of adoption, continuance, or habitual access their outcome operationalises.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract and §2 — "treat our self-report measure as an indicator of perceived use rather than a behavioral count" versus "perceived usefulness tracks with LMS engagement among undergraduates"
**Confidence**: 5 — the adoption/continuance and engagement distinctions are core to the literature this paper positions itself in

### W3: The cited self-report caution is acknowledged but never allowed to bear on the coefficient's interpretation
Limitations restates that self-reports diverge from behavioural traces, but the manuscript never draws the directional consequence for its own number. If, per the paper's own construct decision, both variables are perceptions, then .42 is a perception–perception association carrying shared-method inflation and is more defensibly read as an upper bound on the perceived-usefulness–actual-use relationship than as an estimate of it. That also makes the coefficient not like-for-like with any prior estimate derived from log data — which is the comparison class the Conclusion itself points future work toward. The disclaimer is currently decorative: it names a threat and changes no interpretation.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 Limitations — "self-reports of technology use are known to diverge from behavioral traces"
**Confidence**: 4 — direction of shared-method bias in perception–perception designs is well established, though its magnitude here is unknown

### W4: I could not verify a single cited source, which is the layer this dimension is supposed to check
All six references carry DOIs on the 10.5555 test prefix in consecutive sequence, and three journal titles are near-variants of real outlets rather than those outlets. None of the six is recognisable within the technology-acceptance record. I am not alleging fabrication and cannot adjudicate it; I am reporting that every characterisation of prior work in §1, §2, and §5 — including the definition attribution, the Song distributional framing, and the Vasquez divergence claim — is unverifiable as submitted. The authors should supply resolvable citations. If editorial verification establishes that these sources do not exist, this ceases to be a revisable domain matter and becomes an integrity determination outside a reviewer's remit.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: References — "https://doi.org/10.5555/2050001" and "https://doi.org/10.5555/2050006"
**Confidence**: 4 — high confidence in the observation and in my familiarity with this literature; deliberately low confidence in any inference about cause

### W5: Foundational sources absent and the field's core proposition attributed to 2019–2020 secondary citations
The manuscript defines perceived usefulness in terms that are Davis's (1989) operational definition with "job performance" generalised, and credits the proposition that perceived usefulness relates to adoption and continued use to Costa and Wren (2019) and Delgado (2020). It also characterises the evidence base as substantial while supporting that characterisation with a single citation. Six references cannot warrant field-level statements of this kind. Adding Davis (1989), Venkatesh and Davis (2000), Venkatesh et al. (2003), and Bhattacherjee (2001) is a reference-and-attribution fix that does not alter the reported result, which is why I rate it Minor despite its bearing on the paper's demonstrated command of the field.
**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 and §2 — "a substantial body of work suggests" and "Research on technology acceptance has long proposed that perceived usefulness"
**Confidence**: 5 — the lineage and the origin of the definition are unambiguous

### W6: "Previously validated" over-transfers from the source instrument to the adapted version
The abstract and §3.2 present the measure as previously validated, but what is reported for the adapted six-item version in this sample is internal consistency alone. Alpha is a reliability index; validation of a source instrument does not automatically transfer to an adaptation, particularly one moved from a continued-use framing to a general undergraduate population. The claim should be narrowed to "adapted from a validated instrument, with internal consistency re-established in the present sample," or supported with structural evidence for the adapted form.
**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 — "a six-item scale adapted from Costa and Wren (2019), whose original instrument reported strong internal consistency"
**Confidence**: 4 — standard instrument-adaptation expectation in this literature; the fix may be as small as rewording
