## Review Strategy Recommendations

- **This paper's defining characteristic is a gap between surface polish and substantive integrity.** The prose, citation formatting, and narrative are pre-submission quality, which makes the reviewer team's core job *contradiction-hunting*, not developmental encouragement. Configure all four reviewers to read the abstract, Methods, Findings, and Discussion against one another rather than section-by-section.
- **Hard integrity flags that must not fall through the cracks (assign explicit ownership):** (1) 14 vs. 12 administrators — R1 primary, EIC secondary; (2) 48 survey respondents vs. 9+11 subgroup cells covering only two of three sites — R1 primary; (3) anonymization claim vs. re-identifying quote attributions — R3 primary, EIC aware as a desk-level concern; (4) Delacroix (2018) inverted between Literature Review and Discussion — R2 primary; (5) "balanced/full range of views" vs. "dissenters excluded for space" — R1 and R3; (6) sector-wide generalization from three cases — EIC and R3; (7) over-stated analytic-confidence language ("emerged systematically... giving us confidence") — R1. No two reviewers are assigned the same primary flag, satisfying the non-overlap gate.
- **Reviewer complementarity is deliberate and covers the full defect space:** EIC = fit + whole-paper claim calibration + desk-screen integrity; R1 = analytic procedure, sampling logic, and the statistics; R2 = citation fidelity and field positioning; R3 = ethics/confidentiality and generalization-as-accountability. R3's angle (research-integrity + public-administration accountability) is a genuinely different disciplinary perspective, not merely "broader," and is the one most likely to catch what the author did not anticipate (the confidentiality breach).
- **Likely inter-reviewer tension to watch:** R2 (domain) may be inclined to accept plausible, field-consistent themes at face value, while R1 (method) will press on whether the procedure could yield them and R3 will press on selection bias. The synthesizer should treat R1/R3's process concerns as gating for a study whose whole thesis is that *process integrity* matters — the paper cannot credibly argue that QA should be "meaningful rather than performative" while itself performing rigor it has not demonstrated. Conversely, the synthesizer should ensure R1/R3 do not so bury the paper in defects that R2's fair point — that the negotiated-accomplishment framing is a real and salvageable contribution — is lost.
- **Recommended overall stance:** major revision framing, not reject-on-sight. The contribution is recoverable if the numbers are reconciled, the Delacroix reading is corrected (which actually *strengthens* the argument), the anonymization/attribution conflict is resolved, the excluded dissenting views are reported, the statistical claim is down-weighted or removed, and the generalization is scaled back to the three studied institutions. Reviewers should be constructive about the framing while firm and specific about every integrity flag.

### Summary Assessment

This QUAL-dominant mixed-methods study of administrator perspectives on QA implementation has a defensible design *shape* — purposive recruitment of QA-responsible administrators, in-depth interviews, thematic analysis, a supplementary survey — but the manuscript as written cannot be trusted at the procedural level, and the failure is a methodology failure, not a copy-edit one. Three defects are load-bearing. First, the study's own sample counts contradict each other: the abstract and Findings say fourteen administrators; §3.2 says twelve; the survey is n=48 but the only reported subgroup analysis uses 9+11=20 respondents drawn from two of three sites, never reconciled. A reader cannot know how many people were actually studied. Second, the analysis section describes reflexive-style iterative thematic analysis, yet §4.1 claims themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" — a method-to-claim mismatch — and §4.3 asserts "balanced representation… the full range of views" in the same breath as admitting dissenting participants "were excluded." That is a self-cancelling selection move that voids the balance claim on which the thematic findings rest. Third, a single uncorrected p<.05 test on tiny cells is read up into "institutional type as a possible moderator." The design is repairable, but not by minor revision; the counts, the analysis-claim mismatch, and the selection contradiction must all be fixed before the evidence can be believed.

### Strengths

1. **Appropriate paradigm-to-question fit**: The research question — how administrators *experience and enact* QA implementation (§1) — is genuinely a qualitative question, and a QUAL-dominant interview design with a contextualizing survey (§3.1) is the right instrument for it. The design shape is not the problem; the reporting is.

2. **Purposive, role-anchored recruitment**: §3.2 recruits administrators "with direct QA responsibilities — including quality directors, associate deans, and registry heads" across three deliberately contrasting institution types (public research, public teaching-focused, private). This is recognizable purposive/maximum-variation logic, not a convenience grab, and the role anchoring is appropriate to the RQ.

3. **Adequate basic data-collection detail for the interviews**: §3.3 reports mode (in person / video), duration (45–70 min), and recording/transcription. For a ~2,300-word manuscript this is a reasonable procedural floor for the qualitative strand, even though the protocol content itself is not shown.

4. **Ethics review present in shape**: §3.5 reports institutional ethical approval and informed consent — the reproducibility affordance exists, even though its anonymization claim is later contradicted by the reporting (see W4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Internally contradictory sample counts — a data-integrity defect, not a typo (D1 → warn)**: The abstract and §4/§5 narrative say "Fourteen administrators"; §3.2 says "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)." Separately, the survey is "48 mid-level staff (n=48)" (abstract, §3.2), but the only reported subgroup analysis (§4.4) uses research-university n=9 vs. teaching-focused n=11 — 20 respondents, from two of three sites, never reconciled against 48 (no report of the third site's n, no report of non-respondents or missing data). *Why it's a problem*: the denominator of the entire study is unknown, so no adequacy-of-sample or saturation judgment is even possible, and the survey's descriptive base (M=3.9, SD=0.6) cannot be located within the reported N. *How to improve*: reconcile the administrator count to a single figure and explain the discrepancy; report the full survey disposition (invited / responded / per-site n / missing), and either analyze all 48 or state and justify the analytic subsample.

2. **Analysis-method-to-claim mismatch plus rigor-signaling language (D1 → warn)**: §3.4 describes iterative thematic analysis ("moving between transcripts and an emerging set of themes until a stable structure was reached") — a reflexive-analysis idiom. But §4.1 states themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol, giving us confidence that the pattern was not an artifact." Thematic analysis is not a "structured protocol" method, and "emerged… giving us confidence" is an overstated inference from an unspecified procedure. The section also omits every standard trustworthiness affordance: number of coders, inter-coder agreement or reflexive-team process, an explicit saturation basis, member checking, reflexivity statement, and audit trail. *Why it's a problem*: "systematically," "confidence," and "iteratively… until stable" are doing rhetorical work that the described procedure does not earn; a reader cannot audit whether the three themes are a product of method or of expectation. *How to improve*: name the specific thematic-analysis variant (e.g., reflexive TA), state coder count and coding process, give the saturation/adequacy rationale, and remove "structured protocol"/"systematically" unless an actual structured procedure is documented.

3. **Self-cancelling selection claim (D1 → warn; feeds D3)**: §4.3 asserts the study "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views," then in the same paragraph states that dissenting participants "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure." *Why it's a problem*: excluding disconfirming views because they do not fit the theme structure is textbook confirmation bias, and it directly falsifies the "full range of views" claim — the two statements cannot both be true. This undercuts the trustworthiness of all three themes, since the reader cannot know what proportion of participants the reported structure actually represents. *How to improve*: report the dissenting/skeptical views (they are analytically important negative cases, not filler), and either substantiate the "balanced/full range" claim with the disconfirming evidence included or withdraw it.

4. **Anonymization claim contradicted by re-identifying attributions (D1 → warn)**: §3.5 states data were "fully anonymized… so that no individual could be identified," yet §4.1 attributes quotes to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university." In a three-institution design with single postholders, role-plus-institution descriptors are re-identifying. *Why it's a problem*: this is a reproducibility/ethics-procedure inconsistency that touches the reported consent basis — the described anonymization procedure was not the procedure actually followed. (I flag the design-consistency face of this; the human-subjects/consent implications are Reviewer 3's primary territory.) *How to improve*: either strip institution-linked role descriptors from quote attributions or document the specific consent for identifiable attribution; reconcile §3.5's stated procedure with §4's practice.

5. **Over-interpreted single small-sample subgroup test (D1 → warn; also D3)**: §4.4 reports one uncorrected p<.05 comparison on n=9 vs. n=11 and reads it up into "institutional type as a possible moderator." No effect size, no confidence interval, no assumption check, no correction, and the survey instrument itself is undescribed (items, scale points, anchors, reliability, validity) — M=3.9/SD=0.6 floats without a measurement model. *Why it's a problem*: a single significance test on tiny cells, selected from among the possible role/site comparisons, is a p-hacking/selective-reporting risk, and "moderator" language implies a modeling claim the design cannot support. *How to improve*: describe the instrument (items, scale, reliability); report effect size + CI; down-weight to a descriptive difference or remove the inferential claim; if retained, justify the single comparison and correct for the comparison set.

### Detailed Comments

#### Research Questions & Hypotheses
The RQ (§1) is clear, answerable, and correctly matched to a qualitative design. No a priori hypotheses are posited, which is appropriate for an exploratory thematic study; the quantitative strand carries no stated hypothesis, so its inferential test in §4.4 is post-hoc and should be framed as such.

#### Research Design
§3.1 declares a QUAL-dominant mixed-methods design with the survey as contextualizing — this is a legitimate design type. However, the *mixed-methods integration point is underdeveloped*: the strands are reported side by side (§4.1–4.3 qualitative, §4.4 survey) and only loosely joined in narrative ("corroborated," "adds a note of complication," §4.4/§5). The design does not fully collapse into two unconnected fragments — there is a stated contextualizing rationale and a discussion-level meta-inference — so this is a warn-level integration gap, not a block-level absence. Name the design (convergent? sequential?) and specify where and how the strands are integrated.

#### Sampling Strategy
Purposive, role-anchored, maximum-variation-by-institution-type logic is present and appropriate (§3.2). The fatal problem is not the strategy but the *unreconciled counts* (W1) and the *absence of any saturation/adequacy rationale* for the qualitative sample. Twelve (or fourteen) administrators across three sites can be entirely reasonable for reflexive TA, but the manuscript must commit to one number and state the adequacy basis.

#### Data Collection
Interview procedure is reported at an adequate floor (mode, 45–70 min, recorded, transcribed; §3.3). The interview protocol content is not shown, and the survey instrument is entirely undescribed (§3.3 says only that it "captured respondents' perceptions… along with basic role and tenure information"). The survey undescription is a real gap because an inferential claim (§4.4) is built on it.

#### Analysis Methods
Thematic analysis is named but under-specified, and the "structured protocol"/"emerged systematically" language contradicts the iterative description (W2). The quantitative analysis is a single uncorrected small-cell significance test with no effect size, CI, or assumption reporting (W5). Neither strand meets the reporting bar as written.

#### Results Presentation
Results are legible as prose but **incomplete and internally inconsistent**: disconfirming views are acknowledged as existing and then withheld (§4.3, W3); no results table is provided for the survey (only inline M/SD and one p-value); and the sample counts conflict across sections (W1). Selective-reporting risk is present and self-declared.

#### Reproducibility
Ethics approval and consent are reported (§3.5) but the anonymization procedure is contradicted by practice (W4). No data-availability or analysis-artifact statement, no interview protocol, no survey instrument, no codebook — a second researcher could not reconstruct the analysis. Given the ~2,300-word budget, terseness is expected, but the *missing procedures* here (coder count, saturation basis, instrument) are not explained by word budget.

#### Methodological Fallacies Detected
- **Confirmation bias**: dissenting cases excluded because they "fell outside the three-theme structure" (§4.3) while claiming "full range of views."
- **Ecological / unit-of-inference slip and overgeneralization**: §5 reads a three-institution, ~12-administrator study as "a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole" and asserts "universities across the sector treat QA as…" — an inference well beyond a purposive sample of three sites (D3 concern).
- **P-hacking / selective-reporting risk**: single uncorrected p<.05 subgroup test elevated to "moderator" (§4.4).

#### Assumption Audit

- **Explicit assumptions**: The paper explicitly assumes administrator accounts illuminate "the formation of institutional quality culture."
