### D3: argumentative_coherence
score: block
trigger: "a causal, prescriptive, or generalising claim drawn from evidence that can only license description or association"
block_class: repairable

The Discussion and Conclusion convert twelve (or fourteen — the manuscript does not know) perceptual interview accounts at three institutions in one national system into (a) a sector-wide empirical claim about "universities across the sector" and "administrators everywhere", and (b) a forward-looking efficacy claim that differently designed QA processes "are more likely to foster the internalized commitment" the paper never measured. Both are inferences the design cannot license at any level of hedging. Independently, the third Discussion finding rests on a citation the manuscript itself summarises in the opposite sense two sections earlier, and the three-theme structure is protected from disconfirmation by an exclusion rule that removes the dissenting cases for not fitting the three themes. I considered `fatal` and rejected it: the interview corpus apparently still contains the excluded accounts, so honest re-reporting, deletion of the sector-general and efficacy claims, and correction of the Delacroix attribution could rebuild a defensible (much narrower) argument from existing data. That is substantial re-analysis and rewriting, not an unrescuable argument.

## Review Body

I read this manuscript looking for the seams where the prose carries weight the evidence does not. There are five, and they are not variations on one complaint: an inverted load-bearing citation, a scope jump from three cases to the sector, an exclusion rule that immunises the findings against disconfirmation, a prescriptive efficacy claim with no outcome variable anywhere in the design, and an ethics statement the Findings section falsifies on its first page. Each would independently stall this paper; I list them separately because each has a different repair, and none inherits its band from the others.

The most damaging defect is the Delacroix reversal. Section 2 states, accurately and at length, that Delacroix (2018) "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual" and argues that consultation can become "one more artifact to be documented for the panel" — a position the reference-list annotation confirms. Section 5 then recruits the same source as recommending "broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture" and calls consultation "the engine of internalization." These are not two emphases of one position; they are contradictories, and the manuscript asserts both. Worse, the inverted version is the only citational support offered for the paper's central design recommendation. Strip it and the recommendation has no warrant beyond the authors' preference, because interview accounts of how administrators *feel* about persuasion work cannot establish which process designs *produce* internalisation. The authors owe an explanation of how a source they read correctly in Section 2 became its own opposite in Section 5.

The second seam is the scope jump. Section 3.2 defines the base as three institutions in one national system; Section 5 asserts that "these findings demonstrate that universities across the sector treat QA as a negotiated accomplishment" and that "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension." The paper then refutes itself in Section 6 by calling for "future research" to "test these dynamics across a wider range of institutional types and national systems." If the general account is already established, the recommended test is redundant; if the test is needed, the general account is not established. One of those two sentences must go, and the honest casualty is the sector claim. The related verb choice in Section 5 — the ritual theme "confirms long-standing concerns" — has the same problem at smaller scale: a purposively recruited, non-representative sample can illustrate or complicate a prior concern, not confirm it.

Third, Section 4.3 asserts "balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views" and then, in the next sentence, reports that dissenting participants "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis." Read as reasoning, this is circular: the three themes are the criterion for admitting evidence, and the themes are then presented as what the evidence produced. Any disconfirming account is, by construction, outside the structure and therefore out of the paper. That makes the thematic finding unfalsifiable as reported, and it makes the two claims in that paragraph flatly incompatible. There is also an arithmetic hint worth the authors' attention: the abstract's fourteen minus Section 3.2's twelve is exactly the "minority" described as excluded, which would mean disconfirming participants were removed from the reported N rather than merely from the narrative. If that is wrong, the numbers must be reconciled; if it is right, the exclusion is far more serious than "space."

Fourth, the payoff claim has no measured outcome behind it. Nothing in Section 3 varies QA process design across sites, and nothing measures internalised commitment as a dependent variable; the survey measures perceived quality culture at one time point with no link to process features. The Conclusion nonetheless claims that processes "designed with this negotiated character in mind" are "more likely to foster the internalized commitment that the language of quality culture promises," and the abstract promises "implications for how universities design QA processes." A comparative-effectiveness claim requires either design variation with outcomes or an explicit causal identification argument. Neither exists here.

Fifth, Section 3.5 states that "all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis so that no individual could be identified in reported findings." Section 4.1 then attributes a quotation to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region," and Section 3.2 establishes that exactly one private university is in the sample. That is a single, publicly identifiable person, and the same paragraph pattern recurs with "the associate dean for quality at the public research university." Either the anonymity statement is false or the attributions must be redacted; as printed, both cannot stand.

Beyond these, four narrower reasoning failures matter. Section 4.1's claim that themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" mixes incompatible warrants — emergence is inductive, protocol structure is not, and Sections 3.1 and 3.3 describe a *semi*-structured instrument — and the conclusion drawn from it, that structure gives "confidence that the pattern was not an artifact of any single institution's local circumstances," does not follow from anything about instrument design. Section 4.4 labels the survey as having "corroborated the qualitative picture" and then, two sentences later, reports that it contradicted what the interviews had "led us to anticipate," with Section 5 finally calling it "a note of complication"; a datum that counts as corroboration when it agrees and as complication when it does not is doing no evidential work. The subgroup inference names institutional type as "a possible moderator" on the strength of one contrast between two institutions with one institution per type, which makes type formally indistinguishable from site, uses 20 of 48 respondents with the private institution absent and unexplained, and reports "p<.05" with no named test, effect size, or multiplicity handling. And the priority claim — "the first comprehensive account" — is contradicted by the paper's own Section 2, which describes Pettersen (2022) and Rahman (2020) as turning "attention toward the quality office itself" and "closest in spirit to the present study," while "comprehensive" sits badly beside twelve participants in one system with dissenters removed.

Two smaller inconsistencies for the record: Section 4.1 places the sample in a "region" while Section 3.2 specifies a single national system; and the abstract's "Fourteen administrators were interviewed in depth" and Section 3.2's "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)" cannot both describe the same study. There is real material here — the identity-work theme in Section 4.3, in particular, is a genuine observation about administrators holding the compliance/meaning gap open rather than resolving it, and it is stated with appropriate restraint. That theme is what the design can actually support, and a rebuilt paper that claims only that much, with the dissenting accounts reinstated, would be worth reading.
