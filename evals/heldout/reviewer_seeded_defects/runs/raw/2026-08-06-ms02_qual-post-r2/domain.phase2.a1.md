## Review Body

I read this manuscript as a comparative internal-QA scholar, checking whether its claims about the quality assurance field are the claims that field's evidence actually supports, whether the sources it invokes say what it reports them as saying, and whether its technical vocabulary carries its accepted meaning.

The paper has a real subject. Administrators occupying the hinge between accreditor and department are genuinely under-represented in the empirical QA literature relative to academics and to policy actors, and the manuscript locates that gap accurately. Its literature review is also, in one crucial respect, correct where the Discussion is not: §2 renders Delacroix's argument faithfully, including the observation that consultation itself becomes an artifact produced for the panel. That makes the §5 reversal all the more damaging, because the manuscript demonstrably knows better and still writes the opposite.

The domain problems cluster in four places. First and most serious is the Delacroix reversal, which is not a wording slip but a load-bearing misattribution: the paper recruits a sceptic of participatory QA as an advocate for it and derives a design recommendation from the recruitment. Second, the manuscript's central construct is never defined. "Quality culture" is imported as an honorific from two secondary sources and then measured as a single Likert mean, with no engagement with the established typologies the field uses to disaggregate it. Third, the paper suppresses the very context that would make its policy claims interpretable — no national system, no regime type, no accreditor, no review model — while writing as though "external QA requirements" had a single stable referent across systems. It does not; a paper that cannot distinguish programme accreditation from institutional audit from ESG-aligned external evaluation cannot tell a reader what tension its administrators are actually negotiating. Fourth, the manuscript overclaims in two directions at once: outward, to "the higher education sector as a whole" and "administrators everywhere," and backward in time, to "the first comprehensive account," a priority claim its own §2 refutes by citing Pettersen (2022) and Rahman (2020).

A related concern, and the one I would ask the authors to answer first in revision: is "administrators" a category at all? Quality directors, associate deans, and registry heads occupy three different occupational positions with different relations to QA — the professional QA officer, the academic manager, and the records custodian. The manuscript's own cited literature separates them. Collapsing them into a single analytic voice and reporting a unified three-theme structure is a construct decision that the paper never defends.

I note two matters where the formal call sits with other seats but the domain-side facts are unambiguous. The reported N differs between abstract and methods, and §3.5's anonymization guarantee is contradicted three paragraphs later by descriptors that identify single office-holders. On the §4.4 subgroup statistic, I defer the inferential question to the methodology seat; my domain point is only that an institutional-type moderator cannot be interpreted at all until the reader knows whether the two institutions answer to the same regulator.

### S1: Delacroix rendered correctly in the literature review

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual"

The §2 treatment of Delacroix, and the matching reference-list annotation, are an accurate and usefully compact statement of a real minority position in the quality-culture literature. The manuscript is capable of the fidelity it abandons in §5.

### S2: Defensible three-strand map of the QA literature

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "The literature on QA in higher education can be organized around three strands."

Governance technology, lived experience, and quality culture as such is a recognisable and reasonable partition of the field, and the authors populate each strand with sources that genuinely belong to it rather than forcing citations into slots.

### S3: The gap is correctly located

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "their account remains largely conceptual rather than grounded in administrators' own words"

The characterisation of the intermediary-administrator strand as conceptually developed but empirically thin is a fair reading of that literature, and it justifies an interview-based study of this population.

### W1: §5 inverts Delacroix (2018) and builds a design recommendation on the inversion

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "our findings align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture"
**Confidence**: 5 — I know this debate's positions and the manuscript contradicts itself internally, so no external verification is required.

Delacroix's argument, as the manuscript's own §2 and reference annotation report, is *against* treating consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture. §5 converts him into the recommendation's principal authority. Uncorrected, this publishes a false attribution and grounds the paper's third contribution on it; the "consultation as engine of internalization" recommendation loses its cited warrant entirely once the source is read correctly, and Delacroix in fact predicts the failure mode the recommendation would produce. Repair requires either substituting a source that holds the attributed position or, more honestly, rewriting the recommendation to engage Delacroix as a challenge to it.

### W2: Priority claim contradicted by the manuscript's own literature review

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 "This paper has offered the first comprehensive account of how university administrators experience and enact QA implementation."
**Confidence**: 5 — the refutation is internal to the manuscript.

§2 cites Pettersen (2022) on distributed leadership in the quality office and Rahman (2020) on identity and vocation among quality professionals, and describes this turn as "closest in spirit to the present study." Twelve interviews in three institutions in one system is also not "comprehensive" by any usage current in the field. The claim needs to be withdrawn and replaced with a specific, defensible statement of increment over Pettersen, Rahman, and Silva and Tan.

### W3: Sector-wide generalization from three institutions in one unnamed system

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension between external requirement and internal meaning"
**Confidence**: 5 — comparative QA work is my area and cross-system variation on exactly this tension is well documented.

§5 escalates from three institutions to "universities across the sector," to "the higher education sector as a whole," and to "everywhere," and uses "demonstrate" for thematic interview data. The field's comparative evidence runs against the universality claim: the shape of the compliance/meaning tension varies with regime type, accreditation stakes, and sectoral funding arrangements. Since the manuscript withholds its own system (see W6), it cannot even locate itself on that variation, let alone extend beyond it. This requires rewriting the Discussion's claim structure, not softening adverbs.

### W4: Core construct never defined or operationalized

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.4 "respondents' overall institutional quality culture score was moderately positive (M=3.9, SD=0.6)"
**Confidence**: 5 — construct definition in quality-culture research is directly my specialism.

"Quality culture" carries the paper's title, research question, findings, and conclusion, and is nowhere defined. It is imported from Brennan and Osei (2020) and Ferreira and Lund (2022) as a settled term, then rendered as a single mean with no named instrument, no item content, no dimensional structure, and no stated relation to any established conceptualization. The field distinguishes structural from cultural elements and multiple culture types; a scalar "quality culture score" without that scaffolding is not interpretable and cannot be compared with anything. As written the central argument is not falsifiable: no observation is specified that would count against "quality culture is an ongoing negotiation."

### W5: Canonical quality-culture literature absent, and the "under-theorized" gap claim does not survive it

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §2 Literature Review and the reference list — expected engagement with the field's canonical quality-culture sources such as ESG 2015 Part 1, Harvey and Green on quality conceptions, Harvey and Stensaker on quality-culture types, the EUA Quality Culture Project, Ehlers, Sursock, and Cardoso and colleagues on academics' perceptions; checked §1 gap statement, all three strands of §2, §5 Discussion, and the 13-item reference list
**Confidence**: 5 — I have led two ESG implementation reviews and know this canon in detail.

§1 asserts that quality culture "remains under-theorized." That claim holds only against the thin selection of sources the manuscript actually cites. Set against the standard literature it is false: quality culture has a two-decade theorization including an explicit typology and a large multi-institution European development programme. ESG 2015 Part 1 is the single most consequential reference frame for internal QA in any European-style national system, and its absence means the paper's "external QA requirements" are never situated in the standards that define them. The revision must either engage this canon and restate the gap much more narrowly — plausibly, an empirical gap at the level of administrator practice rather than a theoretical one — or abandon the under-theorization claim.

### W6: National system, regime, and accreditor withheld, leaving policy claims without a referent

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.2 Participants and setting — expected identification of the national QA regime, the responsible agency or accreditor, the external review model and cycle length, and whether all three institutions fall under the same regulator; checked §1 framing, §3.2, §3.3, §4.4 subgroup interpretation, and §5 transferability discussion
**Confidence**: 5 — judging transferability across national QA systems is my core comparative work.

Anonymizing institutions is standard; anonymizing the system is not, and it is not required by participant protection. The cost is severe. "External QA requirements" (§1) has no referent, so readers cannot tell whether administrators are describing programme accreditation, institutional audit, an ESG-aligned external evaluation, or a state inspection regime — arrangements that impose materially different documentation burdens and produce different compliance dynamics. Cross-national readers cannot assess transferability, and the §4.4 institutional-type moderator cannot be read against known sectoral differences, since public research, public teaching-focused, and private institutions are subject to different regulatory treatment in many systems. Naming the system, or at minimum specifying regime type and cycle, is a low-cost repair with high interpretive payoff.

### W7: "Administrators" collapses three distinct occupational positions

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 "including quality directors, associate deans, and registry heads"
**Confidence**: 4 — the occupational distinctions are well established in the QA-professional literature; I cannot see the role composition of the sample.

These are three different jobs with different relations to QA: the quality director is a QA professional whose mandate is the process itself, the associate dean is an academic manager brokering between faculty and centre, the registry head is a records and compliance custodian. The manuscript's own cited sources treat them separately — Pettersen on the quality office, Rahman on quality professionals. No role breakdown is given, no role-differentiated analysis is attempted, and quotations are attributed to positions without any account of whether themes distributed evenly across them. The "identity work" theme in particular is the kind of finding that plausibly belongs to quality professionals specifically rather than to "administrators" generically. Without role-level analysis the analytic category is unearned.

### W8: Sample size inconsistent between abstract and methods

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract "Fourteen administrators were interviewed in depth"; §3.2 "Twelve senior administrators (n=12) with direct QA responsibilities"
**Confidence**: 5 — direct textual comparison.

The reader cannot determine the study's N. In a study whose generality claims are already strained, the basic sample count must be stated once and consistently, and the discrepancy raises a question about whether two interviews were dropped and, if so, on what basis — which bears on W10.

### W9: Anonymization guarantee contradicted by identifying descriptors

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.5 "all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis so that no individual could be identified"; §4.1 "the quality director of the largest private university in the region"
**Confidence**: 4 — the identifiability judgement is confident; the ethics-procedure call belongs to the methodology seat.

"The quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" are singular office-holders once the institutional descriptors in §3.2 are combined with them. This directly contradicts §3.5's guarantee. I flag this as more than an editorial matter: participants gave consent under a stated protection that the manuscript as written does not deliver, and the quoted material is critical of their own institutions. The descriptors should be stripped, and the ethics statement should be re-checked against the reported text rather than against intention.

### W10: Dissenting cases excluded while claiming full-range representation

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "these were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis"
**Confidence**: 5 — the contradiction is explicit and one sentence removed from the claim it contradicts.

The same paragraph asserts that the study "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views." Excluding cases because they fall outside the theme structure is the definition of not capturing the full range, and the excluded cases are precisely the diagnostic ones: sceptics of the QA enterprise are the population whose accounts the field uses to identify compliance cultures. Their removal also inflates the ritualism-plus-recovered-meaning arc that carries §5. The dissenting accounts must be reinstated and analysed, or the representation claim withdrawn and the exclusion justified analytically rather than by space.

### W11: "Emerged systematically from the structured protocol" misuses thematic-analysis vocabulary and contradicts §3.1

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.1 "These themes emerged systematically from the structured protocol"
**Confidence**: 4 — familiar with thematic-analysis reporting conventions; the design contradiction is textual.

§3.1 specifies semi-structured interviews and §3.4 describes iterative coding. "Structured protocol" contradicts both, and "emerged systematically from" a protocol is a category error: themes are constructed by analysts from data, and a protocol shapes what is said rather than warranting that patterns are non-artifactual. The sentence is doing warrant-work it cannot do, but it is correctable by rewording without disturbing the findings.

### W12: External QA vocabulary used undifferentiated

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 "periodic self-evaluation, external review, and continuous documentation of academic standards"
**Confidence**: 5 — these distinctions are basic to the comparative QA literature.

Accreditation, audit, and evaluation are distinct external QA instruments with different objects, thresholds, and consequences, and the manuscript uses "external review," "accreditors," "the panel," and "QA regimes" interchangeably throughout §1, §2, and §4. Combined with W6 this leaves the "external requirement" pole of the paper's central tension unspecified. Defining the instrument type at first use would fix the terminology issue itself; the larger interpretive problem sits with W6.
