# Field Analysis Report

## Paper Basic Information

- **Title**: Building Institutional Quality Culture: Administrator Perspectives on Quality Assurance Implementation in Universities
- **Abstract length**: ~145 words
- **Full text length**: ~2,200 words including references (body ~1,850 words) — unusually short for an empirical mixed-methods article; most target journals expect 6,000–8,000
- **Number of references**: 12 (all carrying `10.5555/` DOI prefixes, i.e. placeholder/non-resolving identifiers)
- **Language**: English → conduct review in English

## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Quality in Higher Education** (Taylor & Francis) — Closest topical home. Publishes exactly this object of study (internal QA, quality culture, the QA office as actor) and already appears in the reference list (Iversen, 2021). Its readership will know the EUA Quality Culture lineage and ESG 2015 cold, which makes the missing canon the first thing an editor notices. Realistic fit *after* substantive revision.
2. **Journal of Higher Education Policy and Management** — Strong fit for the administrator-as-intermediary framing (cf. Silva & Tan, 2021, cited here). Values practitioner-relevant governance work and tolerates modest sample sizes, provided the national system and institutional context are disclosed rather than anonymized into abstraction.
3. **Tertiary Education and Management** — The most forgiving realistic outlet for a qualitatively dominant, single-system administrative study. Explicitly management-of-institutions focused; the distributed-leadership theme fits its scope well.

*Not recommended at current state*: **Studies in Higher Education** / **Higher Education** (Q1). The overgeneralization in §5 ("universities across the sector"), the 2,200-word length, and the unverifiable references would trigger desk rejection before peer review.

## Reviewer Configuration Cards

### Reviewer Configuration Card #1

**Role**: EIC
**Display role**: Journal-Fit Reviewer
**Identity Description**: Associate Editor of *Quality in Higher Education*, 18 years in the field; came to editing from a national QA agency, where she coordinated institutional audit panels before moving to a research chair in higher education governance. Has handled roughly 400 submissions on internal QA systems and is the journal's designated handler for publication-ethics queries.
**Review Focus**:
1. **Fit and originality against the existing quality-culture canon.** The manuscript claims in §6 to offer "the first comprehensive account of how university administrators experience and enact QA implementation." Test that claim against the literature this journal has itself published — the EUA Quality Culture Project reports, Harvey & Stensaker's quality-culture typology, Ehlers, Sursock, Vettori, Cardoso et al., Loukkola & Zhang. None appear. Determine whether the contribution is novel, incremental, or already superseded.
2. **Reference integrity and publication ethics.** All 12 DOIs share the `10.5555/` placeholder prefix; the Delacroix entry embeds an editorial "Annotation" that is not APA and does not belong in a reference list. Establish whether these sources resolve at all before any substantive assessment proceeds. This is a gate, not a comment.
3. **Reader payoff and scope calibration.** At ~1,850 body words with three themes, 12 interviews, and one descriptive mean, ask whether this is a full article, a research note, or a conference paper. Also press on the unnamed "single national system" (§3.2): this journal's comparative readership cannot use findings whose regulatory context is withheld.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the paper's headline construct — "quality culture" — is given an operational definition at all, or whether it floats between "shared values" (§1), a survey score (§4.4), and "ongoing negotiation" (§6) without ever being pinned down; and whether the 14-vs-12 participant discrepancy indicates a drafting error or an uncorrected data problem.
**Possible blind spots**: As a big-picture fit reviewer she may accept the survey component at face value rather than interrogating the p<.05 claim; she may also treat the excluded dissenting cases as a reporting choice rather than an analytic-validity failure. Both must be picked up elsewhere.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Identity Description**: Professor of research methodology specializing in mixed-methods design integration; trained in qualitative rigour frameworks (Lincoln & Guba, Tracy's "big-tent" criteria) and secondarily in small-sample inference. Serves as methodological consultant to two education journals and routinely rejects papers whose "mixed methods" label describes two unconnected studies stapled together.
**Review Focus**:
1. **Numerical and procedural integrity of the sample.** Reconcile abstract ("Fourteen administrators") with §3.2 ("Twelve senior administrators (n=12)"). Then account for the survey arithmetic: 48 respondents total, but the only reported comparison uses n=9 and n=11 (§4.4) — 20 cases, with the third institution and the remaining 28 respondents unmentioned. Demand a participant table with per-site n, role, tenure, and response rate.
2. **Qualitative trustworthiness and the exclusion of disconfirming cases.** §3.4 describes coding in three sentences: no coding framework, no coder count, no reliability or consensus procedure, no saturation evidence, no audit trail, no reflexivity statement, no member checking. Worse, §4.3 states that skeptical/dissenting participants "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure." That is negative-case elimination to protect a coding scheme, and it invalidates the immediately preceding claim of "balanced representation… the full range of administrator views." Also flag the §4.1 non sequitur: themes are said to have "emerged systematically from the structured protocol," which contradicts the semi-structured design in §3.1 and does not license the inference that patterns are "not an artifact of any single institution's local circumstances."
3. **Survey instrument and inferential validity.** M=3.9, SD=0.6 is reported with no scale range, no anchors, no item set, no provenance (adapted or novel?), no reliability coefficient, and no dimensionality evidence for a construct treated as unitary. The "statistically significant difference (p<.05)" names no test, reports no statistic, no df, no effect size, no CI, and no correction — on n=9 vs n=11. Judge whether any inferential claim is defensible or whether the wording must be reduced to descriptive contrast.
4. **Mixed-methods integration.** §3.1 assigns the survey a "contextualiz[ing]" role, but there is no stated point of integration, no joint display, and in §4.4 the survey is said to "corroborate" the qualitative picture in one sentence and then to contradict expectations in the next. Ask what the design actually mixes.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the qualitative claims are traceable to evidence — universal quantifiers such as "recurred across every interview" (§4.2) and "most pervasive" (§4.1) appear without any counts, extract tables, or per-site distribution, so a reader cannot distinguish a pattern from three vivid quotations.
**Possible blind spots**: May under-weight whether the three themes are theoretically novel (they map closely onto decoupling and audit-culture literatures) and may not notice the substantive citation reversal in §5, since that is a domain-knowledge catch rather than a design catch.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #3

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Identity Description**: Senior higher education policy scholar working on comparative internal quality assurance; has led two European Standards and Guidelines (ESG) implementation reviews and published on the gap between external accreditation logics and institutional self-evaluation practice across five national systems. Reads QA manuscripts with the field's citation history in her head.
**Review Focus**:
1. **Citation fidelity — the Delacroix reversal.** §2 correctly reports that Delacroix (2018) "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual, and argues *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of a healthy quality culture," and the reference-list annotation confirms this. §5 then states: "our findings align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture. Consultation, on this reading, is the engine of internalization." The paper's own Discussion inverts the source's argument into its opposite and builds a design recommendation on the inversion. This is a substantive misattribution, not a wording slip, and it destabilizes the paper's third contribution claim.
2. **Absent theoretical framework and incomplete canon.** No framework is nominated anywhere; "quality culture" is imported from Brennan & Osei (2020) and Ferreira & Lund (2022) without definition or operationalization. Missing: ESG 2015 (the single most consequential reference frame for internal QA in the anonymized "national system"), Harvey & Green on quality conceptions, Harvey & Stensaker on quality-culture types, the EUA Quality Culture Project, Ehlers, Sursock, Cardoso et al. on academics' perceptions. Assess whether the "under-theorized" gap claim in §1 survives contact with this literature.
3. **Context suppression and policy interpretability.** §3.2 withholds the national system, the QA regime, the review cycle, and whether the three institutions face the same accreditor. Without that, "external QA requirements" (§1) has no referent, cross-national readers cannot judge transferability, and the institutional-type moderator hypothesis (§4.4) cannot be interpreted against known sectoral differences.
4. **Overgeneralization in §5.** "These findings demonstrate that universities across the sector treat QA as a negotiated accomplishment… can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture: administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension." Twelve (or fourteen) administrators in three institutions in one unnamed system cannot license "everywhere," and the verb "demonstrate" cannot be sustained by thematic interview data.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the paper distinguishes *quality assurance* from *quality culture* from *accreditation compliance* with enough precision to make its central argument falsifiable — and whether "administrators" (quality directors, associate deans, registry heads, per §3.2) is a coherent analytic category or three different occupational positions collapsed into one.
**Possible blind spots**: Likely to accept the thematic analysis procedure as conventional and may not scrutinize the survey statistics; may also treat the anonymization contradictions as an editorial matter rather than an ethics matter.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #4

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Identity Description**: Organizational sociologist of accountability regimes — works on institutional decoupling, ceremonial conformity, and reactivity to measurement (Meyer & Rowan, Power's audit society, Espeland & Sauder, Bromley & Powell) — who also chairs her university's ethics committee for organizational and elite-informant fieldwork. She reads this manuscript as a decoupling study that does not know it is one, and as a small-N elite study with a confidentiality problem.
**Review Focus**:
1. **The paper rediscovers institutional decoupling without naming it.** "We produce beautiful evidence portfolios that nobody reads except the panel. It's theater" (§4.1) is textbook ceremonial conformity; the calendar-bound "season of the year given over to feeding the machine" is Power's audit ritual; §4.3's identity work is a documented response to reactivity. Forty years of organization theory already model this. Ask what the manuscript adds beyond a new empirical site — and whether engaging that literature would sharpen the contribution or dissolve it. Conversely, the *interesting* finding (administrators actively recovering meaning from rituals they themselves label theater) is analytically underexploited: it is the mechanism, and it is described in one paragraph.
2. **Participant identifiability versus the anonymization claim.** §3.5 asserts "all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis so that no individual could be identified." §4.1 then quotes "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" — in a design with exactly one private and one public research university (§3.2). Both speakers are singular role-holders and both are effectively named. The attributed quotes are critical of their own institutions ("It's theater, and everyone knows it's theater"). Determine whether consent covered attributable role-level quotation, whether participants reviewed quotes, and what remediation is required. Under most ethics frameworks this alone blocks publication until resolved.
3. **The counter-reading the paper never tests.** If administrators simultaneously call QA theater and describe themselves as custodians of student interests (§4.3), an alternative explanation is occupational self-legitimation by actors whose jobs exist because of the ritual they critique. The manuscript treats vocational framing as a "coping resource" and stops. No academic, student, or accreditor voice appears, so the administrators' self-account is the only account — and it is accepted rather than triangulated.
4. **Practitioner usability.** §5's recommendation is that "processes that maximize genuine participation should be favored" — which, stripped of the misread Delacroix support, reduces to an unactionable exhortation. What would a QA director do differently on Monday?

**Will particularly care about**: The unexamined assumption that administrators are privileged witnesses to quality culture rather than interested parties within it — the study's design guarantees that the actors whose work is being validated are the only ones asked whether it matters.
**Possible blind spots**: May undervalue the manuscript's genuine contribution to the higher education QA literature specifically (where administrator-level accounts *are* thinner than academic-level accounts), and may push the paper toward organization theory further than the target journals' readerships want. The Journal-Fit Reviewer and Reviewer 2 should hold the line on disciplinary home.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

**Paper characteristics requiring particular attention**

1. **Integrity defects outrank argument quality here.** Four issues are of a class that determines the verdict independently of how persuasive the themes are: the 14/12 participant contradiction, the placeholder DOIs across all 12 references, the Delacroix reversal between §2 and §5, and the anonymization claim contradicted by identifying quote attributions. Reviewers should not spend their word budget on prose polish while these stand.
2. **Two self-refuting passages.** §4.3 claims "balanced representation… the full range of administrator views" and then discloses that dissenting participants were excluded. §4.4 says the survey "corroborated the qualitative picture" and then says the interviews "had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture." Both must be named explicitly rather than absorbed as awkward drafting, because in each case the weaker sentence undermines a claim the paper depends on.
3. **The manuscript is short by roughly two-thirds of a target-journal article.** Much of the required revision is addition, not correction: methods detail, participant table, extract tables with counts, instrument appendix, theoretical framework, context disclosure. Reviewers should signal scale of work honestly rather than issuing a list of minor points.
4. **Developmental REGISTER, evidence-based verdict.** Maturity is early-revised-draft, so wording should be constructive and specific about what would fix each item. The recommendation itself must still follow from the criteria — a warm tone does not convert integrity-class defects into minor revisions.

**Complementarity and tension across the panel**

- **Non-overlap is enforced by ownership assignment.** Reference resolvability and the originality/"first comprehensive account" claim → Journal-Fit Reviewer. Sample arithmetic, coding procedure, negative-case exclusion, survey validity, integration → Reviewer 1. Citation fidelity, theoretical framework, canon gaps, context suppression, sector-level overclaim → Reviewer 2. Theory-of-accountability framing, participant identifiability/consent, missing counter-voices, practitioner usability → Reviewer 3.
- **The excluded-dissent problem is split deliberately.** Reviewer 1 treats it as an analytic-validity failure (negative cases eliminated to preserve a three-theme structure); Reviewer 3 treats the related consent-and-attribution question as an ethics-governance failure. Both are legitimate; the synthesizer should merge them into one high-severity finding rather than presenting them as two half-arguments.
- **Anticipated tension to preserve, not resolve.** Reviewer 3 will argue the themes are already explained by decoupling theory and are therefore not novel; Reviewer 2 will argue the administrator-level empirical account is genuinely thin in the higher education QA literature and is the paper's real asset. Both are correct. The synthesizer should render this as a scoping decision for the author — reframe as a contribution to QA studies that engages organization theory, rather than as a general theory claim — instead of averaging the two positions into "engage more literature."
- **Cross-check the reviewers won't do for themselves.** Nobody is assigned the plain reading of §3.2's "single national system" against §5's "sector as a whole" and §6's "wider range of… national systems." The synthesizer should verify that the sampling frame, the generalization claims, and the future-research statement are mutually consistent; at present they are not.
