## Review Body

Let me first construct the strongest version of this paper's argument, because it deserves that before it is attacked. The authors observe a positive association between dashboard session counts and course completion in a real deployment, they pair it with a self-report correlate that the governing theory predicts, they explicitly name their design as cross-sectional, they cite the literature's own critique of causal overreach, and they include a limitations section that concedes the proxy problem, the self-report problem, and the single-course problem. On that reading the paper is a modest, self-aware descriptive contribution. My task is to grant the data and the methods provisionally and see whether the chain from those findings to the paper's stated claims holds. It does not, and the breaks are not marginal.

The dominant failure is a concede-and-proceed pattern: every concession is made in a location where it costs nothing and is then withdrawn where it would cost something. Section 1 promises "We are careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story that might explain it." Section 5 opens by asserting that "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and that "increasing dashboard engagement therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course." Section 6 escalates further to a "dependable strategy" applicable "worldwide" and "across programs and disciplines," while Section 5.1 has just conceded that a single course and a single interface limit what can be said. The manuscript is not hedged and then overstated by accident; it is hedged in the paragraphs a reviewer scans for candour and unhedged in the abstract, the discussion's topic sentence, and the conclusion, which are the sentences that will be cited. Section 2 makes this worse by citing Ibarra (2023), "Causal language in correlational learning analytics: A critical audit," and then committing precisely the audited error four pages later. That is not an oversight a reader can charitably absorb; it is the paper naming its own defect and proceeding anyway.

Grant the causal language away for a moment and ask whether even the correlational claim survives. I do not think it does in its current form, for two structural reasons the manuscript never engages. First, the predictor is partly a function of the outcome: dashboard sessions accumulate across the fifteen-week term, and a student coded as not retained by definition stopped accumulating them earlier. A student who withdraws in week five cannot register week-ten sessions. Some unknown fraction of r = .24 is therefore arithmetic rather than behavioural, and nothing in Section 3.4 normalises exposure or restricts the count to a common pre-withdrawal window. Second, the sample is conditioned on the outcome. Recruitment happened "midway through the term" via a voluntary LMS announcement; students who had already withdrawn could not answer it, and the log data were pulled only "for the same set of respondents." The design therefore systematically under-samples the non-retained cases on which the entire retention claim depends. Compounding this, Section 3.2 opens by describing "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" and then describes, three sentences later, a self-selected volunteer sample with non-respondents excluded. Those two sentences cannot both be true, and the paper never chooses. Alternative explanations for the association — reverse direction (engaged, persisting students open dashboards more), a third-variable conscientiousness account, and the exposure artifact above — are not named and rejected; they are simply absent.

The second pillar, perceived control, cannot bear the weight placed on it either. It is a single item, measured once, cross-sectionally, after the behaviour it is meant to explain, and Section 5 nonetheless describes it as "a mediating construct." No mediation model is estimated, no temporal ordering exists, and no coefficient is reported. Worse, the paper's own literature review quotes Vandermeer (2023) to the effect that click counts are "rough indicators rather than as faithful measures of the cognitive engagement the theory implicates," and then builds an explicitly cognitive claim — dashboards "scaffold monitoring and adjustment" — on exactly those click counts. A limitation acknowledged in Section 5.1 and then relied upon in Section 5.0 is not a limitation; it is a disclaimer.

Then there is the arithmetic, which independently prevents a reader from auditing anything. The abstract reports r = .42; Section 4.2 reports r = .24. The perceived-control t-test is reported as t(156) when only 87 respondents answered the item and the entire analytic sample is 142, so the stated degrees of freedom describe a sample that does not exist anywhere in the manuscript. The exam comparison is reported as t(140) = 1.31 with p = .008, a pairing that is numerically impossible, and is then narrated as if it were null. Table 2 sums to 127 students in a comparison the text says used all 142. Each of these is individually correctable, but collectively they mean that no inferential number in Section 4 can currently be reconciled with any other. The secondary clarity item compounds the concern: N = 10, M = 3.00, SD = 0.10 on a 1-5 integer scale is arithmetically unobtainable, since integer responses averaging exactly 3.00 across ten students yield either SD = 0 or SD of roughly 0.47 or more.

Several smaller items I am deliberately not tabling, because none is singly decision-relevant, but they accumulate against the manuscript's care: the median split is applied to a distribution the authors themselves describe as right-skewed, which loads near-median students into the "high engagement" group and attenuates whatever contrast the comparison was meant to show; perceived control is reported as 3.847 against an SD of 0.62, a precision the measurement cannot support; Section 3.4 says Pearson correlations were used "between continuous measures" while retention is explicitly dichotomous, so the reported r is a point-biserial coefficient under a mislabel; the reference list contains nine entries that are never cited in the text; and the claim that a required course gives "the sample some disciplinary breadth" is an unsupported bridging premise doing quiet work for the generalisation in Section 6. Separately and outside my scoring remit, Section 2's characterisation of Ferro and Nakamura (2021) appears to invert the cited source, and Section 3.2's statement that students were not informed their behavioural data would be analysed, with no ethics-approval statement anywhere in the manuscript, is a matter an editor must resolve before any scientific question is reached.

I score D3 as a block rather than a fatal block, and I want to be explicit about why, since the finding list is long. A coherent claim does remain identifiable for repair: that in one introductory statistics course, among students who survived to mid-term and volunteered, dashboard session count showed a small positive association with course completion, direction and mechanism unresolved. That claim is not contradicted by the reported findings and could be defended after exposure-time correction, a full-cohort re-analysis, reconciliation of every reported statistic, and wholesale removal of causal and generalisation language from the abstract, discussion, and conclusion. What cannot be repaired by rewording alone is the sampling frame, which will require the logs for the full section rather than for survey respondents. The paper as written argues for something its own results do not license, and it does so after telling the reader it would not.
