## Review Body

I read this manuscript as the reader it explicitly addresses in §6: not a learning-analytics specialist, but someone in an institutional research or data-governance office who would be handed this paper as evidence for or against funding a student-facing dashboard. That is the adjacent-field audience the paper chose, and it is the audience against which cross-disciplinary relevance has to be judged. Judged that way, the paper fails, and it fails in the specific way my dimension is designed to catch: the accessibility is largely adequate, but the export is not earned. The manuscript's local descriptions are legible to an outsider while its headline implications travel to populations, settings, and decision contexts the design cannot reach.

Start with what works, because it is real and easy to under-credit. §1 and §2 are unusually candid for this genre. The introduction names the causal gap in the field's own literature and promises to keep the pattern separate from the story that might explain it. §2 then supplies two standards the paper could have been held to: that relative-standing feedback can demotivate, and that click-based engagement proxies are rough indicators rather than measures of cognitive engagement. §3.1 and §3.3 are written so an outsider can restate them: the sessionization rule is stated, retention is dichotomized with its coding rule given, and the cross-sectional limit is stated in plain terms. An adjacent-field reader can follow this study. That is the accessibility half of my dimension, and it is broadly satisfied.

The substantiation half is where the manuscript collapses. §5 converts a correlation into a causal statement ("increasing dashboard engagement therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course"), and §6 then converts that causal statement into a cross-institutional procurement recommendation for institutions "worldwide," described as "a dependable strategy" and "a practical and generalizable lever... at scale." The empirical basis is r = .24 in one introductory statistics course, one term, one interface, in a volunteer sample recruited mid-term. Nothing in the design licenses transport across programs, disciplines, institutions, or dashboard designs, and §5.1's limitations list, which does note single-course scope and interface specificity, is silent by the time §6 speaks. For my reader that is not a stylistic overreach; it is the operative sentence they would quote in a funding memo. The most obvious rival explanation is never engaged: conscientious students both open dashboards and finish courses, and the paper's own measure (session counts) is precisely the kind of variable that behaves as a proxy for pre-existing diligence. The paper's §2 anticipates this critique by citing Ibarra (2023) on causal language outrunning evidence, and then commits the error it cited.

Two further problems make the recommendation unusable rather than merely overstated. First, the decision parameters an institution needs are entirely absent: no base rate of retention in the course, no distribution of withdrawal timing, no dose-response (is one session enough, or fifteen?), no cost figure, and no comparison against cheaper or better-evidenced retention supports. A recommendation to spend money that supplies none of these cannot be acted on responsibly, and if acted on anyway it displaces supports with stronger evidence. Second, the internal numbers do not agree: the abstract reports r = .42, §4.2 reports r = .24. An adjacent-field reader who reads only the abstract, which is most of them, would walk away with an effect nearly double the one the results support.

Then there is the governance layer, which the manuscript treats as absent rather than as unresolved. §3.2 states that students were not informed their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study, and consent was obtained only for the survey. There is no ethics approval statement, no data-protection basis for the secondary use of trace data, no anonymization or pseudonymization description, and no retention or data-availability statement anywhere in the paper. §5.1 does not mention any of this. In most institutional and statutory frameworks this is a defect in the conduct of the research, not a limitation of it, and it attaches specifically to the log-based analyses that carry the headline result. The minimum remedy is documented approval plus a stated legal basis covering secondary use of dashboard logs; failing that, retrospective consent; failing that, withdrawal of the log-based analyses, which would leave very little of the paper. I record this here because it is decision-relevant to any editor, even though the mandatory dimension it bears on is not mine to score.

Related, and closer to my own dimension: §3.1 discloses that the dashboard showed a peer-comparison band to every enrolled student with no opt-in. That makes the deployment an intervention with a harm profile the paper's own §2 describes. The paper never asks whether anyone was harmed. There is no subgroup analysis by prior achievement, no examination of whether non-retained students were disproportionately exposed to unfavorable comparisons, and no goal-orientation moderator despite Osei (2020) and Berange (2021) sitting in the reference list. Worse, the students most likely to have been harmed are the ones who withdrew before the mid-term recruitment window and are therefore structurally absent from the sample. A study whose sampling frame is defined at mid-term cannot observe the earliest attrition, yet retention is its outcome variable. The population that would falsify the paper's claim is the population the paper cannot see. For an outside reader, this is the single most important boundary condition in the study, and it appears nowhere.

I want to be explicit about what a corrected version could look like, because the governance critique should not swallow the question of publishability. A version that (a) documents its ethical and legal basis or drops the log analyses, (b) states its sampling frame honestly as "students still enrolled and volunteering at mid-term," (c) reports withdrawal timing and the course base rate, (d) reports whatever subgroup or exposure analyses the data permit and states plainly which harm questions the design cannot answer, and (e) replaces §6 with a scoped claim about one course and one interface, would be a modest but honest contribution. The current version is not that paper, and the distance is not editorial polish.

### S1: §2 supplies the demotivation and measurement-proxy standards the field needs

The literature review does not sanitize the evidence base. It states the demotivation risk, ties it to goal orientation, and warns that click-based metrics are rough indicators. This gives an outside reader a usable map of the field's live disagreements and, incidentally, the standard by which this paper should be judged.

- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "click-based engagement metrics should be treated as rough indicators"
- **Confidence**: 4 — routine reading of dashboard-evaluation literature in deployment reviews.

### S2: Design scope is stated in terms an adjacent-field reader can restate

§3.1 states the cross-sectional limit and its consequence directly rather than burying it, and §1 explicitly promises to keep pattern separate from cause. The promise is not kept later, but the framing itself is accessible and honest about design class.

- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.1 "Because the design is cross-sectional, all measures reflect a single term and no student was observed across multiple courses or terms."
- **Confidence**: 4 — regular appraisal of observational designs in institutional research.

### S3: Core measures are operationally defined at outsider-usable granularity

Dashboard engagement, retention, and perceived control each come with a stated operationalization, including the sessionization threshold and the treatment of enrolled-but-absent students. An adjacent-field reader can reconstruct what was counted without insider vocabulary.

- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "A session was defined as a dashboard view preceded by at least thirty minutes of inactivity"
- **Confidence**: 4 — trace-data operationalization is central to my compliance work.

### W1: Log-based analyses rest on undisclosed secondary use of behavioral data

§3.2 discloses that students consented only to the survey and were never informed that their dashboard activity would be analyzed for research. The engagement variable, the retention association, and both group comparisons all derive from that unconsented reuse. This is a defect in research conduct, not a scope limitation, and it is disclosed by the authors themselves rather than inferred.

- **Severity**: Critical
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 "Students were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study."
- **Confidence**: 5 — FERPA/GDPR review of trace-data secondary use is my standing responsibility.

### W2: No ethics approval, legal basis, anonymization, retention, or data-availability statement anywhere

Independently of W1, the manuscript contains none of the documentation an editor or an institutional reader needs to establish that the study was reviewable: no IRB or REC statement, no stated data-protection basis, no description of how logs were de-identified, no retention plan, no data-availability statement. If approval exists, this is a reporting repair; the point is that as submitted, nothing here can be verified.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: absence: whole manuscript — expected an IRB or REC approval statement, a data-protection legal basis for secondary use of dashboard logs, an anonymization or pseudonymization description, and retention plus data-availability statements; checked Abstract, §3.1, §3.2, §3.3, §3.4, §4, §5.1, §6, and the reference list for any separate ethics or data-availability section
- **Confidence**: 5 — institutional ethics documentation review.

### W3: §5.1 limitations omit both the consent question and the harm question

The limitations section covers only measurement narrowness, self-report bias, and single-course scope. An adjacent-field reader uses that list to bound the claim, and this one omits the two issues that most bound it. Fixing the prose does not fix the underlying defects, but leaving the list as-is misrepresents the paper's own uncertainty.

- **Severity**: Minor
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §5.1 "Several limitations qualify these findings. First, dashboard engagement was operationalized narrowly as session counts"
- **Confidence**: 4 — limitations-scoping review across analytics deployment appraisals.

### W4: The intervention's plausible harm was never measured despite the paper's own literature

§3.1 discloses a peer-comparison band shown to all enrolled students with no opt-in. §2 then documents that relative-standing feedback can discourage struggling and performance-avoidance-oriented learners. No subgroup analysis by prior achievement, no goal-orientation moderator, and no exposure analysis for non-retained students appears in §4. The paper asks only whether the intervention helped the retained, which cannot detect the mechanism it cited.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "Being shown one's position relative to peers can discourage struggling students rather than mobilize them"
- **Confidence**: 4 — algorithmic accountability review of student-facing analytics deployments.

### W5: Mid-term volunteer recruitment makes the at-risk population structurally invisible while retention is the outcome

The sampling frame is students who responded to a mid-term announcement and consented. By construction it excludes everyone who withdrew earlier in the term, which is exactly the group whose absence or presence determines whether the retention association means anything. Combined with volunteer self-selection, the result is a sample defined partly by the outcome being predicted. The claim can survive re-scoping to "students still enrolled and volunteering at mid-term," but the paper never states this frame, and no withdrawal-timing data are reported that would let a reader gauge how much attrition falls outside it.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 "Midway through the term, an announcement was posted to the course LMS inviting students to complete a short survey" and "Students who chose to respond, and who consented to the survey, formed the study sample"
- **Confidence**: 4 — sampling-frame reasoning in institutional research; I am not recomputing the reported statistics.

### W6: The causal claim in §5 and the worldwide procurement recommendation in §6 exceed anything the design licenses

§5 asserts that dashboard engagement improved retention and that increasing engagement raises completion probability. §6 then addresses institutions worldwide and calls dashboard investment a dependable strategy across programs and disciplines. The design is a single-course, single-term, cross-sectional correlation of r = .24 on a mid-term volunteer sample, with an untested and obvious confound (conscientious students both open dashboards and finish courses). For the adjacent-field reader this is the paper's operative output, and it is systematically misleading about what the result supports.

- **Severity**: Critical
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 and §6 "increasing dashboard engagement therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course" and "is a dependable strategy for improving retention across programs and disciplines"
- **Confidence**: 4 — deployment-decision appraisal; formal causal-inference adjudication belongs to the methodology seat.

### W7: None of the parameters an institution needs to act on the recommendation are reported

A funding decision requires a base rate, withdrawal timing, dose-response, cost, and a comparison against alternatives. None appears. Without them, the recommendation cannot be operationalized even by a reader who accepts the association at face value, and it risks displacing better-evidenced retention supports.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: absence: §6 institutional recommendation — expected a course retention base rate, a withdrawal timing distribution, dose-response information across session counts, a deployment cost figure, and comparison against alternative retention supports; checked §4.1, §4.2, §4.3, Table 1, Table 2, and §5
- **Confidence**: 5 — I have advised three campuses on whether to fund dashboard rollouts.

### W8: The abstract's effect size contradicts the results section

The abstract reports r = .42; §4.2 reports r = .24. One of these is wrong, and a reader cannot tell which. Since the abstract is what most adjacent-field readers and most citing authors will use, the circulated version of this finding is nearly double the reported one.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract and §4.2 "Dashboard engagement correlated positively with retention (r = .42)" and "Dashboard engagement was positively associated with course retention (r = .24, p = .004)"
- **Confidence**: 3 — direct text comparison only; I am not recomputing the statistics.

### W9: A cited source appears to be inverted, and the equity rationale rests on that inversion

§2 states that dashboards reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students, attributed to Ferro & Nakamura (2021), and says this position underpins the equity rationale for institutional deployment. The reference list gives that work's title as being about dashboards demotivating the lower-achieving student. If the citation is inverted, the paper's equity-oriented justification for deployment is built on a source that argues the opposite, which matters directly to the institutional audience §6 addresses.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 and References "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students" and "When dashboards demotivate"
- **Confidence**: 3 — citation-content check against the manuscript's own reference list, not a domain adjudication of the cited literature.
