### D5: writing_and_structure
score: block
trigger: "Organisation or presentation is broken enough to obstruct assessment"

The manuscript's presentation defeats assessment at three separate points rather than merely inconveniencing a reader. The reference list carries a reserved test DOI prefix on all fifteen entries, so the literature positioning on which every contribution claim rests cannot be checked at all. The analytic sample cannot be recovered from the text: §3.2 states 142, §4.1 reports 87 respondents to the perceived-control item, §4.3 reports a test statistic with 156 degrees of freedom for that same comparison and asserts that all 142 students were classified for the exam comparison, while Table 2 sums to 127. The abstract states an effect size that the Results section does not report. §3.2 describes the sampling frame in two mutually exclusive ways within four sentences. Additionally, no ethics or consent statement appears anywhere, which is a required element under this venue's submission conventions for trace-data work. These are not cosmetic; each blocks a specific act of review. D5 is normal priority and carries no fatal escalation, so this score reflects revision burden rather than a rejection condition of its own.

### D6: venue_fit_and_contribution
score: block
block_class: fatal
trigger: "offers no discernible increment over existing published work"

Topic fit is not in question: student-facing dashboards, trace data, and downstream persistence sit squarely in this venue's remit, and the framing shows genuine familiarity with the track's debates. Contribution is the failure. The field already holds multiple systematic reviews establishing that dashboard evidence on downstream outcomes is thin, heterogeneous, and predominantly correlational — a conclusion the manuscript's own §2 restates. What this study adds is one modest association (r = .24) from one course, one term, one self-selected sample of volunteers, using session counts as the engagement proxy and course completion as the outcome. The engagement measure accumulates over the same term whose completion it predicts, so students who withdrew early had less exposure window in which to generate sessions; the reported association is therefore not separable from time-in-course by the design as described. Nothing else in the manuscript is offered as a distinct increment: §1's contribution sentence names only the setting and the combination of logs with self-report, and §5 positions the finding against "the view that externalized progress cues can support persistence" without identifying any prior claim it revises, extends, or overturns. After the abstract's r = .42 is corrected to the reported .24, after the causal language of §5 and §6 is withdrawn, and after the exposure-window confound is acknowledged, no statement remains that a reader of the existing reviews could not already have made. I bind this to the fatal trigger rather than to the block trigger because the deficit is not overselling a real increment — it is the absence of a locatable one, and no revision of the text alters what these data can show.

## Review Body

I read this manuscript as the handling editor for the dashboard and feedback-intervention track, with two questions in front of me: what a reader could claim after reading it that they could not claim before, and whether the public-facing surfaces — title, abstract, conclusion — say something the study can support. Those are the surfaces that get cited and press-released, and they are where this submission is furthest from its own data.

The manuscript is at its best in §2 and §3.3. The literature review anticipates the field's real self-criticisms: that click counts are not cognitive engagement, that peer comparison can demotivate, that causal language in this literature outruns its designs. §3.3 states its operational rules plainly, including a sessionization threshold and a candid note that the median split sacrifices efficiency for interpretability. An author who writes those sentences knows what a careful dashboard study looks like.

That is what makes §5 and §6 hard to reconcile. Between §2, which cites a critical audit of causal language in correlational learning analytics, and §5, which opens by asserting that dashboard engagement "improved" retention and "raises the probability" of completion, the manuscript abandons the standard it set for itself. §6 goes further, telling "higher education institutions worldwide" that dashboards are "a dependable strategy." Nothing in a single-section, single-term, self-selected sample of 142 licenses either the causal verb or the global scope. The abstract compounds this by reporting r = .42 where §4.2 reports r = .24. I cannot tell whether that is a transcription error or a different analysis; either way, the number most likely to be quoted from this paper is not in the paper.

On my publishability gates: I treat the absence of any ethics or IRB statement, combined with §3.2's explicit disclosure that students were not informed their dashboard activity would be analyzed, as a bar prior to any question of scientific merit. Survey consent does not extend to secondary analysis of behavioural traces, and approval cannot be obtained retroactively for an analysis already conducted. The reference list is a second such bar: every entry uses the reserved 10.5555 test prefix and no cited venue is independently verifiable, so I cannot check a single characterisation of prior work — including the one characterisation I can partly test internally, where §2 attributes a reliable benefit for lower-achieving students to a source whose own title reports demotivation.

Two boundary notes. I am not the seat that adjudicates the internal statistics: where I flag t(156) against 87 respondents, Table 2's 127 against a stated 142, or a p = .008 described as not reaching significance, I am flagging that the prose and the exhibits contradict each other on their face, and I defer the validity question to the methodology reviewer. Likewise the substantive misattribution of prior findings is the domain reviewer's dimension to score, not mine; I record it because it bears on whether a calibrated revision could be trusted to hold. My scores stand on D5 and D6 only; the panel synthesizer owns the decision.

### S1: §2 accurately anticipates the field's own measurement critique
The review does not oversell click-based proxies. It states the conflation problem directly — a student who opens the dashboard once and studies it is scored below one who opens it repeatedly without reflection — and cites the critique rather than burying it in limitations. For a track saturated with uncritical adoption papers, this is the section a reader should keep.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "click-based engagement metrics should be treated as rough indicators rather than as faithful measures of the cognitive engagement the theory implicates"

### S2: Operational definitions are explicit and self-critical
§3.3 gives the sessionization rule, the dichotomous retention coding, the response scale, and an unprompted acknowledgement that the median split is a coarse simplification. This is better reporting discipline than most single-site dashboard submissions I handle, and it is what makes the confounds diagnosable at all.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "This median split is a coarse simplification of a continuous measure and was adopted for interpretability rather than statistical efficiency"

### S3: Conventional structure with both exhibits captioned and cited in text
The manuscript follows expected article organisation, and both tables are numbered, captioned, and referenced from the prose that reports them. Nothing has to be hunted for.

**Evidence Anchor**: table: Table 1 and Table 2, both captioned and invoked in the §4.1 and §4.3 prose that reports them

### W3: No locatable increment over the existing systematic-review consensus
The stated contribution names a setting and a data combination, not a claim. The systematic-review literature the manuscript itself invokes already establishes that dashboard-outcome evidence is thin, heterogeneous, and correlational. Once the abstract's inflated coefficient is corrected and the causal verbs of §5 and §6 are withdrawn, what remains is one r = .24 from one course whose engagement measure accrues over the same interval whose completion it predicts, so withdrawal truncates exposure and the association is not separable from time-in-course. No prior claim is revised, extended, or contradicted anywhere in §5. This is not an oversold real increment; it is the absence of one, and text revision cannot supply it.

**Severity**: Critical

**Confidence**: 5 — editorial baseline for this submission class is precisely the judgement being made here

### W4: Abstract reports r = .42 where Results report r = .24
The abstract's headline coefficient is nearly double the one §4.2 reports, and the abstract then converts it into "a promising lever for improving retention." The abstract is the surface that circulates independently of the article, so as submitted the record would carry a coefficient that appears nowhere in the analysis. Correction requires rewriting both the number and the inference built on it; which of the two values is the analysis of record must also be stated.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract "Dashboard engagement correlated positively with retention (r = .42)", §4.2 "Dashboard engagement was positively associated with course retention (r = .24, p = .004)"
**Confidence**: 5 — simple cross-surface comparison, no recomputation involved

### W5: §5 and §6 assert causation and global scope, abandoning the standard set in §1 and §2
§1 promises to distinguish the pattern from the causal story and §2 cites an audit of exactly this failure mode. §5 then states that engagement "improved" retention and that increasing it "raises the probability" of completion, and §6 recommends dashboard investment to institutions worldwide as "a dependable strategy." A cross-sectional design with a volunteer sample supports neither the verb nor the scope. Repair requires rewriting the Discussion and Conclusion in associational terms and bounding the recommendation to the single course studied; the modest association itself survives that rewrite.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "dashboard engagement improved course retention", §6 "is a dependable strategy for improving retention across programs and disciplines"
**Confidence**: 5 — the manuscript states both its own standard and its violation explicitly

### W6: "Retention" shifts from course completion in Methods to programme-level persistence in the Conclusion
§3.3 operationalises retention as remaining enrolled and sitting the final assessment in one course. The title, and more consequentially §6, use the same word for institutional persistence "across programs and disciplines" and "at scale." These are different constructs with different literatures and different base rates; a student who completes this statistics course may still leave the institution. The manuscript never bridges them, so the recommendation addresses an outcome that was never measured.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 "was coded dichotomously as whether the student remained enrolled and completed the final assessment", §6 "practical and generalizable lever for supporting student success at scale"
**Confidence**: 4 — construct comparison is clear from the text, though I am not the seat that adjudicates the retention literature's definitional boundaries

### W7: §3.2 describes two mutually exclusive sampling procedures
The section opens by stating that participants were drawn using a random sample of enrolled students, then describes an LMS announcement to which students elected to respond, with non-respondents excluded. These cannot both be true, and the difference is decisive for how any association should be read: one implies a probability sample of the course, the other a volunteer sample plausibly enriched for conscientious students who both answer surveys and open dashboards. A reader cannot currently tell which study was run.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section", "Students who chose to respond, and who consented to the survey, formed the study sample"
