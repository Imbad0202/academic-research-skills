## Review Body

I read this manuscript from the seat that has to act on findings like it: LMS renewal committees, onboarding programme design, and the annual argument about where digital-education money goes. My assigned dimension is whether the framing, definitions, and stated implications travel to readers outside the paper's own specialty, and whether claims addressed to those outside audiences are substantiated by what the study actually collected.

The headline judgement first, because my instinct as a practitioner and my obligation as a D4 reviewer point in different directions and only one of them governs. My instinct is that r = .42 between two self-reports supports no resourcing decision whatsoever, and that a Discussion which reaches for "institutional attention" on that basis has overreached. But the manuscript does not assert institutional portability; it flags the practice inference as conjecture in the same sentence, restricts itself to correlational language throughout, and names single-site non-generalisability as its first limitation. My Phase 1 block condition required an *asserted* implication the design cannot bear, and that condition does not fire here. A well-bounded descriptive estimate is a legitimate scholarly object, and demanding immediate actionability of it would be my own occupational bias rather than a review finding. So this is a warn, not a block.

What the warn is actually about is thinness of substantiation at two joints. First, the paper addresses an implication to an audience — institutional decision-makers — without giving that audience anything it can use. "Onboarding that demonstrates concrete usefulness may warrant institutional attention" is not a claim a director can evaluate: it does not say what an institution would do differently on Monday, what the intervention consists of, or what observation would count as evidence that it worked. I have run onboarding redesigns of exactly this kind; the binding constraints are staff time, timetable slots, and instructor cooperation, none of which the paper's evidence speaks to. As written, the implication functions as a section requirement rather than a proposition, and the fix is not a hedge — the hedge is already there — but content.

Second, and more consequential for anyone outside the immediate literature, the site is characterised as "one mid-sized public university" and nothing further. The paper's own generalisation limitation is framed in terms of size, sector, and student profile, yet it reports no value for any of those attributes for its own site, no national system, no platform, and — most importantly for this construct — no statement of whether LMS use is effectively compulsory there. Whether assessment submission, grade release, and attendance run through the platform is the dominant determinant of use frequency on every campus I have sat in procurement meetings for. Where LMS traffic is mandatory, perceived usefulness has little room to move reported frequency; where the LMS is an optional convenience, it has a great deal. Section 4 half-acknowledges this by attributing residual variance to "course requirements and assessment schedules," but the manuscript never tells us what those requirements were at the study site. An adjacent-field reader therefore cannot place this estimate in their own context, which is precisely the interpretive work the limitation section claims to enable. I want to be explicit that the attributes I am asking for — an enrolment band, a national system, whether submission and grades run through the platform — are non-identifying and fully compatible with the site anonymity the paper properly maintains.

Two smaller legibility issues sit alongside these. The instrument is named but never shown, so an outside reader cannot determine what "perceived usefulness" was operationally asked; and the recommendation for behavioural log data in the Conclusion is offered without acknowledging that the study's own anonymity design foreclosed that linkage, which leaves the outside reader unable to tell whether logs were unavailable or simply not pursued.

Against that, the manuscript's epistemic discipline is genuinely above the norm for this literature, and I want that recorded as clearly as the deficits. Its scope statements are unusually honest, its statistical reporting is complete enough for an outsider to locate the estimate in a distribution, and it imports the cautions it cites into its own interpretation rather than citing them decoratively.

### S1: Reverse causation is conceded explicitly, not buried in a hedge
The Discussion names the alternative pathway and states that it is equally consistent with the data. For a reader from an adjacent field who cannot supply the design caveats from tacit knowledge, this is the difference between a portable finding and a misread one.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "the reverse pathway, in which more frequent use raises perceived usefulness, is equally consistent with the data"
**Confidence**: 5 — routine reading of causal-language conventions in educational-technology reporting.

### S2: Sensitivity and precision reported well enough to be reused
A prospective power statement plus a confidence interval, an n, and a non-parametric robustness check let an outside reader judge what the study could and could not have detected. This is what makes an incremental single-site estimate usable in someone else's synthesis rather than merely quotable.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.4 "the study had greater than .80 power to detect a correlation of r >= .19 at alpha = .05"
**Confidence**: 4 — comfortable with correlational reporting standards; not the statistical seat on this panel.

### S3: The central construct is glossed in plain language before it is used
"Perceived usefulness" is defined in ordinary terms at first substantive use rather than treated as self-evident shorthand for readers who already know the acceptance literature. An HCI, information-systems, or higher-education-policy reader can proceed without a detour into the source tradition.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "the degree to which a person believes a technology will help them perform better"
**Confidence**: 5 — direct textual check of first-use definition.

### S4: Cited cautions are absorbed into the paper's own measurement claim
The self-report-versus-logs literature is not merely cited; it changes what the paper says it measured, which is then carried consistently into the Results and Limitations as "perceived" rather than actual engagement. Likewise the multi-campus variability finding is used to frame this estimate as one point in a distribution.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "self-reported estimates of technology use diverge, sometimes substantially, from behavioral log data"
**Confidence**: 5 — traced the caution forward through §3.2, §4, and §6.

### W1: The practice implication names an audience but gives it nothing decidable
The Discussion directs an inference at institutions, but specifies no intervention, no comparison against current onboarding practice, and no criterion that would count as evidence of success. From the chair that would have to act, "onboarding demonstrating concrete usefulness may warrant attention" is not evaluable: it is compatible with a fifteen-minute orientation slide and with a semester-long embedded programme, whose costs differ by two orders of magnitude. The hedge that this is "suggested by, not proven by" the correlation protects the paper's truth-claim but does not discharge D4's substantiation requirement for a claim addressed beyond the specialty. Repair does not mean strengthening the claim — it means either stating what the recommendation concretely proposes and what observation would test it, or dropping the outward-facing framing and letting the estimate stand as the descriptive contribution the rest of the paper says it is.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "onboarding which helps students see concrete usefulness" and "may be worth institutional attention"
**Confidence**: 5 — I have designed and evaluated the onboarding interventions this sentence gestures at, and sit on the committees that would fund them.

### W2: No non-identifying institutional context, so the transferability limitation cannot be exercised
The manuscript asks readers to treat generalisation to institutions of different size, sector, and student profile as an open question, but reports no value for size beyond "mid-sized," no national system, no platform, and no statement of whether LMS use is required for assessment submission or grade release at the study site. Mandate status is not a peripheral covariate for this construct: it directly constrains how much variance in use frequency perception could possibly explain, and it is the first thing a reader on another campus needs in order to decide whether r = .42 is a plausible prior for them. The Results paragraph acknowledges "course requirements and assessment schedules" as unmeasured influences without ever reporting what they were locally, which converts the limitation into a gesture rather than a usable boundary. Because these attributes are reportable in banded, non-identifying form, the anonymity commitment does not explain their absence.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.1 and §6 — expected reportable site attributes (enrolment band, national system, LMS platform, and whether LMS use is required for assessment submission or grade posting); checked Abstract, §3.1, §3.3, §4, §6
**Confidence**: 5 — this is the exact information I use to judge whether an external LMS finding applies to my own institution.

### W3: Instrument content withheld, and the response scale is only partly labelled
The perceived-usefulness measure is identified by citation, item count, and reliability, but no item is reproduced and the nature of the adaptation is never stated, so an outside reader cannot determine what respondents were actually asked. The use item's endpoints are given while its intermediate labels are not, which leaves the reported median category unlocatable on the scale as described. Neither defect touches the estimate, but both force a reader from another field out of the paper to interpret its own measures.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 and §4 "a six-item scale adapted from Costa and Wren (2019)" and "a few times per week"
**Confidence**: 4 — standard instrument-transparency expectation; the source instrument may well be openly available.

### W4: The log-data recommendation is not reconciled with the study's own anonymity design
The Conclusion recommends behavioural log data as the way forward. Every institution running an LMS already holds those logs, and consent-based opt-in linkage is ordinary practice. The manuscript never states whether that route was considered and rejected or simply not taken, so a reader cannot tell what constraint a future study would need to relax — the same anonymity commitment, an ethics-committee position, or nothing but design default. Surfacing the trade-off in a sentence would make the recommendation legible as a decision rather than a gesture at an obvious frontier.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.3 and §7 — expected an explicit statement of whether consent-based opt-in linkage to institutional LMS logs was considered and why it was not pursued; checked §3.3, §3.4, §6, §7
**Confidence**: 4 — familiar with routine log-linkage consent practice; not privy to this committee's constraints.

### W5: Recruitment channel named in field-internal shorthand
"The institution's course-announcement channel" is not a term an outside reader can resolve. Whether that channel sits inside the LMS, in email, or in a separate portal changes what the sample is, and the manuscript's voluntary-response caveat does not disambiguate it. One clause of description would close this.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.1 "distributed through the institution's course-announcement channel"
**Confidence**: 4 — clear definitional gap; the sampling consequences are the methodology seat's to weigh.
