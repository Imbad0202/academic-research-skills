The manuscript's central inferential step is invalid as written. An observational, cross-sectional, mid-term-recruited volunteer sample supports at most an associational statement about one course; §5 and §6 instead assert that engagement "improved" retention, "raises the probability" of completion, and constitutes a "dependable" and "generalizable" lever for institutions "worldwide." No rival explanation for the association is excluded, the primary effect size is reported at two mutually inconsistent values in the abstract and the results, and two of the three inferential statistics cannot be reconciled with the described sample. The argument as stated therefore fails; a narrower, honestly-scoped correlational paper is recoverable, so this is repairable rather than fatal.

## Review Body

Restated as a chain, the argument runs: (P1) dashboard sessions correlate positively with retention in this course; (P2) high-session students report higher perceived control; (P3) self-regulated learning theory predicts that visibility supports monitoring and adjustment; therefore (C) increasing dashboard engagement raises retention, and institutions should invest in dashboards as a dependable, generalisable retention strategy. The step from P1–P3 to C is where the manuscript breaks. P1 is an association measured on a sample constituted after attrition had begun; P2 is a between-group difference on a single self-report item whose reported test statistic cannot have come from the stated data; P3 is a theoretical expectation, not evidence about this deployment. Even granting all three premises at face value, C requires two additional unstated premises the paper never defends: that dashboard engagement is exogenous to persistence intent, and that a single introductory statistics course with one dashboard design licenses claims about programs, disciplines, and institutions worldwide. Both unstated premises are contradicted by the manuscript's own §3.1 and §5.1.

The manuscript is unusually self-undermining. The Introduction promises that the authors "are careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story that might explain it," and §2 approvingly cites an audit of causal language in correlational learning analytics. The Discussion then opens by declaring that engagement "improved" retention and closes the Conclusion with a procurement recommendation. This is not a hedging inconsistency at the margins; the honest framing appears in the sections that make no claims, and the causal framing appears in every section that does. A reader who trusts the abstract and conclusion is being given a different paper from the one the methods describe.

Rival explanations are not merely under-addressed; they are structurally invisible because of how the sample was built. Recruitment occurred "midway through the term," so students who had already withdrawn could not volunteer, which mechanically inflates any engagement–retention association among survivors. Reverse direction is at least as plausible as the stated causal arrow: students who intend to finish keep opening the LMS, and the dashboard sits on the course home page. A third-variable account is equally available, since dashboard sessions are an unadjusted proxy for general course activity and conscientiousness, and no covariate adjustment of any kind is reported. §3.2 also contradicts itself within four sentences, describing both "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" and a sample formed by "students who chose to respond." These cannot both be true, and the difference is decisive for every inference the paper draws.

The arithmetic does not reconcile, and this matters for coherence rather than only for statistics. The perceived-control comparison reports df = 156 when the analytic sample is 142 and only 87 students answered the item; the exam comparison reports t(140) = 1.31 with p = .008 when that t corresponds to p ≈ .19; Table 2's group sizes sum to 127 against a stated 142 and an implied 142; the perceived-control mean of 3.847 is unattainable from 87 integer responses; and the secondary clarity item reports a sample SD of 0.10 for ten integer responses centred exactly on 3.00, where the smallest non-zero value available is roughly 0.47. Notably, only the headline r = .24 with p = .004 at n = 142 is internally consistent, which is what convinces me the abstract's r = .42 is the erroneous figure rather than the reported one.

I considered whether this reaches my fatal threshold, specifically whether the conclusion is presupposed such that no result could have contradicted it. The evidence is suggestive: an exam difference reported as p = .008 is narrated as failing "to reach a comparable level" and as "weaker still" than p = .004, and a point-biserial r of .24 becomes a "dependable strategy." The narrative appears insensitive to the direction of its own statistics. I nonetheless stop short of fatal, because the primary association is arithmetically coherent and a defensible paper survives inside this one: a single-course, survivorship-limited correlational report of a small positive association between dashboard sessions and completion, with the perceived-control finding either recomputed or withdrawn, all causal and policy language removed, and generalisation confined to the observed setting. That is substantial rewriting, not repair of wording, which is why D3 blocks. Whether the numerical impossibilities reflect transcription errors or a data-handling failure is properly the methodology seat's call, and if the latter, fatality should be reconsidered there.

Lower-order observations that do not carry their own decision weight: ten of the sixteen listed references are never cited in the text, which inflates apparent coverage; the final exam score appears as a primary measure in Table 1 and in a reported t-test but is never defined in §3.3; the ten-student clarity subsample has an unspecified selection rule and does no argumentative work; §3.3 asserts that single-item overall ratings "are common in dashboard studies" without citation; §3.1's remark that a required course gives "the sample some disciplinary breadth even within one course" is an unearned move toward generalisability; and three-decimal reporting of a single-item ordinal mean conveys precision the instrument cannot deliver. The manuscript contains no reviewer-directed instruction or other integrity irregularity of that kind.
