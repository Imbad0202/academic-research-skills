## Review Body

I score only D3, which I own. Everything else I report below is offered as evidence for the seats that own it; my findings are not restricted to my scoring remit, but my score is.

The manuscript's argument, stated at its most defensible, is this: administrators occupying the hinge between accreditor and department experience QA as ritual, and rather than dismissing it they perform interpretive work — distributing ownership, framing the job vocationally — through which quality culture is negotiated rather than manufactured. That is a plausible and worthwhile claim, and the interview material offered is suggestive of it. My concern is not that the thesis is implausible. It is that the chain from evidence to conclusion breaks at three separate load-bearing points, and at each break the paper asserts the opposite of what its own text records.

The most serious break is the treatment of Delacroix (2018). Section 2 states, correctly and at length, that Delacroix argues *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture, warning that "participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual" and that "an institution can hold elaborate consultations and still lack any genuine ownership of standards." The reference-list annotation says the same thing. Section 5 then recruits Delacroix in support of the paper's central practical recommendation, describing him as recommending that institutions treat consultation as "the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture." This is not a nuance lost in paraphrase; it is a 180-degree reversal, and the reversed version is doing the work. The paper's third of three headline contributions — the design implication — rests on a source that the paper itself, forty paragraphs earlier, reports as saying the opposite. Worse, the recommendation the paper extracts ("processes that maximize genuine participation should be favored") is precisely the position Delacroix warns is co-opted into performance, and the paper's own ritual-compliance finding is the strongest available evidence for Delacroix's actual warning. The argument therefore not only misuses the citation but discards the one theoretical resource that would have integrated its own data.

The second break is at §4.3. Two adjacent sentences state that the study "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views," and that a minority of participants who questioned the value of the whole QA enterprise "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis." These cannot both be true. The exclusion criterion given is conformity to the theme structure the analysis was supposed to produce, which means the themes were validated against a corpus from which non-conforming cases had been removed. This is selection on the outcome, declared in the text, immediately followed by a comprehensiveness claim that the same paragraph refutes. The consequence reaches the thesis directly: the paper's signature move — that administrators do not resolve the compliance/meaning tension but "held it open" as identity work — is exactly the interpretation that the excluded skeptics would have contested, and the reader has no way to know how many of them there were. I note without asserting a causal link that the abstract reports fourteen interviews and §3.2 reports twelve; the reader is entitled to know whether the two are the same two.

The third break is scope. The abstract is properly bounded to three universities in one national system. Section 5 then claims the findings "demonstrate that universities across the sector" behave in a certain way, that the study "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture," and that "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension." Twelve (or fourteen) purposively recruited interviewees, all reached through institutional QA offices — a sampling route that selects for people invested in QA machinery — cannot license a sector-level claim in any national system, let alone all of them. "Demonstrate" and "confirms" are also the wrong inferential verbs for interpretive thematic work. The Conclusion compounds this with "the first comprehensive account," a priority claim the literature review has already undercut by citing Pettersen (2022) on distributed leadership in the quality office and Rahman (2020) on identity among quality professionals — the paper's own themes 2 and 3 — neither of which is engaged anywhere in the Findings or Discussion.

Two further inferential problems deserve the authors' attention even though they are secondary. Section 4.4 announces that "the survey corroborated the qualitative picture" and then, two sentences later, reports that the survey contradicted what the interviews had led the authors to expect; §5 re-labels the same result "a note of complication." A disconfirmed expectation is not corroboration, and the mixed-methods integration claim currently rests on the mislabeling. And §4.1 asserts that themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol," which conflicts with the semi-structured design declared in §3.1 and, more importantly, inverts the inference: uniformity produced by a common instrument is the mechanism most likely to manufacture cross-site convergence, not evidence against artifact.

Because a load-bearing claim is contradicted by the paper's own reported material at three points, D3 is a block. It is not fatal: a recoverable argument exists, evidence of a recognizable kind is offered, and the repairs — reinstate Delacroix's actual position and re-derive the design implication from it, report and analyze the excluded dissenting cases or retract the comprehensiveness claim, retract §5 and §6 to the abstract's stated scope, re-describe the survey as divergent, and reconcile the sample count and the anonymization statement — are achievable without new fieldwork. They do, however, require rewriting the Discussion and Conclusion rather than softening them.
