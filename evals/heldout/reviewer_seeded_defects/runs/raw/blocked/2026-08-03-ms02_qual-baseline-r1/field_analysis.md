# Field Analysis Report

## Paper Basic Information

- **Title**: Building Institutional Quality Culture: Administrator Perspectives on Quality Assurance Implementation in Universities
- **Abstract length**: ~165 words
- **Full text length**: ~2,400 words (excluding references) — notably thin for a mixed-methods empirical paper; typical Q1/Q2 higher-education journals expect 7,000–9,000 words
- **Number of references**: 12
- **Language**: English

### Internal inconsistencies detected during reading (flagged for reviewer targeting, not adjudicated here)

These are surfaced because they determine how the review team must be configured. They are observations from the manuscript text, not verdicts.

1. **Sample size contradiction**: Abstract states "Fourteen administrators were interviewed"; §3.2 states "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)". The two numbers cannot both be true.
2. **Survey subgroup arithmetic**: §4.4 compares n=9 and n=11 against a total of n=48 across three institutions; the remaining 28 respondents are unaccounted for, and no third-institution figure is given.
3. **Citation–source contradiction**: §5 states the findings "align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture." Both §2 and the reference-list annotation state that Delacroix argues **against** exactly this. The Discussion appears to invert the cited source's position.
4. **Anonymity vs. identification**: §3.5 claims data were "fully anonymized… so that no individual could be identified," yet §4.1 identifies a speaker as "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" — a uniquely identifying description.
5. **Qualitative-inference overreach**: §4.1 claims themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" while §3.3 describes *semi-structured* interviews; §4.3 claims "balanced representation… capturing the full range of administrator views" in the same paragraph that reports dissenting views were **excluded**.
6. **Generalization beyond design**: §5 states findings "demonstrate that universities across the sector" behave in a given way and offers the study as "a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole" works, from a 3-institution, single-national-system sample. §6 claims "the first comprehensive account," a priority claim unsupported by the 12-reference base.
7. **Statistical reporting**: "statistically significant difference (p<.05)" with no test named, no effect size, no test statistic, no distributional justification for n=9 vs n=11.

## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Quality in Higher Education** (Taylor & Francis) — Closest topical fit; the journal's core readership is QA practitioners and scholars, and "quality culture" is a recurring theme in its pages. Its typical empirical article tolerates modest sample sizes if the analytic procedure is transparent. Best realistic home **after** substantial methods build-out. Already cited in the manuscript (Iversen, 2021), which supports fit.
2. **Journal of Higher Education Policy and Management** (Taylor & Francis) — Strong fit for the "administrator as intermediary" framing, which is this paper's most genuinely original angle. The journal publishes administrator-focused and institutional-practice work and welcomes single-system studies. Also already cited (Silva & Tan, 2021), and this paper positions itself as the empirical follow-up to that conceptual piece — a clear contribution narrative.
3. **Higher Education Quarterly** (Wiley) — Higher bar; would require the theoretical contribution to be sharpened well beyond its current state and the generalization claims to be disciplined back to what a three-site design supports. Listed as a stretch target, not a first submission.

*Not recommended as a first target*: *Studies in Higher Education* or *Higher Education* — current word count, reference base, and methodological reporting are far below their thresholds; a desk reject is the probable outcome.

**Review Focus**:

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Identity Description**: Mixed-methods methodologist in an education faculty, specializing in QUAL-dominant sequential designs and the integration problem — how qualitative and quantitative strands are actually joined rather than merely juxtaposed. Trained in thematic analysis and reflexive TA, teaches a doctoral seminar on trustworthiness criteria, and reviews regularly for *Journal of Mixed Methods Research*. Has published on the misuse of significance testing in small-n institutional survey work.

**Review Focus**:
1. **Analytic transparency of the qualitative strand.** §3.4 says coding "proceeded iteratively… until a stable structure was reached" — this is not a reportable procedure. Require: coding framework or codebook, whether analysis was inductive or template-based, how many coders and with what reconciliation process, and what trustworthiness strategies were used (member checking, audit trail, negative-case analysis). Interrogate the §4.1 claim that themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" against §3.3's description of *semi-structured* interviews, and against the absence of any appended protocol.
2. **Treatment of disconfirming evidence.** §4.3 states dissenting participants "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure." Under any recognized qualitative standard this inverts the logic of analysis: negative cases test a thematic structure, they are not excluded by it. Evaluate this alongside the same paragraph's claim of "balanced representation… capturing the full range of administrator views," which the exclusion directly contradicts. Also test the recurring saturation-style language ("recurred across every interview," "recurred repeatedly") against 12 interviews spread over three institutions.
3. **Quantitative strand and integration.** The p<.05 subgroup claim names no test, reports no test statistic, no effect size, and no confidence interval, and rests on n=9 vs n=11; assess whether any inferential claim is defensible and whether the survey instrument's provenance, item wording, scale anchors, and reliability are reported at all (they are not). Reconcile the subgroup Ns against the stated total of 48. Then assess integration: does the survey do analytic work, or is it decoration? Note that §4.4 reads the survey as "corroborat[ing] the qualitative picture" in the same paragraph where it concedes the survey contradicted expectations — evaluate whether a genuine divergence is being reframed as convergence.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the paper's confident inferential language is licensed by its design. The recurring pattern is strong claim wording sitting on absent procedural detail — "systematically," "full range," "statistically significant," "demonstrate," "confirms." Each needs either supporting apparatus or downgraded phrasing.

**Possible blind spots**: May treat the paper as salvageable-by-reporting — assuming the missing procedures exist and simply were not written up. That assumption should not be granted by default; the §4.3 exclusion statement suggests a substantive analytic problem, not a reporting omission. May also under-weight the ethics issue in §4.1 as a writing slip rather than a participant-protection matter.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #3

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Identity Description**: Professor of higher education studies whose research programme is internal QA and quality culture; has published comparative work on how institutions build (or fail to build) internalized quality commitment, and knows the field's foundational literature intimately — the EUA Quality Culture Project reports, Harvey and Green's conceptions of quality, Harvey and Stensaker on quality culture typologies, Newton on academics' responses to QA, and the audit-culture tradition. Frequently reviews for *Quality in Higher Education* and *Higher Education Quarterly*.

**Review Focus**:
1. **Literature adequacy and the missing foundation.** All 12 references date from 2018–2022. A quality-culture paper that cites nothing from the field's constitutive work is not positioned within the literature — it is floating above it. Specify what must be engaged: the EUA quality-culture framework and its structural/cultural distinction, Harvey and Green on quality as a multi-dimensional concept, Harvey and Stensaker's quality-culture typology, Newton's work on academics' lived responses to QA, and the audit-culture literature the paper gestures at through Harlow (2019) without naming. Also assess the field-level claim in §2 that the quality-culture strand is "still emergent" — it has been an active literature for roughly two decades.
2. **Conceptual precision and originality of contribution.** "Quality culture" is never defined operationally, and the paper slides between quality culture, quality assurance, compliance behavior, and institutional culture. Test whether the three themes — ritual compliance, distributed leadership, identity work — are genuinely new findings or restatements of already-established positions the paper itself cites (Iversen/Okonkwo on documentation displacing reflection, Pettersen on distributed leadership in the quality office, Rahman on identity and vocation among quality professionals). If each theme maps onto a cited source, the contribution claim requires reformulation.
3. **Fidelity to cited sources.** §5 attributes to Delacroix (2018) the recommendation that consultation be treated as "the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture," and calls the findings aligned with it. §2 and the reference annotation both state Delacroix argues the opposite. This is a substantive misreading at the point where the paper builds its main practical recommendation — the recommendation currently rests on an inverted source. Check whether other citations are used to support claims their described positions do not support, and whether the §2 "tension" between cultivation and performance is actually carried through the analysis or dropped.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the paper's central practical recommendation survives correcting the Delacroix reading. If Delacroix's actual argument is that participatory consultation is itself co-optable into compliance ritual, then that argument directly threatens §5's recommendation — and the paper's own ritual-compliance finding is evidence for Delacroix, not against him. Engaging this properly would strengthen the paper considerably; leaving it unaddressed leaves the Discussion's core claim unsupported.

**Possible blind spots**: May be so focused on citation gaps and conceptual definition that the paper's genuinely promising contribution — the administrator-as-intermediary empirical angle, which does follow on productively from Silva and Tan (2021) — gets buried under corrections. Synthesizer should preserve that signal. May also default to "add these citations" when the deeper issue is that the argument was not built from the literature in the first place.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #4

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Identity Description**: Organizational sociologist in a public-administration school, specializing in accountability regimes, institutional decoupling, and audit culture; publishes on how organizations respond to external evaluation through ceremonial conformity, and has served as a research-ethics committee member reviewing qualitative studies involving identifiable professional elites. Reads higher education as one instance of a general problem in regulated professional organizations, and brings the theoretical vocabulary this paper is missing.

**Review Focus**:
1. **The unnamed theory the paper is standing on.** The central finding — elaborate documentation maintained for external audiences while disconnected from core work ("theater, and everyone knows it's theater") — is a textbook instance of institutional decoupling and ceremonial conformity, a phenomenon with a large existing theoretical literature (neo-institutional theory, ceremonial conformity, audit society, gaming and performativity under measurement regimes). The paper describes the mechanism without naming it, and therefore cannot explain it, predict its variation, or say anything new about it. Similarly, "distributed leadership" and "identity work" are invoked as theme labels but never connected to their respective theoretical traditions. Specify the frameworks that would let these findings do analytic work.
2. **Participant protection for identifiable elites.** §3.5 claims data were "fully anonymized… so that no individual could be identified," yet §4.1 attributes a quotation to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region," and other quotes are attributed by specific role plus specific institutional type ("associate dean for quality at the public research university," "one registry head"). In a study of 12 senior post-holders across three named institutional types, role-plus-institution attribution is functionally identifying, and the quotations are professionally sensitive ("It's theater"). This is not a stylistic issue; it is a conflict between a stated ethical commitment and the reporting practice, and it requires either revised attribution conventions or an explanation of what consent covered.
3. **Level-of-analysis discipline and the missing external actor.** The paper's most interesting empirical result — administrators perceive ritualism while staff report moderately positive quality culture — is treated as a puzzle to note in passing. From an organizational-analysis standpoint this divergence *is* the finding, and it has a straightforward candidate explanation the paper never considers: decoupling means those closest to the interface with the external body absorb the ceremonial work, which is precisely why they see it as ceremony. Additionally, the paper claims quality culture is negotiated "among administrators, academics, and external bodies," but external bodies were never studied and academics were never interviewed — two of the three negotiating parties are absent from the data. Assess whether the conclusion is sized to what was actually collected.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether this study can say anything that generalizes beyond three institutions in one system, and whether the authors recognize that naming the mechanism (decoupling) is what would let them make a claim about mechanisms rather than about "the sector as a whole." Also whether the survey's positive finding is being downplayed because it is inconvenient — the paper concedes it "had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture" and then labels it corroboration anyway.

**Possible blind spots**: May push theoretical apparatus that would overwhelm a practitioner-facing QA journal's readership, and may under-value the QA-field-specific literature R2 owns. May also treat the higher-education context as generic when sector-specific institutional QA regimes have distinctive features. Synthesizer should moderate the volume of theory recommended against the target journal chosen.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

**Special characteristics requiring attention**

1. **Fluency–substance inversion.** This manuscript is well written at sentence level and structurally complete, which will bias reviewers toward reading it as more mature than it is. Every reviewer should be instructed to check claim language against supporting apparatus rather than judging by prose quality. The recurring pattern is a strong verb sitting on absent evidence: "demonstrate," "confirms," "systematically," "full range," "the first comprehensive account."

2. **Reference-existence verification is a prerequisite, not a review finding.** All 12 references carry `10.5555/` DOIs in sequential order (1042001–1042012); `10.5555` is a reserved test prefix. Journal names, authorship, and volume/page data are plausible but the DOI pattern is not consistent with a real reference list. The Journal-Fit Reviewer should attempt verification before any recommendation is finalized. If the references cannot be verified, that finding supersedes all substantive review commentary and changes the nature of the required editorial response. Reviewers should not build conclusions on top of unverified sources.

3. **Self-contradiction cluster.** The manuscript contradicts itself in at least five places (sample size 14/12; anonymity claim vs. identifying attribution; semi-structured vs. structured protocol; balanced representation vs. excluded dissenters; survey as corroboration vs. survey as contradicting expectations). These are individually correctable but collectively indicate the draft has not been internally reconciled. Reviewers should report them as a pattern, not as isolated typos.

4. **Register: developmental, verdict: evidence-based.** Given early-draft maturity, all four reviewers should write in a developmental register — specific, actionable, oriented to what would make the paper publishable. The register governs wording only. The recommendation itself must follow from the evidence against the criteria; a constructive tone must not soften the verdict, and the paper's real problems (unsupported generalization, inverted citation, disconfirming-evidence exclusion, ethics inconsistency) must be stated plainly.

**Complementarity and tension among reviewers**

- **R2 ↔ R4 tension (productive, keep both)**: R2 will call for grounding in the higher-education QA literature; R4 will call for grounding in organizational/neo-institutional theory. Both are correct about different absences. The synthesizer should present these as complementary — the paper needs field-specific positioning *and* a named mechanism — and should not let one framework crowd out the other. If the target journal is *Quality in Higher Education*, R2's literature takes precedence in depth while R4's theory is applied more lightly.
- **R1 ↔ R4 overlap (deliberate, differently owned)**: Both will flag the disconfirming-evidence exclusion and the ethics/anonymity issue. R1 owns these as methodological-rigor and reporting-standard failures; R4 owns them as participant-protection and level-of-analysis failures. Synthesizer should merge the findings but preserve both rationales, as they imply different fixes.
- **R1 ↔ EIC tension**: R1 may treat missing procedure as a reporting gap that revision can close; the Journal-Fit Reviewer may judge the design itself too thin for the target journal. This is the decision point that determines whether the outcome is major revision or reject-and-resubmit-elsewhere. The synthesizer must resolve it explicitly rather than averaging the two positions.
- **All four ↔ the Delacroix inversion**: R2 owns this finding, but it has consequences for the others. The paper's practical recommendation (§5, third finding) is built on the inverted reading, so R1's assessment of whether conclusions follow from data and the Journal-Fit Reviewer's assessment of reader value both depend on it. The synthesizer should trace this single error through to its downstream effects rather than logging it once as a citation slip.
- **Preserve the positive signal**: Across four critical reviews, one genuine strength must not be lost — the administrator-as-intermediary focus is a real gap in the literature, and the administrator/staff perception divergence is a genuinely interesting empirical result. Both are worth developing. The synthesizer should ensure the report tells the authors what to build on, not only what to fix.
