### D4: cross_disciplinary_relevance

score: block
trigger: "Central interdisciplinary or cross-context generalisation claims are asserted with no supporting evidence or scope conditions"

The block rests on the conjunction of two features. First, §5 escalates a 12-interview, three-institution study into a sector-wide account ("universities across the sector"; "administrators everywhere"), and §6 adds a priority claim ("the first comprehensive account"), with no scope conditions attached at the point of claiming. Second, the manuscript withholds the one contextual fact an adjacent-field reader needs in order to test any transfer claim — which national QA regime these institutions inhabit — while simultaneously importing terms of art from organisational sociology ("ritual compliance", "distributed leadership", "identity work") without definition or citation to their source literatures. The combined effect is that a reader outside higher education QA can neither bound the claim nor decode the constructs through which it is expressed. This is repairable by rewriting rather than by new data, which is why the supporting finding W2 is banded Major, but the dimension itself does not currently pass at the level of "usable outside the home subfield".

## Review Body

I read this manuscript as a sociologist of organisations and professions who works on audit cultures and on how professionals absorb external evaluation regimes. I am not a higher education researcher; I make no judgement about the canonical QA literature, the ESG/accreditation landscape, or the survey statistics, and I defer to colleagues on those. What I can assess is whether the study takes seriously the kind of object it has caught, whether its interview encounter is treated as part of the field it studies, and whether participants have been protected.

The paper has caught something genuinely interesting and does not know what it is. "QA as ritual compliance" — an evidence portfolio "that nobody reads except the panel", produced on a calendar, leaving "nothing behind that a teacher would recognize as helping them teach" — is not a novel inductive discovery. It is a near-textbook description of decoupling: ceremonial conformity to institutional expectations maintained alongside, and insulated from, technical activity. The consequence of not naming it is not merely a missing citation. It is that the paper cannot answer its own best question. Why do administrators sustain, year after year, a system they themselves describe as theatre? The organisational-theory answer — that decoupling is stable precisely because it is functional, buying external legitimacy while protecting the technical core from disruption — is available, well evidenced, and would also dissolve the §4.4 "puzzle" the authors flag as surprising. If ritualism is concentrated in the offices that produce the ceremony, and the technical core is buffered from it, then moderately positive staff sentiment is the predicted result, not an anomaly. The paper reports the divergence as a curiosity for future work when its own data are consistent with an existing explanation. The same holds for §4.3: "identity work" is a named literature with a substantial body of work on identity regulation and professional identity under managerial and audit pressure, and none of it is engaged.

Second, and more fundamental to how I read qualitative material of this kind: these informants are QA professionals. Their occupational competence *is* the production of accounts for evaluative audiences. When a quality director says "it's theater, and everyone knows it's theater," that utterance is doing work — it positions the speaker as someone who sees through the machinery while continuing to operate it, and it establishes critical distance with an academic interviewer who is plainly receptive to hearing it. I am not suggesting the informant is insincere. I am suggesting that a study of how professionals absorb an audit regime cannot treat statements produced inside a research interview as unmediated windows onto experience, because the interview is itself an occasion of accounting within the same institutional field. The paper is one step from this point in §4.3, where it observes that the vocational framing functioned as a coping resource, and does not take it. The minimum remedy is a positionality statement, an account of recruitment through institutional QA offices and what that selects for, and analytic attention to how the accounts were co-produced. The stronger remedy is to make the performance of critical distance part of the finding rather than a threat to it.

Third, the two central themes are in open contradiction and the paper does not reconcile them. §4.1 says QA is empty theatre. §4.2 says administrators spend most of their energy on persuasion and that quality "only works if the heads of department feel it's theirs", with distributed ownership "recurr[ing] across every interview". Both cannot be straightforwardly true as reported. Either the persuasion work lands, in which case the ritual account is overstated; or it does not, in which case §4.2 is aspiration reported as accomplished practice. §4.3's resolution — that administrators "held it open" — is the most promising move in the paper, but it is asserted rather than evidenced, and the participants best placed to adjudicate between the readings are exactly the ones §4.3 says were excluded.

I want to be precise about how far my theoretical demand goes, because I am aware of my own reflex here. Engaging the decoupling and identity-work literatures is a required revision: without it the paper's headline finding is undertheorised in a way that costs it its explanation. Rebuilding the paper as organisational sociology — institutional work, boundary spanning, dual accountability, professional legitimacy under audit — is advisory. Those tools would convert descriptive themes into an explanation of why the intermediary position is structurally strained, which is precisely the apparatus §2 correctly notes Silva and Tan left conceptual and then does not supply either. But that is a bigger paper than the authors set out to write, and the target readership may not require it.

Finally, and independent of everything above: the manuscript as it stands should not be published in any form. §3.5 states that data were fully anonymised so that no individual could be identified. The Findings then attribute the paper's most critical quotations to the quality director of the largest private university in the region and to the associate dean for quality at the public research university. In a three-institution design within a single national system, those descriptors are unique identifiers — the population holding each role is one person, and any reader inside that system will know who spoke. The quotations so attributed are sharply critical of the speakers' own employers. This is foreseeable, avoidable harm; it contradicts the paper's own ethics statement; and in my experience on a research ethics committee it is handled as a reportable protocol deviation rather than as a revision request. It also compounds oddly with the withholding of the national context: the paper masks the analytically necessary detail and exposes the ethically sensitive one. Both directions are wrong, and only one of them is urgent.

### S1: §4.3's refusal to resolve the compliance/meaning tension is the paper's best idea

The observation that administrators locate their professional identity *in* the gap rather than closing it is genuinely valuable and is where a sociologist would want the paper to live. It anticipates, without naming, the identity-work literature on how professionals sustain commitment inside systems they judge hollow. Developed and evidenced, this could carry the paper.

- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3, "Rather than resolving the tension between compliance and meaning, they held it open"
- **Confidence**: 4 — trained in the sociology of professions and professional identity under managerial pressure.

### S2: The research object is well chosen and the gap is correctly located

§2 identifies the administrator as an intermediary and is candid that the closest prior treatment remains conceptual rather than grounded in administrators' own accounts. That is a defensible warrant for an interview study, and the object — the actor who must reconcile external requirement with internal practice — is exactly the position organisational sociology finds most informative about how audit regimes are absorbed.

- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §2, "Silva and Tan (2021) describe this intermediary position as structurally uncomfortable"
- **Confidence**: 4 — routine judgement about problem selection in organisational fieldwork.

### S3: The level-divergence observation in §4.4 is a real finding, undersold

That administrator-level ritualism coexists with more favourable sentiment further down is a substantive result about where audit pressure concentrates in an organisation. The paper treats it as an inconvenience to its qualitative story; it is arguably the most theoretically tractable thing in the data.

- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.4, "the felt tension is more acute at the administrative level than among staff at large"
- **Confidence**: 3 — I do not assess the survey statistics, only the interpretive value of the contrast.

### W1: Role-attributed quotations make participants identifiable and contradict the ethics statement

Within a three-institution, single-system design, "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" are unique role descriptors: one person occupies each. The quotations attached to them are critical of their own institutions. This is deductive disclosure of a kind that exposes named individuals to employment and reputational risk, and it directly contradicts §3.5's assurance that no individual could be identified. Two further absences compound it: there is no indication that consent covered publication of role-attributed quotations, and no ethics approval reference or approving body is given, so the assurance cannot be verified. Remedy: strip all role-plus-institution attributions to a generic participant identifier, remove institution-type pairing from quotation attributions, re-consent affected participants, and state the approval reference. This is not a stylistic matter and cannot be traded off against the paper's merits.

- **Severity**: Critical
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.5 and §4.1, "all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis so that no individual could be identified in reported findings" and "the quality director of the largest private university in the region"
- **Confidence**: 5 — sits on a university research ethics committee where deductive disclosure in small-N organisational research is the recurring issue.

### W2: Sector-wide generalisation from three institutions, asserted without scope conditions

§5 moves from three institutions to "universities across the sector" and to a claim about what administrators face "everywhere", and §6 adds a priority claim to being the first comprehensive account. Nothing in the design licenses this: three purposively accessed institutions in one unnamed national system, with participants recruited through their own QA offices, support claims about those settings and a warranted argument about mechanism, not a description of the sector. The Conclusion's gesture toward future work in other systems does not repair a claim already made in the indicative. Remedy: state the claim as analytic rather than empirical generalisation, name the scope conditions (regime type, institutional mix, recruitment route), and delete the priority claim.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §5, "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension between external requirement and internal meaning" and "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture"
- **Confidence**: 4 — routine assessment of scope claims in small-N organisational fieldwork.

### W3: The paper's central finding has an existing theory it neither cites nor engages

Ritual compliance sustained alongside insulated technical practice is decoupling; the unread evidence portfolio is ceremonial conformity. "Identity work" likewise names a developed literature. Neither is cited, and the borrowed vocabulary is used without definition, which is also why an adjacent-field reader cannot tell whether the terms carry their technical sense or a colloquial one. The analytic cost is concrete: the paper cannot explain why administrators sustain a system they call theatre, and it presents the §4.4 sentiment divergence as an unexplained puzzle when decoupling predicts it. Remedy: define the imported constructs at first use, engage the organisational-theory account of why decoupling is stable and functional, and re-read §4.4 through it.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: absence: §2 and the reference list — expected engagement with the organisational-theory literatures on decoupling and ceremonial conformity and on identity work; checked §2, §4.1, §4.3, §5, and all twelve references
- **Confidence**: 5 — neo-institutionalist by training, working directly on audit cultures and quantification.

### W4: Informant accounts are treated as transparent reports rather than as situated performances

The informants are professional producers of accounts for evaluative audiences, recruited through their own QA offices, speaking to a receptive academic interviewer. "It's theater, and everyone knows it's theater" is a positioning move as well as a report. §4.3 notices that the vocational framing does work for the speaker and stops there instead of extending the same reflexivity to the ritual-compliance theme. Absent a positionality statement, an account of the recruitment route's selection effects, and analytic attention to how accounts were produced, the reader cannot distinguish a finding about QA from a finding about how QA professionals talk about QA to researchers. Remedy: add positionality and reflexivity, and treat the performance of critical distance as an analysable feature of the data.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3, "appeared to function as a coping resource"
- **Confidence**: 4 — has run comparable interview fieldwork inside organisations whose informants are fluent in audit language.

### W5: Themes 4.1 and 4.2 contradict each other and are never reconciled

QA cannot be theatre that leaves nothing behind and also the object of successful coalition-building that produces genuine departmental ownership, both reported without qualification and both said to recur across every interview. The manuscript presents the two as complementary themes when they are competing accounts of the same practice. §4.3's "held it open" is offered as the bridge but is asserted rather than evidenced with case material. Remedy: state the tension explicitly, specify the conditions under which each account holds (which institutions, which levels, which phases of the review cycle), and evidence the reconciliation rather than asserting it.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.2, "This relational, distributed understanding of leadership recurred across every interview" and "Half my job is convincing people this matters"
- **Confidence**: 4 — reading organisational accounts for internal consistency is core to this method.

### W6: Dissenting participants were excluded, while the paper claims balanced representation

§4.3 claims the study captured the full range of administrator views and, in the same passage, reports that sceptics of the whole QA enterprise were dropped because they did not fit the three-theme structure. Fitting the coding frame is not a defensible exclusion criterion, and the excluded cases are precisely those that could adjudicate the §4.1/§4.2 contradiction and test whether the ritual theme is a shared perception or an artifact of the accepted frame. Remedy: withdraw the balanced-representation claim, report the dissenting accounts, and treat them as a negative case that either bounds or strengthens the analysis.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3, "the study achieved balanced representation of perspectives" and "these were excluded for space"
- **Confidence**: 4 — sampling and negative-case treatment in qualitative organisational research.

### W7: The national QA regime is withheld, so transferability cannot be assessed

The setting is given only as "a single national system". For a reader outside higher education QA, the regime's design is the primary determinant of what these accounts mean: whether review is periodic or continuous, consequential or advisory, standards-based or improvement-oriented. Without it, the paper's transfer claims cannot be evaluated even in principle, and the reader cannot tell whether the ritualism described is a general property of audit regimes or a response to one regime's particular incentives. Remedy: characterise the regime and its stakes at the level of design features, which is achievable without naming the country if masking is a venue or ethics requirement.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.2 Participants and setting — expected identification or design-level characterisation of the national QA regime governing the three case institutions; checked Abstract, §1, §2, §3.2, §5, §6
- **Confidence**: 3 — I may be unaware of a higher education venue convention that permits or requires this masking.

### W8: The intermediary position is observed but not theorised

§2 correctly diagnoses that the closest prior work left the administrator's intermediary position conceptual, then delivers three descriptive themes without supplying the missing apparatus. Boundary-spanning roles, institutional work, and the structural strain of dual accountability are directly applicable and would turn description into explanation. I mark this Minor because the paper is publishable as a descriptive contribution once W1–W7 are addressed, and because I recognise the risk of pushing a theoretical demand beyond what the target readership needs.

- **Severity**: Minor
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: §2, "the hinge between external requirement and internal practice"
- **Confidence**: 3 — confident about the available tools, less so about the venue's expectations.

### W9: Participant count differs between the abstract and the methods section

The abstract reports fourteen interviewees; §3.2 reports twelve with an explicit n=12. One of the two is wrong, and a reader outside the field has no way to tell which. I raise it only as a visible textual inconsistency and leave its analytic consequences to the methodology reviewer.

- **Severity**: Minor
- **Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract and §3.2, "Fourteen administrators were interviewed in depth" and "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)"
- **Confidence**: 4 — plain textual comparison, no statistical judgement involved.
