## Review Body

I read this manuscript as a higher education scholar who works on undergraduate persistence and on how institutions actually measure it. My question is narrow: does the paper's central concept survive translation into the field whose outcome variable it borrows? It does not, in its present form. What the study measures is a course-completion indicator in one statistics section. What it calls that measure, and what it claims on the strength of it, belong to a different construct at a different unit of analysis.

In higher education research and in institutional-research practice, retention denotes continued enrolment in an institution or programme across an enrolment boundary — the fall-to-fall rate is the canonical operationalisation, and persistence is the parallel term used when a student continues anywhere in the sector. Course-level outcomes have their own vocabulary: completion rates, pass rates, and in the gateway-course literature the DFW rate, which the authors' own reference list gestures at through Halloran (2020). These are not stylistic alternatives. A student who completes this statistics course and then leaves the institution is retained on the paper's measure and not retained on the field's. The paper never acknowledges the difference, and the Conclusion's promise of "a practical and generalizable lever" for retention "across programs and disciplines" is a claim about the construct the paper did not measure.

The coding decision compounds this. Formal withdrawal and enrolled-but-absent non-sitting are distinct behaviours with distinct antecedents, distinct administrative consequences (transcript notation, financial-aid implications, refund deadlines), and distinct policy levers. Collapsing them into one binary is a defensible analytic choice only if it is argued for and if the component counts are shown. Neither happens here. I also note that non-sitting is, in most grading regimes, a performance outcome that yields a failing grade rather than an exit — so the outcome variable mixes an exit behaviour with a performance failure and then gets interpreted as persistence.

On literature fidelity, one problem is serious enough to require correction before any reader can evaluate §2's equity argument, and I detail it below. Beyond that specific inversion, the review's shape is misleading in a way that matters to a domain reader: sixteen references are listed and six are cited. The uncited ten include the two entries that bear most directly on the paper's claims — Halloran (2020) on gateway-course interventions and Wexler & Ojo (2020) on cautionary LMS-trace retention modelling. A paper that borrows the retention outcome from the persistence literature and then engages that literature through zero in-text citations has not situated itself. Three decades of retention-intervention research, and the Tinto/Bean/Astin traditions along with their contemporary critiques, are absent; the entire theoretical account is one sentence of self-regulated learning framing, itself uncited at first statement.

Two matters fall outside my dimension and I route them rather than score them. To the methodology reviewer: the reported test statistics do not survive arithmetic inspection — t(156) is impossible on 87 respondents to the perceived-control item, t(140) is inconsistent with the 127 cases Table 2 reports, and t(140) = 1.31 does not yield p = .008. If the headline correlation is likewise not computable as reported, D1's fatal condition may be engaged where mine is not; I have scored only the domain consequences of the reporting, not the statistical validity. Also to methodology: §3.2 describes the sample as "a random sample of students enrolled" and then describes a mid-term volunteer announcement with non-respondents excluded, which are incompatible sampling accounts, and the last sentence of that section states that students were not informed their dashboard activity would be analysed. To the devil's advocate on D3: §1 and §2 promise correctness about correlational scope and cite Ibarra (2023) approvingly for auditing causal overreach, and then §5 opens with "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and asserts that increasing engagement "raises the probability" of completion, with §6 calling it "a dependable strategy." That is the exact error the paper cites Ibarra to condemn, and it is the reason the strength I record at S1 does not survive into the Discussion.

### S1: Faithful representation of the measurement and causal-scope critiques

The §2 treatment of click-based engagement proxies is accurate to the field's current position and unusually candid in conceding that the present study shares the flaw ("Most dashboard studies, including the present one, infer engagement from coarse behavioral proxies"). The Calloway (2019), Vandermeer (2023), and Ibarra (2023) characterisations are each consistent with what those sources present themselves as arguing. This is the part of the literature review a domain reader can trust — which is why the Discussion's abandonment of it is so costly.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "click-based engagement metrics should be treated as rough indicators rather than as faithful measures"

### S2: Accurate handling of goal orientation in relative-standing feedback

The paragraph on demotivation risk represents the achievement-goal literature correctly: the direction of a peer-comparison effect is conditional on framing and goal orientation, and performance-avoidance learners plausibly read unfavourable comparison as threat. This is the correct scope condition, stated in the field's own terms. It also happens to be the paragraph that contradicts the preceding one, which is the substance of W2.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "Performance-avoidance oriented students, in particular, may interpret an unfavorable comparison as a threat to be avoided"

### S3: The dashboard's interface features are specified

§3.1 names what the dashboard actually displayed, including the peer-comparison band, and states that it was available to all students from week one with no opt-in. Many dashboard studies omit this, which makes their relative-standing findings uninterpretable. §5.1's acknowledgement that interface features may shape response is the correct inference from that specification — though it is directly contradicted by §6's claim of generalisability.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.1 "engagement metrics, assignment progress, and a peer-comparison band"

### W1: "Retention" names a construct the study did not measure, and the inference is scaled to that construct

The outcome is completion of one course's final assessment in one term, with no student observed across terms or courses (§3.1 says so explicitly). The manuscript calls this retention in its title, abstract, measures, results, discussion, and conclusion, and then draws an implication for "higher education institutions worldwide" and for retention "across programs and disciplines." A reader in this field cannot accept any of those claims as stated: the paper has no enrolment-boundary observation, so it has no purchase on institutional or programme persistence. The repair is threefold — name the construct correctly (course completion, or the complement of the withdrawal-plus-non-sitting rate), justify the collapse of formal withdrawal and enrolled non-sitting as one exit category or separate them, and restate the contribution at the scale the data support, which is one required quantitative-reasoning section at one institution in one term. I flag for the venue-fit reviewer that this restatement materially changes what the contribution is.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 and §6 "was coded dichotomously as whether the student remained enrolled and completed the final assessment" and "improving retention across programs and disciplines"
**Confidence**: 5 — this is the definitional core of retention measurement in higher education research and institutional practice, and the field's usage is not contested.

Correction to my own band: on reflection about decision impact, I am recording this as Critical rather than Major, because the claim the paper advertises cannot be believed at all until the construct is renamed and the inference rescaled, and because the Conclusion's institutional claim is unsupportable rather than merely overstated. See the Severity line below, which supersedes the line above.

**Severity**: Critical

### W2: The source underpinning the equity rationale appears inverted, and the Discussion's alignment claim rests on the inversion

§2 asserts, as established fact, that "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students, who are said to gain the most from externalized progress cues (Ferro & Nakamura, 2021)." The reference list titles that work "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student." On its face the cited finding has been reversed in direction. Three consequences follow. First, the "equity-oriented rationale for institutional dashboard deployment" that the paper says this position underpins has no cited support. Second, the very next paragraph cites Osei (2020) for the discouragement effect, so §2 states a settled improvement finding and its contradiction two sentences apart without noticing — which mischaracterises a genuinely contested literature as resolved, and "reliably" is doing work no source here licenses. Third, the Discussion's claim that "Our finding that engagement tracks retention aligns with the view that externalized progress cues can support persistence" is anchored to the inverted reading; if Ferro & Nakamura report demotivation among lower achievers, the paper's aggregate association neither aligns with nor bears on that account, and the authors would owe readers a subgroup analysis by prior achievement that the manuscript does not attempt. I ask the authors to verify the source and, if the title reflects its findings, rewrite §2's equity paragraph and the Discussion sentence that depends on it.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 and reference list "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students" versus "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student"
**Confidence**: 4 — I judge direction from the reference title and the internal contradiction with the Osei paragraph; I cannot inspect the source itself, which is why I frame this as requiring verification.

### W3: The retention denominator cannot be reconciled with the reported exam analyses

By the paper's own coding, a student who did not sit the final is not retained and therefore has no final-exam score. §4.3 nonetheless states that all 142 students in the primary analytic sample were classified into engagement groups for the exam comparison, while Table 2 reports group sizes of 66 and 61, which sum to 127. Either reading is fatal to the headline number as reported. If all 142 have final-exam scores, then all 142 are retained on the paper's definition, the outcome has no variance, and r = .24 with retention cannot exist. If only 127 sat the final, then the exam analysis silently uses a subset, the reported degrees of freedom are wrong, and the retention rate must be reverse-engineered by the reader as 127/142. Table 1 gives no per-measure n, so nothing in the manuscript resolves which is the case. This is distinct from the omission at W4: here the reported figures actively contradict each other, and the contradiction sits underneath the paper's central claim rather than beside it. The authors must state the n for every measure and reconcile the retention coding with the exam sample.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: table: Table 2, Final exam comparison by engagement group (n = 66 and n = 61)
**Confidence**: 4 — the arithmetic and the definitional implication are plain; I defer to the methodology reviewer on which of the two readings the underlying data support.

### W4: No retention base rate, no withdrawal count, no split between the two exit behaviours

A retention study reports its base rate. Nothing in this manuscript states how many students were coded not retained, how that number divides between formal withdrawal and enrolled non-sitting, or what the completion rate was in the course as a whole against the analytic sample. Without those figures a domain reader cannot judge whether the outcome had enough variance to correlate with anything, cannot compare the section to gateway-course norms, cannot assess whether the sample's completion rate differs from the several-hundred-student enrolment it was drawn from, and cannot evaluate the practical significance the Discussion asserts. Reporting the counts is a small revision, and it is the precondition for evaluating everything else.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §4 Results — expected a course-completion base rate plus separate counts of formal withdrawals and enrolled non-sitters; checked Abstract, §3.3, §4.1, Table 1, §4.2, §5.1
**Confidence**: 5 — reporting the outcome base rate is a baseline expectation in retention research and in institutional-research practice.

### W5: The abstract and the results report different effect magnitudes

The abstract reports r = .42; §4.2 reports r = .24. These are not roundings of one another — they imply roughly 18% versus 6% of shared variance, and the Discussion's own hedge ("reliable but not large") fits the second and not the first. The abstract is the figure that will be quoted and meta-analysed. Whichever value is correct, the other must be removed, and the Discussion's characterisation of effect size checked against it.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract and §4.2 "Dashboard engagement correlated positively with retention (r = .42)" and "Dashboard engagement was positively associated with course retention (r = .24, p = .004)"
**Confidence**: 5 — direct comparison of two stated values in the same manuscript.

### W6: "Self-regulated learning behavior" is claimed as a measure but never measured

The abstract states that the study measured dashboard engagement, self-regulated learning behaviour, and course persistence. §3.3 contains no SRL instrument. What exists is a single-item perceived-control rating. In this literature a control belief and regulatory behaviour are distinct constructs measured by distinct instruments — the self-report SRL inventories, trace-based strategy indicators, and think-aloud protocols all exist precisely because a global control rating does not stand in for strategy use. The Discussion then treats the perceived-control difference as "consistent with a self-regulated learning account in which dashboards scaffold monitoring and adjustment," when nothing in the design observed monitoring or adjustment. Either add an SRL measure or delete the claim from the abstract and confine the Discussion to perceived control. I note that Kessler & Amadou (2019), a synthesis of SRL in digital environments, sits uncited in the reference list.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract and §5 "we measured dashboard engagement, self-regulated learning behavior, and course persistence" and "consistent with a self-regulated learning account in which dashboards scaffold monitoring and adjustment"
**Confidence**: 4 — the construct distinction is standard; I allow that the authors may consider the perceived-control item a proxy, in which case they must argue for it.

### W7: Ten of sixteen references are never cited, and the retention literature the paper borrows from is entirely unengaged

The in-text citations are Calloway (2019), Ferro & Nakamura (2021), Osei (2020), Rutledge & Berange (2022), Vandermeer (2023), and Ibarra (2023). The other ten entries appear only in the list. This inflates the apparent grounding of a review that engages six sources, and two of the unengaged entries are the ones a persistence reviewer would look for first: Halloran (2020) reviews gateway-course intervention studies, which is the exact genre and setting of this paper, and Wexler & Ojo (2020) is a cautionary study of retention modelling with LMS trace data, which is the exact method. A manuscript that borrows the retention outcome owes the reader a statement of what it adds relative to the intervention literature that has been accumulating for three decades; at present the contribution is asserted against a gap in the dashboard literature only. Either cite these works and position against them, or remove the entries.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §2 Literature Review — expected in-text engagement with Halloran (2020) on gateway-course retention interventions and Wexler & Ojo (2020) on cautionary LMS-trace retention modeling; checked §1 through §6 in-text citations against all 16 reference-list entries
**Confidence**: 5 — I enumerated the in-text citations directly against the list.

### W8: An uncited mediation literature is invoked for a mediation the study did not test

§5 states that the results complement "accounts emphasizing perceived control as a mediating construct." No account is cited, and no mediation was modelled — the design is cross-sectional, the mediator candidate is a single item measured mid-term, and the analysis is two independent-samples t-tests. Mediation language should be removed or replaced with an explicit statement that the pattern is compatible with, but does not test, such an account, with a citation supplied for whichever accounts the authors have in mind.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "It also complements accounts emphasizing perceived control as a mediating construct"
**Confidence**: 4 — the absence of citation and of any mediation model is verifiable from the text.

### W9: The self-regulated learning phase model is stated without attribution and its phases are used inconsistently

§1 introduces a three-phase cycle of forethought, performance, and reflection with no citation, though this is a specific named model rather than general background, and locates the dashboard's contribution in the reflective phase. §2 then locates it in "the forethought and self-monitoring phases," treating self-monitoring as a phase in its own right when in the model being paraphrased self-observation is a process within the performance phase. The result is that the theoretical mechanism moves between sections without explanation. Attribute the model at first use and fix the phase to which the dashboard's feedback is hypothesised to contribute.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 and §2 "learners cycle through phases of forethought, performance, and reflection" and "dashboards are hypothesized to support the forethought and self-monitoring phases of learning"
**Confidence**: 4 — the phase architecture is standard in the SRL tradition; the internal inconsistency between sections is plain on the page.
