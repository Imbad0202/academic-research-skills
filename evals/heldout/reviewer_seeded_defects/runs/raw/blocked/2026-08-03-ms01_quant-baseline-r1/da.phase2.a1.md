## Review Body

The most that this manuscript's design and data can support is a single descriptive sentence: among 142 students in one section of one introductory statistics course, the number of dashboard sessions accumulated over a term was modestly and positively associated with completing the final assessment, and among a subset of survey respondents, more frequent dashboard users reported a higher single-item sense of control. The paper does not stop there. It asserts causation in the Discussion, asserts a universal institutional prescription in the Conclusion, and asserts in the Abstract a correlation of a magnitude that its own Results section contradicts. The argument therefore fails not at its edges but at the junction where evidence is converted into conclusion.

The causal leap is the paper's own self-refutation, not merely an inferential slip. Section 1 promises that "We are careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story that might explain it, a distinction that the dashboard literature has not always maintained," and Section 2 approvingly cites an audit of exactly this failure. Section 5 then opens with "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and states that increasing engagement "therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course," and Section 6 claims the association both accompanies and "raises" retention. A cross-sectional observational design with no comparison condition, no temporal ordering of exposure and outcome, and no covariate adjustment licenses none of this. The paper cannot simultaneously hold itself out as the corrective to causal overreach and commit the overreach in its two most-read sections; whichever sentence a reader believes, the other is false.

Worse, the rival explanation that most plausibly generates the reported association is never named. Dashboard sessions are counted "during the term," while non-retention is defined as withdrawing before the final assessment or not sitting it. A student who withdraws in week five has five weeks in which to accumulate sessions; a student who completes has fifteen. The exposure variable is therefore partly a function of the outcome, and a positive correlation is close to arithmetically guaranteed even if the dashboard has no effect whatsoever. Reverse causation, differential exposure windows, and ordinary third-variable confounding by prior achievement, conscientiousness, or course commitment all fit r = .24 at least as well as the self-regulation story, and the Limitations section addresses none of them; it names only proxy coarseness, self-report bias, and single-site scope. A single sentence acknowledging that the design is correlational does not discharge the obligation to state which rival accounts the design cannot exclude. Selection compounds this: the analytic sample is described in one clause as "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" and in the next paragraph as those who "chose to respond" to a mid-term LMS announcement. Those are incompatible sampling frames, and only one of them supports any inference beyond the volunteers.

The manuscript also contains a cluster of reported statistics that cannot all be true of the described data, and each of them sits under a load-bearing claim. The Abstract reports r = .42 where Section 4.2 reports r = .24. The perceived-control group comparison reports t(156) when the entire primary analytic sample is 142 and only 87 respondents answered the item, which would imply df = 85. The exam comparison reports t(140) = 1.31 with p = .008 when that statistic corresponds to p ≈ .19, and the accompanying sentence says the comparison "did not reach a comparable level" while the printed p-value is below the stated alpha of .05. Table 2's group sizes sum to 127 against a text claim that all 142 students were classified. The secondary clarity item reports a sample SD of 0.10 for ten integer responses on a 1-5 scale, which is arithmetically impossible; with M = 3.00 exactly and integer responses, the smallest attainable non-zero SD is roughly 0.32. Individually each of these might be a transcription error. Collectively they mean a reader cannot determine which numbers the argument actually rests on, and I flag for the methodology seat that this pattern is also consistent with an analysis that was never run on the sample described, in which case the defect is not repairable by revision.

The second leg of the argument — perceived control — is weaker than the paper's language admits even setting the impossible df aside. The Abstract states that the study "measured dashboard engagement, self-regulated learning behavior, and course persistence," but no self-regulated learning behaviour was measured anywhere; a single-item global rating of felt control is not a behavioural measure of regulation, and Section 3.3 concedes it is one item chosen to limit burden. Section 5 then upgrades this item to "perceived control as a mediating construct" without any mediation analysis, without temporal ordering, and on a subsample. Mediation is a causal claim about sequence, and nothing in the data speaks to sequence.

The literature review is used inconsistently in ways that damage the argument rather than merely the citations. Section 2 asserts as established fact that "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," attributing this to a source whose own title is "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student"; the paper then builds "much of the equity-oriented rationale" on that premise and promises to "return to it in the Discussion," which it never does. Meanwhile the demotivation and goal-orientation heterogeneity the review takes seriously — including the possibility that peer-comparison bands drive performance-avoidance students away — is dropped entirely before the Conclusion recommends encouraging all students to engage, with no subgroup analysis offered. The same decorative-hedging pattern governs the engagement measure: the review concedes click counts are "rough indicators rather than faithful measures," and Section 3.3 concedes the median split is "a coarse simplification," yet the recommendation is precisely to raise the click count, which requires the proxy to be the thing that matters. Smaller matters compound the impression that hedges are placed where they will not constrain conclusions: a single-item integer mean reported to three decimals (3.847); a Pearson r reported for a dichotomous outcome without acknowledging it is point-biserial; final exam score appearing in Table 1 though never defined in Measures; "several hundred" enrolled students with no accounting of the gap to 142; and nine reference-list entries that are never cited in the text.

I score D3 as a block rather than a fatal block for one reason only: a recoverable argument does exist underneath the overreach. If the Abstract's r is corrected to match the Results, all causal and prescriptive verbs are removed, the exposure-window and reverse-causation accounts are analysed rather than ignored (at minimum by censoring session counts at a fixed early-term window before any withdrawal), the sampling description is made internally consistent, the degrees of freedom are reconciled with the actual analytic samples, and the universal-deployment recommendation is deleted, what remains is a modest, honest, single-site correlational note. That is a different and much smaller paper than the one submitted, but it is reachable by revision, which is why I stop short of the fatal trigger. The current version's core claims — that dashboard engagement improves retention, and that dashboards are a dependable and generalizable retention lever for institutions worldwide — are not supported by anything in Section 4.
