# Field Analysis Report

## Paper Basic Information

- **Title**: Perceived Usefulness and Self-Reported Use of a Learning Management System: A Cross-Sectional Survey of Undergraduate Students
- **Abstract length**: ~155 words
- **Full text length**: ~1,700 words of body text (~1,900 including abstract and references) — well below the 5,000–8,000-word norm for a full empirical article in this field; this is structurally a **brief report / research note**
- **Number of references**: 6

### Note on this manuscript's unusual profile

This paper inverts the common failure mode. Most manuscripts at this length and evidentiary depth overclaim; this one systematically **under**claims. Causal language is scrupulously avoided, the reverse-causality pathway is named, the self-report/behavioral-trace gap is acknowledged in both the literature review and the limitations, and generalization is explicitly bounded. Reviewers must be configured so they do **not** spend their effort attacking causal overreach that is not present. The live questions are contribution, sampling transparency, measurement adequacy, and construct validity of the dependent variable.

## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Research in Learning Technology** (ALT, open access; Q2/Q3) — Best fit. Publishes bounded single-institution studies, values transparent and modest reporting, and has an editorial culture receptive to work that is honest about its limits. Would still require a stated response rate and a stronger contribution argument.
2. **Electronic Journal of e-Learning** or **Contemporary Educational Technology** (Q3) — Realistic acceptance targets for an incremental single-site correlational finding. Lower novelty bar; expects competent execution and clear reporting, both of which the paper largely has.
3. **Australasian Journal of Educational Technology** (Q1/Q2) — Stretch target. Achievable only with substantial expansion: institutional LMS log data as a criterion measure, multivariate modelling with course-level and assessment-load covariates, and a full response-rate/nonresponse analysis. Should not be submitted in current form.

**Not recommended**: *Computers & Education*, *British Journal of Educational Technology*, *Internet and Higher Education*, *IJETHE*. All would desk-reject on incremental contribution regardless of methodological cleanliness.

## Reviewer Configuration Cards

### Reviewer Configuration Card #1

**Role**: Editor-in-Chief
**Identity Description**: Senior Editor of *Research in Learning Technology*, background in learning technology policy and open scholarship in UK/Australasian higher education; has handled several hundred single-institution LMS adoption submissions over a decade and desk-rejects the majority of them on contribution grounds; a vocal advocate for publishing modest, well-bounded findings but insistent that "modest" is not the same as "thin."
**Review Focus**:
1. **Contribution against a saturated literature.** The perceived-usefulness/use association has been meta-analyzed across e-learning contexts for over fifteen years. Does one more single-site *r* add anything, and does the manuscript make an explicit argument for why this sample, this platform, or this moment is informative? Currently the contribution claim is "an incremental data point" — the EIC must decide whether that is sufficient or whether it is an admission of non-publishability.
2. **Reference base adequacy and integrity.** Six references for an empirical article in a mature field is a fit-for-purpose failure at any tier. The foundational source of the construct itself (Davis's 1989 formulation of perceived usefulness) is absent, as is the entire UTAUT literature. Separately, the DOIs cited all sit under the `10.5555` prefix, which is a reserved test/example prefix rather than a live registrant range, and several journal titles are near-miss variants of real journals (*British Journal of Educational Technology Studies*, *Computers & Education Review*). Every citation must be independently resolved before any editorial decision.
3. **Format fit.** At ~1,700 words of body text this is a brief report, not a full article. The EIC should decide whether to redirect it to a short-communication format rather than assess it against full-article expectations.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the manuscript's own honesty about its limits ("incremental," "design-bounded," "modest") functions as scholarly discipline or as a pre-emptive defence against the question of why this should be published at all. The EIC's core judgement is whether a well-executed non-contribution is publishable.
**Possible blind spots**: May under-weight the fact that execution quality here is genuinely above average for the tier, and may dismiss the paper wholesale rather than identify the specific expansion path (log data + multivariate design) that would make it viable. Also unlikely to engage the psychometric detail.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1 — Methodology
**Identity Description**: Quantitative survey methodologist and psychometrician in an educational measurement department, specializing in the validity of self-report behavioural measures, common method variance, and single-item indicator performance; regularly consulted on survey instrument adaptation and reviews for measurement-focused journals.
**Review Focus**:
1. **Common method bias, entirely unaddressed.** Both variables are self-reported, on the same instrument, at the same time point, in adjacent scale format. This is the textbook condition for shared-method inflation of the observed correlation. The manuscript never mentions common method variance, offers no procedural remedy (item separation, differing response formats, marker variable), and runs no post hoc diagnostic. The reported *r* = .42 must therefore be treated as an upper bound on the substantive association, and the paper does not say so.
2. **Single-item ordinal dependent variable.** A five-point frequency item has unknown reliability, cannot have its reliability estimated, and coarsens a continuous behaviour into five bins. This attenuates the correlation in the opposite direction from CMB, and the paper discusses neither force. Anchors for categories 2–4 are never reported, so "a few times per week" as the median category cannot be located on the scale. No disattenuation is attempted and no rationale for a single item over a multi-item use scale is given.
3. **Sampling transparency and the duplicate-removal contradiction.** No response rate is reported and no denominator (total enrolled undergraduates) is given, so nonresponse bias is entirely uncharacterizable — the voluntary-response limitation in §6 is stated but not evidenced. Separately, §3.3 states that no identifying information was collected and that responses could not be linked to individuals, yet §3.1 reports the removal of five duplicate entries. These claims are in tension and the deduplication mechanism must be specified. Also missing: what "adapted" means for the Costa and Wren instrument (which items changed, how many), any factor-structure evidence in the present sample (α = .88 establishes internal consistency, not unidimensionality), and any demographic breakdown despite the claim that the sample "spanned all four year levels."

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the single reported coefficient can bear the interpretive weight placed on it once two uncorrected and opposing biases — method inflation and measurement attenuation — are acknowledged simultaneously.
**Possible blind spots**: Likely to treat the substantive question as settled and focus exclusively on estimation properties; may not question whether "frequency of access" is a meaningful construct at all, or engage with the field's contribution economics.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #3

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 2 — Domain
**Identity Description**: Senior higher education researcher specializing in technology acceptance and adoption in post-secondary institutions; has published comparative work on TAM, TAM2, and UTAUT applications in e-learning contexts and contributed to meta-analytic syntheses of acceptance predictors across learner populations.
**Review Focus**:
1. **The theoretical lineage is missing at its root.** "Perceived usefulness" is not a generic phrase; it is a defined construct from a specific instrument tradition. Davis (1989), Venkatesh and Davis (2000), and the UTAUT family are all absent. The manuscript borrows the construct's authority while citing none of its sources, and the operational definition offered in §2 is a paraphrase without attribution to the original formulation. This is a substantive literature failure, not a formatting one.
2. **Positioning against existing synthesis.** The paper claims comparability with prior work but never states what the prior distribution of effect sizes actually is. Meta-analytic estimates of the perceived-usefulness/use relationship in e-learning contexts exist and are well cited. Without anchoring *r* = .42 against that distribution, the reader cannot judge whether this result is typical, high, or low — which destroys the "incremental data point" rationale the paper rests on. Song (2018) is invoked for cross-institutional variability but no range is reported.
3. **Omitted domain-standard covariates.** In higher education specifically, LMS access frequency is heavily structured by course design, assessment deadlines, whether attendance or submission is LMS-mediated, and discipline. The Discussion concedes this ("course requirements and assessment schedules") but the design collects none of it. A domain reviewer will see this as the difference between an association and an artefact: if LMS-mandatory courses are unevenly distributed across the sample, the correlation is partly confounded by curricular structure. At minimum, year level, discipline, and course-mandated LMS dependence should have been collected and reported.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether this is a study about students or a study about course architecture that has mistaken itself for a study about students. Also whether the practical recommendation on onboarding (§5) has any warrant in the higher education implementation literature, given it is supported by a single practitioner reference.
**Possible blind spots**: Likely to accept self-report as the field's normal data source without challenge, since much of the TAM literature is built on it; may push the author toward a fuller acceptance model that the paper deliberately and defensibly declined to test.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #4

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 3 — Cross-disciplinary / Practical
**Identity Description**: Learning analytics researcher with an information systems and student data governance background, working in an institutional analytics unit; specializes in LMS trace-data modelling, the construct validity of engagement metrics, and the ethics and governance of institutional learning data. Publishes on why click-frequency measures fail as engagement proxies.
**Review Focus**:
1. **"Frequency of access" is the wrong dependent variable, regardless of how it is measured.** The learning analytics field's central finding of the last decade is that raw access frequency correlates poorly with meaningful engagement or outcomes — a student opening the LMS six times a day to check whether a grade posted is not more engaged than one who logs in twice and works for an hour. The paper's construct is login count, but its framing throughout ("engagement," "how much they use it") implies something richer. This is a construct validity problem sitting upstream of every measurement question Reviewer 1 raises, and fixing the measurement instrument would not fix it. Dwell time, resource-type breadth, and submission behaviour are the field's minimum alternatives.
2. **The log data was available and the paper knows it.** Every institution running an LMS holds server-side access logs. The manuscript names behavioural log data as a limitation (§6) and as future work (§7), which concedes that self-report was a design *choice*, not a constraint. A cross-disciplinary reviewer will ask directly: why was the criterion measure not obtained, was it attempted, and was the barrier technical, governance-related, or ethical? If it was a data governance barrier — a real and legitimate one, given anonymous consent and student privacy regimes — that should be stated as a substantive methodological constraint with its reasoning, which would considerably strengthen the paper's account of itself. If it was simply not attempted, the entire design is difficult to defend in 2020s educational technology research.
3. **The institutional actionability of *r* = .42.** The paper recommends attention to LMS onboarding. From an institutional research standpoint, a shared-variance figure of roughly 18% between a perception score and a self-reported login count provides essentially no basis for resource allocation, and the paper does not report *r*² or translate the finding into any institutional decision frame. Meanwhile, learning analytics already offers better-evidenced levers on the same problem. The practical section should either be substantiated or withdrawn.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether a study that acknowledges the superiority of trace data, has access to trace data, and recommends institutional action anyway has earned the right to that recommendation. Also whether student data governance considerations were a genuine and reportable design constraint.
**Possible blind spots**: May undervalue perception measures as such — perceived usefulness is a legitimate psychological construct that logs cannot capture, and the reviewer's log-data preference should not collapse into dismissing self-report wholesale. May also apply large-institution analytics infrastructure assumptions to a mid-sized university that may lack the capability.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

### First-party deterministic checks completed on the numerical claims

These were verified by direct computation, not inference, and reviewers should be told they are clean so review effort is not wasted re-deriving them:

- **Sample accounting**: 233 − 14 incomplete − 5 duplicate = 214. ✅ Consistent.
- **Confidence interval**: For *r* = .42 with *n* = 214, the Fisher *z* 95% CI back-transforms to [.303, .525], reported as [.30, .52]. ✅ Correct.
- **Power claim**: With *n* = 214, α = .05 two-tailed, power to detect *r* = .19 computes to ≈ .798 — meets ".80" only on rounding. The claim of "greater than .80" is marginally overstated at exactly *r* = .19 and becomes true at *r* ≈ .191. Trivial in practice; note it, do not build a critique on it.
- **Spearman vs Pearson** (ρ = .40 vs *r* = .42): consistent with a monotonic association and no strong non-linearity. ✅ Plausible.
- **Descriptives**: mean 3.6, SD 0.8 on a five-point scale. ✅ Within bounds.

**Not verifiable from the manuscript and requiring editorial action**: all six DOIs use the `10.5555` prefix, a reserved test/example range that will not resolve to live records, and several journal titles are close variants of real titles rather than exact matches. This must be resolved by the EIC before any substantive review verdict is issued. Flagged as an integrity check to perform, not as a finding.

### Particular characteristics requiring attention

1. **Do not review this paper as if it overclaims.** It does not. Every reviewer should be instructed that the causal-language critique is pre-empted in the text (§2, §5, §6) and that repeating it constitutes wasted review capacity. The register should be developmental where the paper is disciplined and firm where it is thin — but the verdict itself stays evidence-based against the criteria, not softened by the author's modesty.
2. **The central tension is contribution, not execution.** Execution is above the tier norm. Contribution is at or near zero. Reviewers should be asked to separate these judgements explicitly rather than letting good execution launder weak contribution, or letting weak contribution obscure that the expansion path is clear and achievable.
3. **The §3.1/§3.3 internal contradiction** (duplicate removal under stated anonymity) is the single hardest factual problem in the manuscript and is assigned to Reviewer 1. No other reviewer should duplicate it.

### Complementarity and tension between reviewers

- **R1 ↔ R4 boundary is deliberately drawn**: R1 owns the *psychometric properties* of the instruments as administered (reliability, method variance, attenuation, sampling). R4 owns the *choice of construct and data source* (frequency as a proxy, trace data as the foregone alternative, governance). Both touch self-report; they must not be allowed to converge into the same comment. If R4's report drifts into instrument reliability, the synthesizer should suppress it in favour of R1's.
- **Expected direct conflict — R2 vs the author's stated scope**: R2 will push toward a fuller acceptance model (TAM2/UTAUT with mediators), which the paper explicitly and reasonably declined to test. The synthesizer must not let "you should have tested a full model" become a required revision; the legitimate version of R2's point is the missing foundational citations and the missing effect-size benchmark, both of which are fixable without changing the design.
- **Expected direct conflict — R4 vs R2 on data source**: R2 comes from a tradition built almost entirely on self-report and will find the design normal; R4 will find it indefensible. This is a genuine disciplinary disagreement, not a resolvable one, and the synthesizer should surface it to the author as a positioning choice (acceptance research vs learning analytics framing) rather than adjudicating it.
- **EIC and R2 will converge on the reference base.** Deduplicate at synthesis: the EIC owns the integrity/resolvability question, R2 owns the substantive lineage gap.
- **No reviewer is assigned to language or presentation.** The prose is clean and this would be a low-yield review axis.
