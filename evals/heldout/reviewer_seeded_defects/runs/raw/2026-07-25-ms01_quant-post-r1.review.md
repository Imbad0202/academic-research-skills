### Internal inconsistencies the panel must be anchored to (not a review verdict — a factual inventory for reviewer configuration)

1. **Correlation magnitude conflict**: Abstract reports *r* = .42; §4.2 reports *r* = .24, *p* = .004.
2. **Degrees of freedom vs. sample size (perceived control)**: §4.3 reports *t*(156) = 3.02, implying *N* ≈ 158, but §4.1 states only 87 respondents answered the perceived-control item.
3. **Test statistic vs. *p*-value (final exam)**: §4.3 reports *t*(140) = 1.31, *p* = .008. A *t* of 1.31 on 140 *df* corresponds to *p* ≈ .19. The surrounding prose ("did not reach a comparable level," "the difference was small") also contradicts the stated *p* = .008.
4. **Table 2 vs. text**: §4.3 states all 142 students were classified into engagement groups and reports *df* = 140 (implying *N* = 142), but Table 2 reports *n* = 66 + 61 = 127.
5. **Final exam score vs. retention coding**: Table 1 reports final exam scores across the analytic sample, yet §3.3 codes students who "did not sit the final" as not retained — non-retained students cannot have final exam scores. The retention base rate (how many of the 142 were retained) is never reported.
6. **Sampling description self-contradiction**: §3.2 first states a "random sample of students enrolled in the course section," then describes a mid-term LMS announcement to which students self-selected. These are incompatible; the design is a volunteer sample.
7. **Abstract vs. Methods measure mismatch**: The abstract claims the study measured "self-regulated learning behavior"; §3.3 operationalizes only dashboard sessions, dichotomous retention, and a single perceived-control item.
8. **Citation–source mismatch**: §2 cites Ferro & Nakamura (2021) for the claim that dashboards "have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," but that reference is titled *"When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student."* The in-text claim appears to invert the cited source.
9. **False precision**: Perceived control reported as *M* = 3.847 from a 1–5 integer single-item scale (*n* = 87).
10. **Causal claim escalation**: §1 explicitly promises to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story," and §2 approvingly cites Ibarra (2023) on causal language outrunning evidence. §5 then states dashboard engagement "improved course retention" and "raises the probability that a student completes the course"; §6 calls dashboard investment a "dependable strategy" and a "generalizable lever" for "higher education institutions worldwide."
11. **Ethics gap**: §3.2 states "Students were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study." No IRB/ethics approval statement appears anywhere.
12. **Survivorship structure**: Recruitment occurred midway through the term, so students who had already withdrawn could not enter the sample — the outcome variable is partially conditioned on the sampling mechanism.

## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Journal of Learning Analytics (SoLAR)** — The closest topical home: student-facing dashboards, LMS trace data, and the field's own methodological self-critique are core to its remit, and it does publish correlational work when scope is stated honestly. It is also the venue most likely to demand exactly what this manuscript lacks: a defensible engagement construct beyond click counts, reported effect sizes, and claims bounded to association. Recommended as primary target *conditional on* the causal language being retracted and the statistics reconciled.
2. **British Journal of Educational Technology (BJET)** — Strong fit for dashboard deployment studies with a higher-education audience and an appetite for institutional implications. BJET's reviewers routinely require multivariable analysis and a clearly described intervention artifact; the manuscript currently describes the dashboard interface only in one sentence, which would block replication. Realistic only after substantial methodological strengthening.
3. **Australasian Journal of Educational Technology** (alternate: *Journal of Computing in Higher Education*) — The realistic outlet for a single-course, single-term observational study *if* the authors scale the contribution down to a well-bounded local-context report with transparent limitations. Recommended as the fallback tier rather than a lesser ambition: it is where this evidence base actually sits.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

**Cross-disciplinary coverage strategy.** This manuscript sits across four areas — learning analytics, quantitative educational measurement, educational psychology (SRL/goal orientation), and data ethics/governance. R2 owns the core discipline (learning analytics + higher-education persistence) and absorbs the SRL theory question, since the paper's SRL framing is a literature-and-theory problem rather than a separate psychological-measurement problem. R1 owns measurement and inference. R3 covers ethics, governance, and interface/deployment — the areas that are entirely unaddressed by the manuscript and that no other reviewer is configured to reach.

**Special characteristics requiring particular attention:**

1. **Surface polish masks internal inconsistency — this is the panel's principal calibration risk.** The prose is fluent, the structure is complete, the limitations section is present and self-aware, and the references are formatted with DOIs. A reviewer reading for quality of argument will likely rate this manuscript far higher than a reviewer reading for consistency of numbers. Instruct every reviewer to check the arithmetic in §4 against §3.2 and §4.1 before forming an overall impression, not after.

2. **The manuscript pre-empts its own criticisms without acting on them.** §2 cites Ibarra (2023) on causal overreach; §2 cites Vandermeer (2023) on click-count proxies and explicitly concedes "including the present one"; §3.4 concedes the median split is "a coarse simplification"; §1 promises to distinguish pattern from cause. Then §5 asserts that engagement "improved" retention and §6 calls it a "dependable" and "generalizable" lever. Reviewers should be warned that self-aware hedging in the Literature Review and Limitations does not discharge the claims made in the Discussion, Conclusion, and Abstract — and should evaluate the strongest claim the paper makes anywhere, not its most careful one.

3. **Two of the three headline results may not exist as reported.** The perceived-control comparison (*t*(156)) cannot be computed from 87 respondents, and the exam comparison pairs *t*(140) = 1.31 with *p* = .008 while the prose describes it as non-significant. If R1's audit confirms these, the panel is not reviewing a study with modest findings but a study whose reported findings cannot be reconstructed from its own Methods. This distinction should govern the recommendation category, and the synthesizer should hold the verdict open until R1 reports.

4. **Reference verifiability is an open question, not an assumption.** All 15 references carry `10.5555/` DOIs with sequential-looking suffixes and distribute across only four journal titles, and 9 of the 15 are never cited in text. Reviewers should flag the citation-integrity question explicitly rather than assume the reference list is sound; R2 in particular should report the Ferro & Nakamura title-versus-claim mismatch as a distinct finding from general literature thinness.

5. **Register.** Standard rigorous peer-review register, not developmental — the manuscript is a competent revised draft, not a first draft, and the authors are plainly capable of executing what they attempt. Tone should stay constructive and specific, but per the register rule, tone adjusts wording only: the recommendation must follow the evidence against the criteria regardless of how well written the manuscript is.

**Anticipated complementarity and tension between reviewers:**

- **R1 ↔ R2 (complementary, low overlap).** R1 audits numbers; R2 audits sources and theory. Both are integrity questions about whether the manuscript's assertions match its underlying material, arriving from opposite directions. Expect these two to converge on the same overall severity by independent routes, which strengthens the finding.
- **R1 ↔ R3 (productive tension).** R1 will likely frame the mid-term recruitment window as a selection-bias threat to estimate validity; R3 will frame the same fact as evidence that dashboard-induced withdrawal is unobservable by design. These are the same structural feature read as a statistics problem and as an ethics problem. The synthesizer should present both readings rather than collapsing them, because the ethical reading survives even if the estimate is later corrected.
- **EIC ↔ R2 (possible tension on disposition).** The EIC may find the topic well-suited and the writing publishable and lean toward major revision on framing grounds; R2 may find the citation inversion and the 9 uncited references disqualifying on integrity grounds. Surface this disagreement explicitly rather than averaging it.
- **R3 ↔ EIC/R2 (deliberate non-overlap).** No other reviewer is configured to raise consent, IRB, data governance, or deployment cost. If R3's findings do not appear in the synthesis, the panel will have silently endorsed a recommendation for worldwide institutional deployment of a system whose study analyzed students' behavior without informing them.
- **Shared blind spot across all four — assign to the synthesizer.** None of the four cards is configured to press hard on **reverse causality and third-variable confounding** as the primary explanation: students who intend to persist, or who are more conscientious, both use the dashboard more and complete the course. R1 will likely note it under selection bias; R2 under limitations. The synthesizer should ensure it is treated as a first-order alternative explanation for the paper's central claim, not as a footnote in §5.1.

## Contract Paraphrase

**D1 — methodology_rigor.** From the editorial chair, this dimension asks whether the study's design and data handling would survive the scrutiny of the referees I would assign it to, without my having to adjudicate technical minutiae myself. For a deployment study in learning analytics, the editorially salient question is whether the design supports the inferential weight the paper puts on it: whether a retention outcome measured alongside voluntary dashboard use is presented as association or as effect, whether the analytic units, time window, and sample are stated precisely enough that a reader could tell what was compared to what, and whether the reporting includes the quantities (denominators, effect sizes, uncertainty, missingness) that a reader needs in order to judge rather than merely accept. I am not re-running the analysis; I am asking whether the methodological surface is complete and honest enough that the paper's conclusions are auditable by my readership. Reproducibility affordances — instrument description, data availability, enough operational detail to replicate the deployment — count here because my journal's readers are people who might attempt the same thing at their own institution.

**D2 — domain_accuracy.** This is the question of whether the paper is a competent citizen of its literature. Learning analytics and the retention/persistence literature in higher education both have long, well-populated bodies of prior work, and the editorial risk is a paper that re-derives a known result while presenting it as new, or that misrepresents what prior work established. I look for whether the constructs are used the way the field uses them — engagement, retention, persistence, and course completion are not interchangeable, and a gateway-course context has its own established findings about attrition. I also check whether cited work is characterised accurately rather than recruited as generic support, and whether the paper's factual claims about tools, platforms, institutional context, and prior effect magnitudes are correct. Getting the domain wrong is not a stylistic problem for me; it is the fastest route to a published paper that the field will have to correct.

**D3 — argumentative_coherence.** My most characteristic editorial lens: does the paper hold together from title to conclusion? The chain runs research question → what was actually measured → what the results license → what the paper concludes → what the title and abstract promised. Coherence fails when a link in that chain is silently widened, most often when a correlational engagement–retention finding is narrated in causal or prescriptive language by the discussion, or when the conclusion answers a broader question than the one the study posed. I also treat internal contradiction — abstract claiming more than results, limitations section quietly conceding something the conclusion ignores — as a coherence defect rather than a writing defect, because it changes what the reader takes away. This dimension is where over-promising and under-delivering gets caught.

**D4 — cross_disciplinary_relevance.** This paper sits at an intersection: learning analytics as a technical/educational-technology field, and higher education research on retention as an institutional and policy field. My readership spans both, plus practitioners who run these deployments. So the dimension asks whether an adjacent-field reader can follow the paper without insider knowledge — whether platform-specific terms and engagement metrics are defined rather than assumed, whether the institutional context (what a gateway course is, what retention means at this institution, what the local baseline is) is legible to someone outside that system, and whether any claim of interdisciplinary reach is actually substantiated rather than asserted in the final paragraph. A single-site deployment study can be highly relevant to other settings, but only if it gives outsiders the anchors needed to judge transfer. This dimension is high priority, not mandatory, which is right: failing it caps the paper's reach without necessarily invalidating it.

**D5 — writing_and_structure.** The presentational floor. Is the manuscript organised so a reader can find the question, the method, the result, and the limits; are tables and figures self-supporting and consistent with the text; does the paper meet venue conventions for an empirical report? The 2,487-word count matters here as an editorial fact: this is a short-format submission, so I judge structure against short-format expectations — every section must earn its space, and the risk is not verbosity but omission of load-bearing detail. I hold this at normal priority and refuse to inflate it: prose I find graceless is not a defect, but structure that hides what the study did is.

## Scoring Plan

### D1: methodology_rigor
- `what_to_look_for`: An explicit statement of design (observational deployment, quasi-experiment, or otherwise) and of what the comparison is; definitions of the dashboard-engagement measure and the retention outcome with their measurement windows; sample size, cohort definition, and the denominator behind every rate; whether self-selection into dashboard use is acknowledged and how it is handled; statistical reporting that includes effect magnitude and uncertainty, not only significance; treatment of missing data and of students who left mid-term; any ethics/IRB and data-governance statement appropriate to student-level records; enough deployment detail (what the dashboard showed, to whom, when) that another institution could reproduce the intervention.
- `what_triggers_block`: The paper's central retention claim rests on an analysis whose design cannot support it and the gap is not disclosed — e.g., causal or effectiveness language for a comparison of self-selected dashboard users against non-users with no adjustment, no comparison group, or no acknowledgement of selection; OR the core quantitative result is unauditable because sample size, denominators, or the outcome definition are absent; OR the analysis contains a stated procedure that is invalid on its face for the data described.
- `what_triggers_warn`: The design is defensible and correctly labelled, but the reporting is thin in ways that limit independent judgement — missing effect sizes or confidence intervals, unreported attrition or missingness, an engagement metric defined only loosely (e.g., "active users" without a threshold), absent ethics/data-access statement, or reproducibility detail compressed below what a short-format empirical report still owes its readers.

### D2: domain_accuracy
- `what_to_look_for`: Correct and consistent use of the field's constructs — engagement, retention, persistence, completion, gateway course — with the paper's own operational definitions matching the terms it borrows; accurate representation of the learning-analytics literature on dashboards and of the higher-education retention literature it invokes; whether prior findings are cited with the right direction and magnitude; whether the stated contribution is actually novel against well-known prior deployments rather than a restatement; correctness of factual assertions about platforms, metrics, and institutional mechanics.
- `what_triggers_block`: A load-bearing claim is factually wrong about the domain — prior work misrepresented in a way that manufactures the paper's gap, a construct used with a meaning that inverts the field's (e.g., retention outcomes reported as persistence or vice versa in a way that changes the finding), or a central result presented as new when it is an established finding the paper does not engage.
- `what_triggers_warn`: Domain handling is broadly sound but locally imprecise — terminology used loosely or drifting between sections, citations that support a weaker claim than the sentence they are attached to, a literature framing that omits an obvious relevant strand (e.g., the known selection critique of dashboard-engagement studies), or novelty asserted without positioning against the nearest prior work.

### D3: argumentative_coherence
- `what_to_look_for`: A single, findable research question, and a conclusion that answers that question and no larger one; consistency of the claim's strength across title, abstract, results, discussion, and conclusion; whether the discussion's inferential verbs match the design (associated with vs. increases vs. should be adopted); whether stated limitations are actually honoured by the conclusions rather than parked; whether recommendations to institutions are traceable to something the study measured; absence of arguments that assume what they set out to show (engagement as both proxy for and cause of persistence).
- `what_triggers_block`: The central argument breaks — the conclusion or title asserts a causal, prescriptive, or generalised claim the study's own results cannot license and the gap is never acknowledged; OR the paper contradicts itself on its headline finding across sections; OR the stated research question and the reported analysis are not about the same thing.
- `what_triggers_warn`: The spine holds but slips in places — occasional causal phrasing in an otherwise correlational account, a discussion that reaches modestly beyond the evidence before pulling back, limitations acknowledged but not reflected in the abstract's confidence, or a conclusion that restates results without closing the loop on the question posed.

### D4: cross_disciplinary_relevance
- `what_to_look_for`: Definitions supplied for platform-specific and analytics-specific terms at first use; the institutional context made legible to a reader outside that system (what the gateway course is, its size and role, the local retention baseline, the policy stakes); implications addressed to identifiable adjacent audiences — institutional researchers, higher-education administrators, instructional designers — rather than to "stakeholders" generically; any claim of transferability accompanied by the contextual parameters a different institution would need to judge fit; framing that a higher-education researcher without learning-analytics training can enter.
- `what_triggers_block`: The paper is unreadable to its adjacent-field audience on the points that matter — the core engagement metric or the outcome is never defined in field-neutral terms, or the paper makes explicit interdisciplinary/generalisation claims (this transfers to other institutions, courses, or sectors) with no substantiation and no contextual anchors at all.
- `what_triggers_warn`: The paper is followable but insular — undefined jargon or platform-internal metrics passing without gloss, a single-site context described too thinly for outside readers to assess transfer, or implications stated at a generic level that neither adjacent field could act on.

### D5: writing_and_structure
- `what_to_look_for`: Recognisable and complete sections for an empirical short report (question, method, results, discussion, limitations), each proportionate to a 2,487-word budget; tables and figures that are self-explanatory, labelled, and numerically consistent with the text; consistent reporting of numbers between abstract, results, and any exhibit; a title and abstract that describe what the paper actually contains; reference list complete and consistent; venue conventions for an empirical article observed.
- `what_triggers_block`: Structure defeats comprehension — a load-bearing component of an empirical report is missing entirely (no method or no results section in substance), or exhibits contradict the text on the headline numbers such that a reader cannot tell what the finding is.
- `what_triggers_warn`: The manuscript is navigable but presentationally deficient — disproportionate sections given the word budget (long framing, compressed results), figures or tables lacking labels, units, or denominators, minor numeric inconsistencies between text and exhibits, an abstract that under-describes the actual content, or reference/format irregularities against venue conventions.

I review at the level of fit, contribution, claim integrity, and whether my readership would be served or misled — technical adjudication of the statistics belongs to the methodology referee.

### Overall Recommendation

### Confidence Score

**4** — High confidence on journal fit, contribution, claim integrity, and the abstract-versus-body audit, which are my seat's core competencies. ### Summary Assessment

This manuscript reports an observational, single-course study of 142 undergraduates in an introductory statistics course, relating LMS-logged dashboard session counts to dichotomous course completion, with a single-item perceived-control measure as a secondary strand. The topic sits squarely in this journal's remit, the literature review is unusually well-informed about its own field's failure modes, and the prose is clean. That surface is precisely what makes the manuscript hazardous. The abstract reports a correlation of .42 that the Results section reports as .24; the abstract claims measurement of "self-regulated learning behavior" that the Methods never operationalize; a load-bearing literature claim asserts the opposite of what its cited source's own title reports; the Methods give two incompatible accounts of how the sample came to exist; and the reported test statistics cannot be reconstructed from the described sample. The Introduction promises to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story," and the Discussion and Conclusion then assert that engagement "improved" retention, "raises the probability" of completion, and constitutes a "dependable" and "generalizable lever" for institutions "worldwide." The contribution, as executed, is one more single-site correlation added to the stock of work the paper itself diagnoses as inadequate. I could not forward this to referees for revision in good conscience.

### Strengths

1. **The literature review is genuinely well-oriented to the field's own methodological self-critique**: §2 names the click-proxy problem, the demotivation risk, and the causal-overreach critique, and applies the first of these to itself. **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §2 — "Most dashboard studies, including the present one, infer engagement from coarse behavioral proxies such as page views or session counts"`] | **Confidence**: 5 — this is the literature I edit for.

2. **The engagement measure is operationally specified at a level that would permit metric-level replication**: the sessionization rule is stated concretely rather than left to "active users." **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §3.3 — "A session was defined as a dashboard view preceded by at least thirty minutes of inactivity"`] | **Confidence**: 4 — standard trace-data practice in this journal's submissions.

3. **Local methodological candor about the median split**: the authors name the analytic cost of their own simplification rather than presenting it as a design choice without consequence. **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §3.3 — "This median split is a coarse simplification of a continuous measure and was adopted for interpretability rather than statistical efficiency"`] | **Confidence**: 4 — evaluable from the text alone.

4. **The Discussion's third paragraph correctly calibrates the magnitude**: §5 states that the association "was reliable but not large" and that dashboards may "help at the margin." This is the one place where the manuscript's claim strength matches its evidence, and it demonstrates that the authors are capable of the register the rest of the paper abandons. **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §5 — "the modest size of the engagement-retention association counsels against overstatement"`] | **Confidence**: 4 — assessed against the reported effect.

### Weaknesses

1. **The abstract misreports the headline finding and claims a measure the study never took**: the abstract states *r* = .42; §4.2 states *r* = .24. The abstract also states that the study "measured dashboard engagement, self-regulated learning behavior, and course persistence," but §3.3 operationalizes only dashboard sessions, dichotomous retention, and a single perceived-control item. A reader who reads only the abstract — which is most readers, and every reader of an indexing service — is materially misled about both the size of the effect and what was measured. *Improvement direction*: the abstract must report the quantity the Results report, and must name only measures the Methods define.
   - **Severity**: Critical | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: Abstract — "Dashboard engagement correlated positively with retention (r = .42)" vs. §4.2 — "(r = .24, p = .004)"`] | **Confidence**: 5 — direct comparison of two stated numbers; no expertise required beyond reading both.

2. **A load-bearing literature claim asserts the opposite of what its cited source reports**: §2 states that "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students... (Ferro & Nakamura, 2021)," while that reference is titled *"When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student."* §2 declares this claim load-bearing ("This position underpins much of the equity-oriented rationale... and we return to it in the Discussion"), and §5 does return to it. The manuscript also contradicts itself internally: the very next paragraph of §2 reports, via Osei (2020), that relative-standing feedback discourages struggling students. *Improvement direction*: recheck the claim against the source and rebuild the equity argument in §5 on whatever the source actually establishes.
   - **Severity**: Critical | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §2 claim sentence vs. §References, Ferro & Nakamura (2021) title`] | **Confidence**: 4 — I am comparing the in-text claim to the reference-list title, not to the full source text; the internal contradiction with the Osei paragraph is independent of that check.

3. **Causal escalation across the paper, against the paper's own stated commitment**: §1 promises to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story," and §2 approvingly cites Ibarra (2023) on causal language outrunning evidence. §5 then opens with "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and asserts that "increasing dashboard engagement therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course." §6 escalates further. The §5.1 Limitations never mention causality, selection, or reverse causation. The most obvious alternative account — students who intend to persist, or who are more conscientious, both use the dashboard more and complete the course — is nowhere considered, and it is a first-order rival explanation for the entire finding, not a footnote. *Improvement direction*: every inferential verb in §5 and §6 must be reduced to association, and the reverse-causality and third-variable accounts must be treated as competing explanations in the Discussion body.
   - **Severity**: Critical | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §5 — "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and "raises the probability that a student completes the course"`] | **Confidence**: 5 — the mismatch between design label and inferential verb is my seat's core competency.

4. **The reported test statistics cannot be reconstructed from the described sample**: §4.3 reports *t*(156) = 3.02 for a perceived-control comparison, but §4.1 states that only 87 respondents answered that item. §4.3 reports *t*(140) = 1.31 with *p* = .008, while the surrounding prose describes the same difference as small and as not reaching "a comparable level." §4.3 states that "All 142 students in the primary analytic sample were classified into engagement groups," while Table 2 reports 66 + 61 = 127. I do not adjudicate the correct values; I record that two of the paper's three inferential results cannot be tied to a sample the Methods describe, which means those results are not evaluable as reported. *Improvement direction*: recompute and report every statistic against a stated analytic *n*, and reconcile every test with its accompanying prose.
   - **Severity**: Critical | **Evidence Anchor**: [`table: Table 2 (n = 66 + 61 = 127) vs. §4.3 text ("All 142 students... were classified") and df = 140`] | **Confidence**: 4 — arithmetic and internal consistency are checkable at my altitude; the full statistical audit is the methodology referee's.

5. **The Methods give two incompatible accounts of how the sample came to exist, and the consequence is never disclosed**: §3.2 first states that "Participants were drawn from the course enrollment using a random sample," then describes a mid-term LMS announcement to which students "chose to respond." These cannot both be true; the design is a volunteer sample. Because recruitment occurred mid-term, students who had already withdrawn were structurally ineligible, so the sampling window is conditioned on partial survival of the outcome variable. §5.1 does not mention selection at all. Compounding this, the retention base rate — how many of the 142 were retained — is never reported anywhere in the paper, so the reader cannot see the denominator behind the study's central outcome. *Improvement direction*: state the design honestly as a volunteer sample, report the response rate against the enrolled population and the retention base rate, and treat the survivorship structure as a threat to the finding rather than an unstated feature.
   - **Severity**: Critical | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §3.2 — "using a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" vs. "Students who chose to respond, and who consented to the survey, formed the study sample"`] | **Confidence**: 5 — the contradiction is on the page.

6. **No ethics approval statement, and the Methods disclose that behavioral data were analyzed without informing students**: §3.2 states plainly that "Students were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study," while the survey component *did* obtain consent — indicating the authors distinguished the two channels and proceeded anyway. There is no IRB or ethics-approval statement, no data-availability statement, no funding disclosure, and no conflict-of-interest statement anywhere in the manuscript. This is a desk-level gate at this journal independent of everything else in this report, and it is not remediable by rewriting, because the data were already collected under those conditions. *Improvement direction*: supply the ethics approval and its protocol number, state the legal/institutional basis for the secondary use of trace data, and add the missing declarations; if no approval exists, that must be disclosed to the editor before any further consideration.
   - **Severity**: Critical | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §3.2 — "Students were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study"`] | **Confidence**: 4 — journal ethics policy is squarely my responsibility; the governance analysis proper belongs to the ethics referee.

7. **The Conclusion's prescriptive scope is unrelated to the evidence base**: §6 tells "higher education institutions worldwide" that investing in dashboards is "a dependable strategy for improving retention across programs and disciplines" and a "practical and generalizable lever," on the basis of a modest correlation among volunteers in one section of one introductory statistics course at one institution. The recommendation is also unactionable: the dashboard artifact is described in a single sentence (§3.1), with no cost, staffing, integration, or maintenance considerations, and §5.1 concedes that "the particular interface features may shape how students respond." A practitioner reading §6 cannot implement anything, and "encourage students to engage with them" is not a coherent institutional lever when engagement is a click-count proxy the authors themselves call inadequate. *Improvement direction*: bound the conclusion to the studied context, and either specify the artifact at implementable detail or drop the deployment recommendation.
   - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §6 — "For higher education institutions worldwide, the implication is clear"`] | **Confidence**: 5 — calibration of claim to evidence base is the editorial decision I make most often.

8. **The contribution beyond the existing correlational stock is not established**: §2 correctly observes that "Much dashboard research relies on cross-sectional or correlational designs," and the study then supplies one more cross-sectional correlation without a design feature, a measure, or an analysis that advances past that stock. §5 positions the paper within the "who benefits from dashboards" debate, but the study never disaggregates by achievement level — the central axis of that debate — despite having final exam scores in hand. As it stands the paper instantiates the problem it diagnoses. *Improvement direction*: either state the contribution as a bounded local-context report, or disaggregate by prior achievement so the paper speaks to the debate it invokes.
   - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: [`absence: §5 and §4 — no analysis stratified by achievement level appears in Results or Discussion, despite final exam scores being reported in Table 1 and Table 2`] | **Confidence**: 5 — I assess contribution against this literature routinely.

9. **The reference apparatus does not meet submission standards and raises a verifiability question I cannot resolve**: 15 entries appear, of which 6 are cited in text; 9 are never cited. All 15 are drawn from four journal titles, and every DOI shares a `10.5555/` prefix with sequential-looking suffixes. I raise this as an open question requiring author response rather than an allegation. Substantively, canonical dashboard reviews and the established gateway-course persistence scholarship are absent from a paper whose framing depends on both. *Improvement direction*: cite or remove every listed reference, verify each DOI resolves, and engage the persistence literature the retention claim relies on.
   - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §References — 15 entries listed; in-text citations appear only for Calloway, Ferro & Nakamura, Osei, Rutledge & Berange, Vandermeer, and Ibarra`] | **Confidence**: 3 — the citation count and DOI pattern are verifiable from the manuscript; the resolvability of the DOIs is not, from the text alone.

10. **"Retention" drifts from a course-level operationalization to an institutional claim**: §3.3 codes retention as remaining enrolled and sitting the final assessment within one 15-week course. §6 concludes about retention "across programs and disciplines" for institutions "worldwide." In this field, course completion and institutional persistence are distinct constructs with distinct literatures; the Conclusion's scope depends on treating them as one. *Improvement direction*: use "course completion" where that is what was measured, and reserve "retention" for the institutional construct the study did not observe.
    - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §3.3 — "Course retention was coded dichotomously as whether the student remained enrolled and completed the final assessment" vs. §6 — "improving retention across programs and disciplines"`] | **Confidence**: 5 — construct discipline in the persistence literature is core to this journal's remit.

11. **Table 1 cannot be read consistently with the Methods, and the study's outcome has no reported denominator**: Table 1 reports final exam scores as a descriptive for the primary measures, while §3.3 codes students who "did not sit the final" as not retained — meaning non-retained students have no final exam score, and the table's population is therefore not the analytic sample the text describes. No retention rate is stated anywhere. *Improvement direction*: state the *n* underlying each row of Table 1 and report the retention base rate in §4.1.
    - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: [`table: Table 1, "Final exam score (0-100)" row, reported without an accompanying n, against §3.3's retention coding rule`] | **Confidence**: 4 — the inconsistency is legible from the table and the Methods together.

12. **Self-regulated learning is invoked as a frame but does no theoretical work**: §1, §2, and §5 all lean on SRL, yet no specific model, phase structure, or theorist is named, and the manuscript never states which SRL construct the single perceived-control item is meant to operationalize. A one-item overall rating with no reliability or validity evidence is asked to stand in for a regulatory construct across three sections. *Improvement direction*: name the model and its phases, state which construct the item indexes, and either justify the single item against that construct or present it as a descriptive adjunct rather than as SRL evidence.
    - **Severity**: Major | **Evidence Anchor**: [`text: §1 — "self-regulated learning models in which learners cycle through phases of forethought, performance, and reflection" (no model or source named)`] | **Confidence**: 4 — sufficient for the editorial judgment that the frame is decorative; the measurement critique proper belongs to the domain referee.

### Detailed Comments

#### Journal Fit

Topically this is a close fit and I want to be clear about that: student-facing dashboards, LMS trace data, retention outcomes, and the field's methodological self-scrutiny are all core to this journal's remit, and we do publish correlational work when its scope is stated honestly. The fit failure is not topical but evidentiary and ethical. This journal has spent years arguing that learning analytics must not overclaim; publishing a paper that tells institutions worldwide that dashboards are a "dependable" retention lever, on a *r* = .24 among volunteers in one course, would contradict the position the journal exists to advance. Length and article type are appropriate. If the authors choose not to undertake the re-analysis this would require, the *Australasian Journal of Educational Technology* or the *Journal of Computing in Higher Education* would be reasonable homes for a scaled-down, well-bounded local-context report — but only after the citation, statistical, and ethics issues are resolved, which are venue-independent.

#### Originality

The core association — students who use a dashboard more complete their courses at higher rates — is among the most-replicated observations in this literature, and the manuscript's own §2 says so. Originality would have to come from the design, the measure, or the analysis. The design is cross-sectional with no adjustment; the measure is the click-count proxy the paper itself calls inadequate; the analysis is bivariate. The one place where a genuine contribution was available — disaggregating by achievement level to speak to the "who benefits" debate the Discussion enters — is not taken, despite exam scores being in hand. I cannot identify what a reader of this journal would learn here that they did not already know.

#### Significance

If the finding held exactly as reported, its significance would be modest and local, which the authors themselves acknowledge in §5's third paragraph. But the finding as reported cannot be relied on: the headline correlation appears at two different values, two of three inferential tests do not reconcile with their stated samples, and the outcome has no reported base rate. Significance is therefore not assessable. The manuscript's *potential* significance is real — a well-executed institutional deployment study with proper adjustment and a defensible engagement construct is exactly what this field lacks — which is why I would want to see this study return rather than disappear.

#### Structural Coherence

This is where the manuscript fails most visibly at my altitude. The chain from title to conclusion breaks at three separate joints. The title says "Evidence from an Undergraduate Learning Analytics Deployment," which is fair. The abstract then asserts a correlation the Results do not report and a measure the Methods do not define. The Introduction commits explicitly to separating pattern from cause; the Discussion's opening sentence violates that commitment; the Limitations section does not notice. The Conclusion generalizes to a population the study never sampled. Notably, the manuscript pre-empts nearly every criticism I have raised — the click-proxy problem, the causal-overreach critique, the coarseness of the median split — and then proceeds as though naming a problem discharged it. Self-aware hedging in the Literature Review does not license the claims made in the Discussion and Conclusion, and I evaluate the strongest claim a paper makes, not its most careful one.

#### Title & Abstract

The title is accurate and appropriately bounded — it says "dashboard engagement and course retention," which is what the study is about, and it does not promise causation. The abstract is the manuscript's most serious presentational defect. It carries a correlation coefficient nearly double the one in the Results, names a construct the study did not measure, and closes by calling dashboard engagement "a promising lever for improving retention," which converts an association into an intervention target in a 127-word summary that will circulate far more widely than the paper. For a reader who reads only the abstract — the majority — this is not a rounding error in emphasis; it is a different study.

#### Conclusion

The Conclusion does not answer the research question the Introduction posed; it answers a larger one. §1 asks "whether students who engage more with a learning analytics dashboard are more likely to persist in and complete their course" — a well-formed associational question the study is designed to address. §6 answers a question about whether institutions worldwide should invest in dashboards, which the study cannot address at all, and it does so with the words "dependable" and "generalizable." It also introduces "and raises" as a causal verb in its first sentence, directly contradicting §1's methodological promise. This is the single paragraph I would ask the authors to rewrite first, because it is the paragraph a practitioner will act on.

### Questions for Authors

1. Which correlation is correct, .42 or .24, and how did the other value come to appear in the manuscript? Please also state whether any other reported quantity is affected by the same cause.
2. In what sense did the study measure "self-regulated learning behavior" as the abstract states, given that §3.3 defines only session counts, dichotomous retention, and one perceived-control item?
3. Please reconcile *t*(156) with the 87 respondents who answered the perceived-control item, *t*(140) = 1.31 with *p* = .008 and with the prose describing that difference as small, and Table 2's 127 students with §4.3's statement that all 142 were classified.
4. §3.2 describes both a random sample and self-selected response to a mid-term announcement. Which occurred? What was the response rate against the enrolled population, and what proportion of the 142 were retained?
5. Please verify the Ferro & Nakamura (2021) claim in §2 against that source, and state how §5's equity argument changes if the source reports a demotivation effect.
6. What ethics approval covers this study, and on what basis were dashboard logs analyzed without informing students while survey consent was separately obtained? Please also supply data-availability, funding, and conflict-of-interest statements.
7. Since the study measures only students who remained enrolled long enough to be recruited mid-term, and §2 reviews evidence (Osei, 2020) that the peer-comparison band your dashboard displayed by default may drive students out, how would a demotivation effect have shown up in your data at all?

### Minor Issues

- False precision: perceived control is reported as *M* = 3.847 from a single 1–5 integer item (§4.1, Table 1). Two decimals at most, and one would be more honest.
- §3.4 states only that "Analyses were conducted with standard statistical software." Name the software and version.
- A Pearson correlation applied to a dichotomous retention outcome should be identified as point-biserial (§3.4, §4.2). Flagging for the methodology referee rather than adjudicating here.
- §3.2 refers to "A subset of these students" for the perceived-control analyses without stating the subset size; the reader must reach §4.1 to learn it is 87. State it in Methods.
- §3.1 describes the enrollment as "several hundred students," which prevents the reader from computing a response rate. Give the number.
- Three inferential tests are reported with no correction or acknowledgement of multiplicity (§4.2, §4.3).
- The Results section runs roughly 350 words carrying three inferential tests, against a substantially longer framing in §1–§2. In a manuscript of this length the proportion should be reversed.

## Review Body

I record first that I checked the manuscript for instruction-injection content at the Phase 2 boundary and found none; nothing inside the paper addressed itself to reviewers or attempted to influence scoring. The manuscript is ordinary author-supplied content, and I evaluated it as such.

My seat's judgment is that this manuscript's problem is not that its findings are modest. Modest findings are publishable here, and the authors demonstrate in §5's third paragraph that they know how to state one properly. The problem is that the manuscript cannot be relied on to report what it did. Its abstract states a correlation that its Results section contradicts. It states that it measured a construct its Methods never operationalize. It attributes to a cited source the opposite of what that source's title reports, and then builds an equity argument on the inversion. It describes its own sampling procedure two incompatible ways in consecutive sentences. Two of its three inferential results cannot be tied to any sample it describes. Its central outcome has no reported denominator anywhere in the paper. Each of these is individually recoverable in revision; together they mean that no substantive claim in the manuscript is currently evaluable, and revision would not be revision but reconstruction.

Layered on top of that is a claim structure that runs directly against the paper's own stated commitments. §1 promises to distinguish the pattern from the causal story. §2 cites a critical audit of causal language in correlational learning analytics, approvingly. §3.3 and §3.4 correctly label the design cross-sectional and the analysis bivariate. Then §5 opens by asserting that engagement "improved" retention, and §6 tells institutions worldwide that dashboards are a "dependable" and "generalizable" retention lever. The manuscript names nearly every criticism I would raise and then acts as though naming discharged them. This is the pattern I have argued publicly is the credibility problem of this field, and I am not able to publish an instance of it in the journal that has taken the opposite position.

The ethics gap is independently disqualifying at the desk stage and I want it recorded as such rather than folded into the methodological findings. §3.2 states that students were not informed their dashboard activity would be analyzed, while survey consent was separately obtained — meaning the authors distinguished the two channels and proceeded. No ethics approval, data-availability, funding, or conflict-of-interest statement appears. Unlike the statistical problems, this one is not fixable by re-analysis: the data already exist under those conditions. It requires disclosure to the editor and, depending on what the disclosure reveals, may bound what can be published from this dataset at all.

I want to be equally clear about what I am not saying. The study is not without value and the authors are not incapable — the literature review is better-informed than most submissions I see, the sessionization rule is properly specified, and the candor about the median split is the mark of researchers who understand their own methods. A version of this study that reports a bounded course-level association with proper adjustment, an honest description of the volunteer sample, a stated retention base rate, disaggregation by prior achievement, and a conclusion that stops at the edge of the evidence would be a paper worth reading. That paper would also be a genuine contribution, because it would model the discipline §2 says the field lacks.

On disposition: the contract's governing condition for my scores is F1, whose action spans rejection and major revision. Within that band my seat's recommendation is rejection rather than major revision, on the ground that the required work — reconciling or recomputing every reported statistic, re-establishing what the cited literature actually says, resolving the sampling contradiction, and clarifying the ethics basis — exceeds what a revision round can verify. I would rather see this return as a new submission, where the reconstructed analysis can be reviewed on its own terms, than pass through a revision cycle that asks referees to check whether numbers now match. I record for the synthesizer that a colleague who weighs the manuscript's topical fit and the recoverability of its underlying study more heavily could land on major revision without contradicting the evidence in this report; the disagreement would be about remediation cost, not about the findings.

Where the methodology referee's audit differs from my arithmetic, theirs governs. ### Detailed Comments

#### Research Questions & Hypotheses

The research question in §1 is clear and answerable in principle: "whether students who engage more with a learning analytics dashboard are more likely to persist in and complete their course." That is an associational question, correctly phrased, and a cross-sectional design can address it. No formal hypotheses are stated, which is acceptable for a study framed this way. The problem is not the question but that §5 and §6 answer a different, interventional question that was never posed and cannot be addressed by this design.

#### Research Design

Correctly identified as observational and cross-sectional, with the temporal consequence drawn explicitly (§3.1). There is no comparison condition of any kind — no non-adopter group, no prior cohort, no other section, no pre/post contrast — and none is needed for the associational question as posed. But the absence of any comparison structure, combined with the absence of covariate adjustment, is precisely what makes the §5–§6 language untenable. A meaningful strengthening is available without new data collection: the dashboard was live from week 1 (§3.1) and logs exist for the full enrolled cohort, so a design that measures exposure over an early window and the outcome at term end, on the full cohort rather than on volunteers, would be both more defensible and larger.

#### Sampling Strategy

The frame is stated two incompatible ways within one subsection (W4). The operative description is volunteer response to a mid-term announcement. Three deficits follow: the response rate is unrecoverable against a "several hundred" denominator; no comparison of respondents to non-respondents is offered on any variable, although dashboard-session counts exist for the entire enrolled population and would make that comparison trivial; and the recruitment timing removes early withdrawers by construction, so the sample is conditioned on partial realisation of the outcome. No sample-size justification of any kind is offered. I want to be precise about what this does and does not establish: it does not show the reported association is spurious, and it does not establish a bias direction — attenuating and inflating mechanisms are both present. It establishes that the estimate applies to an unquantified, self-selected, already-persisting subpopulation, and that the paper provides no basis for extrapolating beyond it.

#### Data Collection

Log extraction is the strongest part of the Methods (W-list item 16 notwithstanding): the counting unit and sessionization rule are both stated, which is more than most trace-data studies give. The survey side is much weaker. A single global item stands in for "perceived control over learning," with no reliability evidence (impossible for a single item), no validity evidence, no source, and no statement of which construct in the invoked self-regulated-learning frame it operationalises. The justification offered — "single-item overall ratings are common in dashboard studies to limit survey burden" (§3.3) — is a prevalence claim, not a validity argument, and it is uncited. Response timing relative to the outcome is not stated.

#### Analysis Methods

The analysis is wholly bivariate: one correlation and two independent-samples *t*-tests. For a binary outcome with an observational exposure this is under-specified by a wide margin; a logistic model, or a discrete-time survival model if withdrawal timing is available, is the field-standard specification and is the minimum a resubmission would need. The stated plan (§3.4) does not describe the headline analysis, and the headline coefficient is mislabelled (W9). No assumption checking is reported anywhere, which matters most for the explicitly right-skewed predictor.

**Statistical reporting completeness (Step 4a)**: **Unacceptable.** Against the seven checklist items: (1) effect sizes — absent for all tests, and none interpreted; (2) confidence intervals — absent everywhere, including for the headline estimate; (3) statistical power — no a priori analysis, and the non-significant exam result is interpreted substantively without any Type II discussion despite ≈25% achieved power for the observed *d* = 0.23; (4) assumption testing — none reported, on a predictor the paper itself describes as right-skewed; (5) missing data — 38.7% item missingness neither quantified nor handled beyond implicit listwise deletion; (6) APA format compliance — false precision (three decimals from a 1–5 integer scale), inconsistent decimal places, no CIs, non-self-describing tables; (7) red flags — the *df*/*p* irreconcilabilities in §4.3, the abstract/results correlation divergence, an unreported median cut-point, and three uncorrected tests. I record the red flags as reporting-integrity failures requiring reconciliation against the source data; I am not in a position to distinguish transcription error from anything else, and I do not.

#### Results Presentation

Results are complete in the narrow sense that the weaker exam comparison is reported rather than dropped — that is to the authors' credit. But the presentation is not verifiable. The headline estimate (§4.2) is a bare *r* and *p* with no *n*, no CI, and no table; the retention base rate is absent; the perceived-control comparison has no group *n*s, means, or SDs anywhere; and Table 1 carries no denominators. Table 2 is the sole auditable exhibit, and auditing it is what exposed the *df* error. The selective-reporting risk here is not that unfavourable results were hidden — it is that the reader cannot check any favourable one.

#### Reproducibility

Not reproducible as reported. An independent team could reconstruct the engagement measure only approximately (platform unnamed, sessionization deferred to an unspecified default); could not reconstruct the engagement groups (cut-point unreported); could not reconstruct any reported test (sample denominators contradictory); and has no data, no code, no named software, and no ethics record to work from. The one bright spot is that Table 2 is reproducible from its own contents.

#### Methodological Fallacies Detected

Against the standing checklist:

- **Reverse causation** — present and unaddressed. Remaining enrolled is a precondition for continuing to log in; the causal arrow the Discussion asserts may run backwards, and §5.1 does not raise it.
- **Survivorship bias** — present by construction. Mid-term recruitment renders pre-recruitment withdrawers ineligible, so the most informative non-retained cases are absent from the analytic sample.
- **Endogeneity / omitted variables** — present and unaddressed. Prior achievement, conscientiousness, and intent to persist plausibly drive both the exposure and the outcome; no adjustment is attempted and no omitted-variable discussion appears.
- **Confirmation bias** — partial. The non-significant exam comparison is reported, which counts against this reading; but §5 carries the favourable result into an interventional claim while giving the unfavourable one one clause, and the misreported *p* = .008 would, at face value, have made it significant.
- **P-hacking** — not established. Three tests with no multiplicity correction is a reporting gap (FWER ≈ .14), not evidence of a search over specifications, and I do not allege one.
- **Ecological fallacy, Simpson's paradox, overfitting, multicollinearity** — not applicable. Data and inference are both at student level; no subgroup structure invites a Simpson check; no model is fitted and no predictor set exists.

I scanned the manuscript for instruction-directed content aimed at reviewers or graders. None is present: the text contains no imperatives addressed outside the paper, no scoring requests, and no reviewer-directed appeals. This check is clean, and I record it so the panel knows it was run.

## Review Body

This manuscript is well written, structurally complete, and candid about several of its own weaknesses, and that combination is exactly what makes its condition easy to miss. The methodological verdict turns on a single fact established by hand-recomputation rather than on any judgement of quality: two of the three headline inferential results cannot be produced by any analysis of the sample the Methods describe. The perceived-control comparison is reported at *t*(156) = 3.02, implying roughly 158 observations against 87 respondents to that item and a total analytic sample of 142. The exam comparison pairs *t*(140) = 1.31 with *p* = .008, an impossible pairing — *t* = 1.31 at 140 *df* gives *p* ≈ .19 — that also contradicts the surrounding prose describing the difference as small and not comparable. Table 2's own cells reproduce *t* = 1.31 exactly at *df* = 125 on *n* = 127, contradicting the text's assertion that all 142 students were classified. Independently, the abstract's *r* = .42 lies outside the approximate 95% CI of [.08, .39] around §4.2's *r* = .24, so the two are distinguishable estimates rather than roundings of one quantity, and the perceived-control mean of 3.847 cannot arise from 87 integer responses on a 1–5 scale. No substantive conclusion in §4 is presently evaluable, and that precedes every other methodological concern.

Beneath the reconciliation failures sit design problems that would need attention even after the numbers are corrected. The sampling frame is described both as random and as volunteer self-selection within one subsection; the operative description is volunteer, the response rate is unrecoverable against a "several hundred" denominator, and mid-term recruitment renders pre-recruitment withdrawers structurally ineligible, conditioning the sample on partial realisation of the outcome. I decline to assign a direction to the resulting bias: survivorship truncation attenuates a positive association while self-selection on persistence-related disposition inflates it, and the net sign is not determinable from what is reported. The analysis is wholly bivariate with no covariate adjustment, so confounding by intent-to-persist and reverse causation stand as fully viable rival explanations of the reported association; §5.1 raises neither. No effect size, confidence interval, or power statement appears anywhere, which makes the exam null uninterpretable at roughly 25% achieved power for the observed *d* = 0.23 — yet §5 interprets it substantively.

The claim language then travels well past all of this. §1 promises to distinguish pattern from cause and §2 endorses the field's causal-overreach critique; §5 states that engagement "improved" retention and "raises the probability" of completion, and §6 calls dashboards a "dependable strategy" and a "generalizable lever" for "institutions worldwide." A study cannot diagnose its field's characteristic error in the Literature Review and then commit it in the Discussion. Compounding this, the outcome is measured as course completion within one 15-week course but concluded upon as institutional retention, and §2's characterisation of one cited source appears to invert a finding it then builds on in §5.

What the authors have is real and worth rebuilding: log data on a full enrolled cohort, a live dashboard from week 1, an explicitly specified exposure measure, and institutional records that plausibly contain prior achievement. A study drawn from the full cohort rather than from volunteers, measuring exposure over an early window that precedes the outcome, fitting a logistic or discrete-time survival model with prior-achievement and baseline-activity adjustment, and reporting adjusted estimates with confidence intervals, would be both more defensible and considerably larger than what is reported here. That is a different study, not a revision of this one, which is why my narrative recommendation sits at the reject end of the contract's band. The immediate obligation is narrower and non-negotiable: reconcile every reported statistic against the analytic file and state, for each, the sample it was computed on.

- **Research gap argument**: Not persuasive, for a specific reason. #### Theoretical Framework

- **Appropriateness**: SRL is a defensible frame for a dashboard study; it is the field's usual choice and the one the deployment's logic implies. The problem is not selection.
- **Application depth**: Superficial (W8). The framework names phases it does not attribute, shifts its own phase partition between §1 and §2, guides neither the measurement nor the analysis, and receives no feedback from the results. Nothing in §5 revises, extends, or challenges SRL; the conclusions are simply declared "consistent with" it. A framework applied this way cannot be disconfirmed by the study, which is the diagnostic test for decorative use.
- **Alternative frameworks**: If the paper's actual outcome is course completion, the honest framework is a persistence model, not an SRL model — Tinto's integration account and Bean's attrition model both make direct predictions about course-level behavior, and Astin's involvement theory would give "engagement" a theorized meaning rather than a measured one. If the paper wants to keep SRL, then Zimmerman's cyclical phases or Winne and Hadwin's COPES architecture need to be named and mapped, and perceived control needs to be identified as what it is — closest to a motivational-belief construct in the Pintrich tradition, not a regulatory process. ## Review Body

This manuscript fails on domain grounds for two reasons that are independent of each other, and a domain reader can establish both without leaving the document.

The first is source misrepresentation. §2 ¶2 states that dashboards "have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students" and attributes it to Ferro and Nakamura (2021), a source the manuscript's own reference list titles *"When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student."* The claim and the title point in opposite directions about the same population. This is not a peripheral citation: §2 ¶2 tells the reader the position "underpins much of the equity-oriented rationale for institutional dashboard deployment, and we return to it in the Discussion," and §5 ¶2 duly returns, aligning the study's result with "the view that externalized progress cues can support persistence." The paper's equity argument is therefore resting on a reading its own bibliography contradicts (W1). This weighs more heavily with me than any coverage gap, because an incomplete literature review misses evidence while a reversed citation manufactures it. Around it sit further integrity signals that the editorial office should resolve rather than assume: nine of fifteen references are never cited, all fifteen span four venues, and every DOI shares one prefix with sequential-looking suffixes (W11).

The second is that the paper's central construct is not the construct its conclusion is about. §3.3 codes retention as remaining enrolled and sitting the final in one 15-week statistics course. §6 concludes that dashboards are a "dependable strategy for improving retention across programs and disciplines" for "higher education institutions worldwide." Course completion and institutional retention are distinct outcomes with distinct determinants — the distinction Tinto's departure framework was built to maintain — and collapsing them changes what the study found (W5). Compounding this, the retention literature that would have enforced the distinction is absent entirely: no Tinto, no Bean, no Astin, no critique tradition, in a manuscript whose own reference list contains an uncited gateway-course retention review and two further uncited entries from a persistence journal (W7).

Underneath both sits a framework that is named rather than used. Self-regulated learning carries the paper's mechanism across §1, §2, and §5, yet no model is identified, the phase structure §1 ¶2 paraphrases goes unattributed and is re-partitioned differently in §2 ¶3, and no SRL construct is ever mapped to anything measured — while the abstract nonetheless claims the study measured "self-regulated learning behavior," which §3.3 shows it did not (W2, W8). A framework that guides neither the design nor the interpretation cannot be disconfirmed by the study, and this one is not: §5 declares the results "consistent with" SRL and moves on, without confronting that the null exam comparison is evidence against the very monitoring-and-adjustment mechanism it proposes (W12).

The argument then escalates past all of it. §1 ¶4 promises to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story," §2 ¶5 cites an audit of causal overreach approvingly, §5 ¶3 concedes that the modest association "counsels against overstatement" — and §5 ¶1 says engagement "improved" retention while §6 calls dashboard investment a dependable and generalizable lever (W3). Dashboard use here was voluntary and recruitment happened mid-term, so the students who opened the dashboard are plausibly the students who were already going to finish, and students who had already withdrawn could not enter the sample at all. That confound is named on none of §3.2, §3.4, §5, or §5.1 (W4). Meanwhile the manuscript enters the "who benefits from dashboards" debate (§5 ¶2) without disaggregating by achievement level despite having exam scores (W9), and offers as its contribution another instance of the cross-sectional correlational design that §2 ¶5 identifies as the field's problem (W10). Separately, the reported quantities do not agree with each other: the abstract's *r* = .42 against §4.2's *r* = .24, Table 2's 127 against §4.3's 142, and a *p* = .008 attached to prose calling the difference small — I flag these as a domain reader who cannot evaluate claims against numbers that contradict themselves, and leave the inferential audit to Reviewer 1 (W6, W13).

I want to close on what is genuinely here, because it is not nothing. §2 is a synthesized argument rather than a citation list, and it cites against the paper's own eventual position three times. The engagement operationalization in §3.3 is more precise than most of this literature manages. The median split is disclosed as a concession rather than sold as a design choice. The authors are plainly capable of the work. A bounded report — one gateway course, one term, a modest association between dashboard session counts and course completion, stated as association, with the selection problem named and the persistence literature engaged — is a publishable paper. This is not that paper, and the distance between them is not a revision.

### Detailed Comments

#### Assumption Audit

- **Explicit assumptions**: The paper states its theoretical premise clearly in §1 — "the theoretical appeal of such dashboards rests on the assumption that visibility supports self-regulation" — and states its design premise, that behavioral logs plus self-report can connect behavior to subjective regulatory experience. §1 also makes an explicit methodological commitment: "We are careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story that might explain it." That commitment is stated and then not kept in §5 and §6. From my seat the failure to keep it is not a wording slip; the recommendation in §6 depends on the causal reading.

- **Implicit assumptions**: Four premises carry weight without ever being stated. (i) That data generated for course delivery may be repurposed for research without further notice to the people who generated it — this is assumed by §3.2 rather than argued, and it is precisely the assumption my field contests. (ii) That the dashboard is a stable, transferable object such that "invest in dashboards" names a coherent action, when §5.1 concedes interface features may drive the response. (iii) That engagement is a thing an institution can increase directly, when the measure is a session count. (iv) That students who did not respond to a mid-term announcement are exchangeable with those who did.

- **Paradigmatic assumptions**: The manuscript rests on the rational-self-monitor model of the learner: give the student accurate information about their standing and they will adjust effort accordingly. This is a paradigmatic commitment of the ed-tech tradition and is contested in adjacent literatures — motivation research (the same information can trigger avoidance rather than adjustment, as §2 itself notes via Osei), behavioral economics (information provision frequently fails to change behavior), and the sociology of education (persistence is shaped by financial, care-work, and belonging constraints that no interface reaches). §2 knows this. §5 reverts to the unqualified model. Naming the model as a premise, rather than treating it as background fact, would materially improve the paper.

#### Cross-Disciplinary Connections

- **Parallel research**: Research ethics and information-privacy scholarship has an established framework for exactly this situation — data collected under one set of norms flowing into a different context. That literature would let the authors argue their position on the log data rather than leave it as a bare disclosure. The higher-education persistence tradition has a settled definition of retention that would prevent finding 6.

- **Borrowing opportunities**: Two concepts would strengthen the paper immediately. *Contextual integrity* (information-privacy theory) gives the authors vocabulary to state whether an LMS-to-research flow respects the norms under which students generated the data. *Proxy-target degradation* — the well-established public-administration and economics observation that a measure adopted as a target ceases to be a good measure — directly undermines §6's "encourage students to engage" and should be addressed rather than avoided; the authors have already conceded the proxy's weakness in §2, so this is a short step. A third, from HCI: treating the dashboard as a designed artifact with a specification, so that "dashboard" in §6 names something replicable.

- **Methodological borrowing**: From evaluation research and from HCI deployment studies: pair the log analysis with a small number of student interviews about what they did after looking at the dashboard, and with instructor accounts of what the signals changed in their teaching. That converts an untestable causal chain into an observable one, at modest cost, and is achievable within the same course. From institutional research: obtain the full-cohort withdrawal record rather than the volunteer sample, which is the only way to see the students the intervention may have cost.

#### Practical Impact

- **Real-world application**: As written, a practitioner can do nothing with this paper except cite it in a procurement justification, which is the one use it is least equipped to support. The dashboard is unspecified, the cost is unknown, the recommended action ("encourage engagement") is undefined, and the outcome is course completion in one statistics section. The risk here is not academic-but-useless; it is worse — actionable-sounding and unactionable, which invites institutions to buy something on the strength of a claim the study cannot make.

- **Implementation feasibility**: Barriers the manuscript does not address: LMS integration and data-pipeline work; ongoing maintenance and the staffing to interpret signals; instructor training and the labor of acting on flags; and the governance work of telling students what is being collected and why. Unintended consequences the manuscript's own §2 predicts and §6 ignores: demotivating students who compare unfavorably, and, at institutional scale, a monitoring apparatus normalized without a corresponding consent practice.

- **Stakeholders**: Missing entirely — instructors (as actors, not conduits), advisors and student-success staff, and students as parties with an interest in their own data rather than as sources of it. Power asymmetry is untouched: the students in this study were observed by the institution that grades them, without being told, and the paper does not register this as a feature of the setting.

#### Broader Implications

- **Ethical dimensions**: The central one is finding 1. Two further points. First, the manuscript's own structure demonstrates the authors knew consent was live — they obtained it for the survey. Second, this is a paper about monitoring infrastructure that recommends worldwide adoption of monitoring infrastructure while modelling non-disclosure to the monitored. Publishing it as-is would give that practice a citation.

- **Social impact**: Gateway courses are where institutional stratification is most visible. A recommendation to invest in self-monitoring tools, absent any distributional analysis, risks directing resources toward students who are already equipped to use them and away from the financial, advising, and structural supports that the persistence literature associates with the students most at risk. The paper does not claim this outcome, but §6's unqualified "dependable strategy" invites it.

- **Future directions**: The most valuable follow-up is not a larger correlational study. It is (a) the full-cohort withdrawal analysis that would make the predicted harm visible, and (b) a consent-and-notice design study asking what students think should happen to their trace data — a question this field asks far less often than it asks what the traces predict.

### D1: methodology_rigor

- `what_to_look_for` — How the engagement measure is operationalised (raw login counts vs. dwell time vs. a composite, and whether the choice is justified); the retention outcome's exact definition and measurement window (course completion? final-week enrolment? next-term re-enrolment?); presence and identity of a comparison condition (randomised, quasi-experimental, historical cohort, or none); whether covariates plausibly confounded with both engagement and persistence (prior GPA/entry attainment, credit load, term standing, financial-aid or work status, first-generation status) are measured and adjusted for; N, attrition from the analytic sample, and how missing/partial log data are handled; effect sizes and uncertainty intervals rather than bare p-values; whether the analytic decisions were fixed before seeing outcomes or are visibly outcome-contingent; what a reader would need to reproduce the analysis and whether the deployment context (single course, single institution, single term) is stated as a design fact.

### Strongest Counter-Argument

Suppose I hold the opposite view — dashboards do nothing for retention, and this paper's association is an artifact of how it counted. The refutation writes itself out of the manuscript's own Methods.

Dashboard engagement (§3.3) is a cumulative count of sessions accrued across the term. Retention (§3.3) is coded as remaining enrolled and sitting the final. A student who withdraws in week nine stops accruing sessions in week nine. The predictor and the outcome are therefore two readings of one underlying quantity — weeks of continued participation — and no normalization for exposure window (sessions per enrolled week, or truncation at withdrawal) appears anywhere in §3.3 or §3.4. An *r* of .24 is within reach of what differential accrual alone would produce. No dashboard mechanism is required to generate it.

Layer the sampling on top. Recruitment occurred "[m]idway through the term" (§3.2), so every student who left in the first half is structurally absent, and the volunteers who remain are self-selected on exactly the disposition — willingness to answer a course announcement — that independently predicts persistence.

Then note what the causal account fails to predict. If dashboards work by scaffolding self-regulation, exam performance should move. It does not: 72.0 versus 69.2, *t* ≈ 1.31 (Table 2). The selection-plus-accrual account fits both results. The causal account fits one and must explain the other away, which §5 does by calling it help "at the margin."

The paper never states this rival explanation — not in §5, not in §5.1, not in §6. A study whose Introduction pledges to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story" cannot leave the most parsimonious pattern-only explanation unnamed.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths

1. **Differential exposure window (the strongest, and entirely unaddressed).** Sessions accumulate only while a student remains enrolled; withdrawn students stop accruing. The association between a cumulative count and an outcome defined as "still here at the end" is partly definitional. This explanation is more parsimonious than the SRL mechanism — it requires no psychology at all — and it predicts exactly the pattern observed: a modest retention association and a null exam difference. The manuscript never raises it.
2. **Selection into the sample plus reverse causation.** Mid-term volunteer recruitment selects on continued enrolment and on responsiveness to course communications. Students who intend to persist both answer announcements and open dashboards. The arrow runs from disposition to both variables. §5 asserts the opposite ordering without a design that can distinguish them.
3. **Third-variable confounding with zero adjustment.** No prior achievement, no baseline LMS activity, no credit load, no term standing. Conscientiousness alone would produce this correlation. The paper's analysis (§3.4) is bivariate throughout, so this explanation is not merely unaddressed — it is untestable with what was collected.
4. **Dashboard sessions as a proxy for general LMS presence.** §3.1 places the dashboard on the course home page, reachable at any time. A student visiting the course site frequently for any reason accumulates dashboard sessions. The measure may index "being an actively participating enrolled student," which is close to the outcome by construction.
5. **The interpretive path not taken.** Given the null exam result and the modest association, an equally available reading is that the dashboard does nothing and both signals reflect participation. The paper considers only degrees of dashboard benefit ("help at the margin"), never zero benefit. That the null hypothesis is never entertained as a live reading is the confirmation-bias signature here.

### Unexamined Premise

The paper assumes that a measure it has itself declared invalid for the construct can nonetheless serve as a valid policy target.

## Review Body

This manuscript will read as finished to a fast reader, and that is the first thing an editor should be told. The prose is fluent, the IMRaD structure is complete, the limitations section is present and sounds self-aware, and the reference list is formatted with DOIs. My seat's job is to report what survives arithmetic rather than what survives a first reading, and on that test the manuscript does not currently contain a reconstructible study.

Take the numbers first, because everything else is downstream of them. The paper reports three inferential results. The retention correlation appears at two incompatible magnitudes — .42 in the abstract, .24 in §4.2 — with no reconciliation, which means a reader of the abstract alone receives an association roughly three times larger in variance-explained terms than the Results section will defend. The perceived-control comparison is reported as *t*(156) = 3.02, but §4.1 states that 87 respondents answered the item; a two-group comparison on 87 cases has 85 degrees of freedom, not 156. The same strand's descriptive statistic is arithmetically impossible: a mean of 87 integer responses requires the mean times 87 to be a whole number, and 3.847 × 87 = 334.689, with the nearest attainable values being 3.839 and 3.851. Both statistics for a result the abstract carries as a headline are therefore unreconstructible from the paper's own Methods.

The third result is the most instructive, because Table 2 tells us which part of the text is wrong. Recomputing the independent-samples *t* from Table 2's own cells — 66 and 61 students, means of 72.0 and 69.2, SDs of 11.1 and 13.0 — gives a pooled SD of about 12.05, a standard error of about 2.14, and *t* ≈ 1.308. That reproduces the reported 1.31 almost exactly, at 125 degrees of freedom. So the analysis was run on 127 students, the text's claim that "[a]ll 142 students in the primary analytic sample were classified into engagement groups" is false, the reported *t*(140) is false, and the reported *p* = .008 is false at either degrees-of-freedom value: *t* = 1.31 corresponds to *p* ≈ .19. The prose around it — "did not reach a comparable level," "the difference was small" — describes a null result, which is what the statistic actually is. The *p*-value is the outlier, and it is the one that would have made the result significant.

Underneath the arithmetic sits a structural problem that no revision of the numbers would fix, and it is the point I would press hardest. Dashboard engagement is defined in §3.3 as a cumulative session count across the term. Retention is defined in the same subsection as remaining enrolled and sitting the final. Sessions accrue only while a student is enrolled and active, so a student who withdraws in week nine stops accruing in week nine. The predictor and the outcome are therefore two partial readings of one underlying quantity — duration of continued participation — and nothing in §3.3 or §3.4 normalizes for exposure window. An association of .24 is within reach of what differential accrual alone would produce, with no dashboard mechanism involved. Add the sampling: §3.2 recruits "[m]idway through the term," which removes every early leaver from the sample by construction, and does so through a volunteer announcement, which selects on responsiveness to course communications — a disposition that independently predicts persistence. The same paragraph opens by calling this "a random sample," a description it contradicts three sentences later and never withdraws.

The rival explanation this builds is not merely available; it fits the data better than the paper's own. If dashboards operated through the self-regulation mechanism §1 and §5 describe, exam performance should move. It does not — 72.0 against 69.2, *t* ≈ 1.31 — and §5 disposes of this by calling the effect help "at the margin." Selection plus accrual predicts both results at once. The manuscript never states this explanation, in §5, in §5.1, or in §6. Neither reverse causation nor third-variable confounding is named anywhere either, and since §3.4 is bivariate throughout, with no prior achievement, no baseline LMS activity, and no course-load measure collected, these rivals are not merely unaddressed but untestable with what was gathered.

What makes this a coherence failure rather than an ordinary limitation is that the manuscript diagnoses its own condition and then does nothing with the diagnosis. §1 pledges to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story, a distinction that the dashboard literature has not always maintained." §2 approvingly cites Ibarra (2023) on causal language outrunning evidence, concedes with Vandermeer (2023) that click-count proxies are inadequate "including the present one," and reports Osei (2020) on the demotivation risk of relative-standing feedback. Then §5 states that engagement "improved course retention" and "raises the probability that a student completes the course," and §6 calls institutional dashboard investment "a dependable strategy" and a "generalizable lever" for "higher education institutions worldwide." The Literature Review functions as inoculation: naming the criticism discharges it rhetorically while leaving the claims untouched. §5 does not even hold one position internally, asserting production in its first paragraph and counselling "against overstatement" in its third.

Two further items. §2 ¶2 attributes to Ferro and Nakamura (2021) the claim that dashboards "have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," while the paper's own reference list titles that work *"When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student."* I cannot check the source's contents within scope, and I do not need to: either the attribution is wrong or the claim is unsupported, and §5 ¶2 builds the equity argument on it either way. Separately, §3.1 records that the peer-comparison band was displayed to all students by default with no opt-in, and §2 ¶3 predicts that exactly this feature drives performance-avoidance-oriented students away. Because recruitment happened mid-term, any student the band drove out cannot appear in the sample. The design excludes its own predicted harm by construction and then recommends deployment.

On my own calibration: none of my eight CRITICAL findings rests on an assertion about what the field ought to do. Each is grounded in a contradiction internal to the manuscript, in arithmetic performed on the manuscript's own figures, or in a rival explanation whose burden falls on the paper's causal claim rather than on an external standard. Where a finding did depend on a norm I cannot ground within scope — the uncited reference entries, the decimal-reporting convention — I capped it at MINOR and labelled it. On the consent asymmetry in §3.2, where students consented to the survey but were not told their logs would be analyzed, I hold the finding at MAJOR strictly on the internal inconsistency in the authors' own applied standard, and I leave the ethics analysis to the seat configured for it. I also ran the surface-form parity gate deliberately, because this manuscript is precisely the shape that defeats it: polished prose, technical vocabulary, a limitations section that sounds rigorous. My verdicts on the three largest findings would be identical had the same claims arrived in clumsy prose, because they rest on arithmetic and definitions; and I have credited the fluency of §2's hedging with nothing, having checked whether those hedges constrain any downstream claim and found that they do not.

The manuscript contains no attempt at reviewer instruction or format manipulation; on that axis it is clean.

Finally, the "so what." §2 states that dashboard research already relies heavily on cross-sectional and correlational designs and that causal language routinely outruns the evidence. The differentiating contribution this paper offers is §1's promise to hold that line. It does not hold it. Strip the causal language and the generalization, and what remains is a single-site correlation of .24, on a proxy the authors themselves call inadequate, in a sample that cannot see its own early leavers — one more instance of the problem the paper opens by diagnosing.

### Decision Rationale

Four seats recommended Reject in their own words and the fifth's body supports the same end; the contract's governing condition, F1 at severity 90, fired on all five cards, with F2 firing on 5/5 and F3 on 2/5. ### Closing

After careful consideration across five independent reviews, we are unable to accept this manuscript for publication. That decision rests on the reconcilability of what is reported, not on the modesty of what was found: this journal publishes bounded correlational work, and §5's third paragraph shows the authors know how to state such a finding properly.

We want the record to be equally clear about what the panel found genuinely good, because all four non-DA seats said so independently. The median split is disclosed as a concession rather than sold as a design choice. Table 2 is the one exhibit in the manuscript that survives independent arithmetic, and it survives it exactly.

What the authors hold is worth rebuilding: log data on a full enrolled cohort, a dashboard live from week 1, an explicitly specified exposure measure, and institutional records that plausibly contain prior achievement and withdrawal timing. A study drawn from the full cohort rather than from volunteers, measuring exposure over a window that precedes the outcome, fitting a model appropriate to a binary outcome with prior-achievement adjustment, disaggregating by achievement level, reporting the base rate, and stopping at the edge of its evidence would be a genuine contribution — because it would model the discipline §2 correctly says this field lacks. We would welcome that study as a new submission.

If the authors prefer not to undertake the re-analysis, the EIC notes that a scaled-down, well-bounded local-context report could find a home at the *Australasian Journal of Educational Technology* or the *Journal of Computing in Higher Education* — but only after the citation, statistical, and ethics items are resolved, since those are venue-independent.
