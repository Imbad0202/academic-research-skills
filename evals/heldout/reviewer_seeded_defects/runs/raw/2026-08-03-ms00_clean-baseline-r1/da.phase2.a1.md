## Review Body

I read this manuscript as a chain and attacked each link. Reconstructed, the argument runs: (P1) perceived usefulness was measured with a six-item adapted scale showing α = .88; (P2) self-reported weekly LMS access was captured with one five-point ordinal item; (P3) the two covary at r = .42, 95% CI [.30, .52], ρ = .40, n = 214; therefore (C1) among these respondents, perceived usefulness is positively and moderately associated with self-reported use; (C2) this result is consistent with prior technology-acceptance research; (C3) it therefore constitutes an incremental, comparable data point for the literature; and (C4) onboarding that helps students see concrete usefulness may merit institutional attention. Link P3→C1 holds. The interval, the coefficient, and the sample size are mutually consistent (Fisher-z on r = .42 at n = 214 reproduces [.30, .52] to two decimals), the Spearman check is the right robustness move for a five-category outcome, and the paper does not smuggle causal verbs into its headline. I therefore found no singleton defect capable of invalidating the central claim, and the CRITICAL band below is empty by finding, not by courtesy. The score is `warn`, not `pass`, because links C2, C3, and C4 do not carry the weight the paper places on them, because the sample that licenses C1 may be conditioned on the very variable C1 is about, and because one pair of Methods statements cannot both be true.

The most consequential attack surface is the sampling frame. Recruitment ran through "the institution's course-announcement channel." At most universities that channel is the LMS itself or a notification service driven by it. If so, the invitation was visible in proportion to the dependent variable: students in the bottom category of the use item ("rarely or never") are close to unsamplable, which is consistent with the reported median sitting at "a few times per week." The paper never discloses the channel's relationship to the LMS, never gives an enrolled-undergraduate denominator, and never reports a response rate, so a reader cannot determine whether the observed association was estimated on a truncated range of the outcome. §6 treats this only as generic volunteer bias ("students who engage more with institutional channels may be overrepresented"), which is a weaker and differently-shaped concern than selection on the outcome. This matters twice over: it bounds the reference class of C1 far more tightly than the abstract's closing "among undergraduates," and it makes C4 an inference about low-engagement students drawn from a sample that may structurally exclude them.

The second attack lands on the paper's own statement of what it contributes. C2 and C3 are asserted three times (Abstract, §5, §7) and the comparison is never performed: not one prior point estimate, range, or confidence interval appears anywhere in §2, §4, §5, or §7. §2 concedes that "effect sizes vary across samples and instruments" and that Song (2018) found strengths varying by institution — which is precisely why an unquantified "consistent with prior research" is unfalsifiable here. Any positive coefficient of any magnitude would satisfy it. Since the paper's declared contribution is comparability ("an incremental data point, comparable with prior work"), the contribution claim rests on a comparison the manuscript neither makes nor equips the reader to make. The repair is cheap — quote the prior estimates and say plainly whether .42 sits inside or outside them — but until it is made, the claim is decoration.

Third, the epistemic-caution passage in §5, which is the paper's signature virtue, mis-enumerates the alternatives. It states that the reverse pathway "is equally consistent with the data" and stops there, and §6's four limitations likewise cover only single-site generalizability, self-report, cross-sectionality, and volunteer response. Confounding by common cause is absent from both. This is not an omission the authors were unaware of: §2 cites Ibarra and Poll (2021) for the role of context "in shaping both perception and use" and declares "we take these cautions seriously." A common cause — a course whose assessments run through the LMS raises both perceived usefulness and access frequency — is the leading rival account for a bivariate correlation with no covariates, and it is the one alternative the design cannot address at all, since only two variables were measured. Framing the inferential limit as a two-way directional choice understates it and makes the caution read as thorough when it is partial.

Fourth, §3.1 and §3.3 make mutually exclusive procedural claims. Removing "5 duplicate entries" requires a basis for judging two submissions to originate from one respondent, yet §3.3 states that no identifying information was collected and that responses could not be linked to individual students. One of these is inaccurate. Either quasi-identifiers (IP, cookie, session token) were retained, in which case the anonymity assertion presented to readers and implicitly to the ethics committee needs correcting, or the duplicate rule was heuristic and undocumented, in which case an undisclosed exclusion criterion altered the analyzed n. The five records are immaterial to r = .42; the unresolved contradiction in a compliance statement is not.

Several further defects are real but do not, on their own, reach Major, and I record them here rather than inflating them into a band they have not earned. (a) Construct drift: the abstract's closing sentence upgrades the measured variable to "LMS engagement" and §5 refers to "factors bearing on engagement," after §2 had correctly committed to treating the item as "an indicator of perceived use rather than a behavioral count" on Vasquez's (2020) authority. The body's usage is mostly disciplined, so this is a wording repair — but it lands in the paper's most-read sentence. (b) An untested attribution sits inside §4: "reported engagement reflects many influences beyond perceived usefulness, including course requirements and assessment schedules." Neither construct was measured, no citation is given, and its placement in Results rather than Discussion is likely why confounding never reached §6. (c) The residual-variance inference is one-sided: shared variance of roughly 18% is read as evidence of multiple substantive influences, when unquantified unreliability in a single coarse ordinal item is an equally available account. No reliability evidence exists for the outcome measure, in sharp contrast to the α reported for the predictor. (d) The sensitivity claim is presented as a design property — "the design was sensitive to small-to-moderate associations" — although no a priori target n is stated and 214 is simply what arrived; the threshold is also marginal, since roughly 215 cases are needed for .80 power at r = .19, making "greater than .80" a hair optimistic. (e) r² is never numerically reported despite the paper's self-description as "transparently reported," and no table or figure of descriptives accompanies the asserted scatterplot inspection.

To be explicit about what survived my attack: the paper does not commit the errors that would have forced a block. The conclusion is stated over "214 undergraduates at one university," the causal disclaimer is in the abstract rather than buried, the ordinal outcome is acknowledged as ordinal and checked non-parametrically, and the numbers reconcile across §4, §5, and §7. The defects above are located in the paper's framing of its own warrant and contribution, not in the finding itself, and every one of them is repairable in revision without new data — with the single exception of the confounding limitation, which is structural to a two-variable design and must therefore be stated rather than remedied.
