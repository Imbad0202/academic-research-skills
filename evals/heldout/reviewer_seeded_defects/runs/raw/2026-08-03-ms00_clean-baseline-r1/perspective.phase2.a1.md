## Review Body

I read this paper as the person it is nominally addressed to. I direct a centre for teaching and learning, I own the LMS and its clickstream, and I sign off on the onboarding programme the Discussion recommends. My question is narrow: after reading this, would I do anything differently on Monday morning, and does the paper tell me honestly whether I should?

On accessibility, the paper does well. The construct is defined in plain words rather than left to the instrument, the statistics are reported completely enough that a non-specialist can see what was and was not estimated, the hedging is consistent rather than decorative, and the Limitations section names four real constraints instead of the usual two ritual ones. An adjacent-field reader will understand what was measured and will not be misled about causality. That is why this is a warn and not a block: nothing here is opaque and nothing is overclaimed in the strong sense.

The problem is the second half of D4: substantiation of relevance. The paper's only claim on my world is the onboarding implication, and the correlation does not bear on it at any value. I cannot construct a decision that flips between r = .20, r = .42, and r = .60. If perceived usefulness had correlated at .20 I would still want onboarding to show students concrete value rather than announce that a platform exists; if it had correlated at .60 I would still not know whether onboarding moves perception, whether moved perception moves access, or whether both are downstream of assessment design. The paper concedes this in a subordinate clause and then keeps the recommendation in the Discussion and in the Abstract, which is where a busy practitioner or a provost will read it. Concession in one place plus retention in two is not substantiation.

Two further gaps are specific to transfer, and both are unusual because the authors already hold the missing information. First, this is a survey about use of a system that logs every access, at the authors' own institution, on the same population, in the same three-week window. The paper cites Vasquez (2020) to the effect that self-report and logs diverge substantially, lists that divergence as Limitation 2, and never says why logs were not obtained or why even a small consented validation subsample was infeasible. In my institution that is a data-governance and ethics-amendment question with known answers, not a wall. If a wall existed here, name it, because the reader's estimate of how much the finding is worth depends entirely on which it was. Second, the paper never says which platform this is or where the three-week window fell in the term. Those two facts are the whole of what another institution needs to judge applicability, and their absence converts an acknowledged single-site limitation into an unremediable one.

Finally, a practitioner reads the Results attribution of residual variance to "course requirements and assessment schedules" and recognises the right answer to the wrong question. Whether submission is gated through the LMS, whether instructors mandate it, whether assessment deadlines cluster, whether a mobile app exists, and course modality are what determine access frequency on my campus. The paper names none of them as measured variables while invoking two of them interpretively. I accept that a bounded descriptive note is not obliged to model variance, and I have tried not to review the paper the authors did not write. But the paper does claim comparability with prior work and positions itself as one point in a cross-institutional distribution, and that positioning cannot be sustained without saying what kind of site this point came from.

My net judgement: this is an honest, legible, competently hedged note whose practical relevance is asserted rather than earned, and whose transferability is blocked by two facts the authors could add in a sentence each. Revision should either substantiate the onboarding implication or remove it from the Abstract and demote it to a research suggestion, state why log validation was not done, and report the platform and the term position.

### S1: Causal restraint is maintained where it is easiest to lose

The paper does not merely disclaim causality in the abstract and then drift; it states the reverse pathway explicitly in the Discussion and attributes it to a cited caution rather than to authorial modesty. For an outside reader this is the single most useful signal that the correlational framing is real.

- **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5 "the reverse pathway, in which more frequent use raises perceived usefulness, is equally consistent with the data"`

### S2: The central construct is defined in outsider-readable language

Perceived usefulness is glossed conceptually before it is operationalised, rather than being left to be inferred from six unquoted items. A reader from assessment, from student affairs, or from institutional research can follow what is being claimed without entering the acceptance-model literature.

- **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 "the degree to which a person believes a technology will help them perform better"`

### S3: Sample accounting is transparent enough to audit

Received, excluded, and analysed counts are all reported with the reason for each exclusion. This is a small thing that is routinely omitted, and its presence lets a practitioner reconstruct the denominator instead of trusting it.

- **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 "233 responses were received; 14 incomplete submissions and 5 duplicate entries were removed"`

### W1: Behavioural log data existed on the same server for the same population and window, and the paper never says why it was not used

The study measures use by self-report at an institution that records every LMS access, for the same students, during the same three weeks. The paper's own literature review states that self-report diverges from logs "sometimes substantially," and Limitation 2 repeats it. What is missing is any account of why logs were unobtainable or why a consented validation subsample was infeasible: an ethics-amendment constraint, a governance refusal, an anonymity trade-off that would have broken the no-linkage guarantee in §3.3, or simply a scope decision. Each of those has a very different implication for how much weight I can put on the coefficient. As written, an adjacent-field reader cannot tell whether the self-report measure was a considered compromise or an unexamined default, and therefore cannot tell whether "reported use" is a usable proxy for the behaviour any institutional decision would target.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §6 Limitations and §3.3 Procedure and ethics — expected a stated reason log data was unavailable or subsample validation infeasible; checked Abstract, §1, §3.1, §3.2, §3.3, §3.4, §4, §5, §6, §7`
- **Confidence**: 5 — I operate the LMS, own its clickstream, and process the governance and ethics approvals this would require.

### W2: The onboarding implication is invariant to the result and is nonetheless carried into the Abstract

The recommendation that onboarding should help students see concrete usefulness is a reasonable practitioner intuition, and I hold it myself. But nothing in the reported correlation tests it, and no value of the coefficient would change it: the paper's design cannot distinguish perception causing access, access causing perception, or assessment design driving both, which the Discussion itself concedes. An implication that survives every possible result is not evidence-derived. The paper hedges it in place ("suggested by, not proven by") and then advertises it in the Abstract as one of the paper's two discussion contributions, which is the version most readers and most institutional decision-makers will retain. Because the practical implication is the paper's principal claim on relevance beyond its own subfield, its thin substantiation is what drives my warn on D4. Repair is available without new data: state plainly that the correlation neither supports nor tests the onboarding proposition, remove it from the Abstract, and reframe it in the Discussion as a hypothesis a design study would have to test.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5 "modest support for the intuition that LMS onboarding which helps students see concrete usefulness"`
- **Confidence**: 4 — I design, budget, and evaluate the onboarding programmes this recommendation targets.

### W3: The variables practitioners know dominate LMS access are invoked interpretively but never measured

The Results section attributes residual variance to course requirements and assessment schedules. That is very likely correct, and it is exactly the problem: those factors, together with whether assignment submission is gated through the LMS, whether instructors mandate its use, whether a mobile app exists, and course modality, determine access frequency on my campus far more than perception does. None was collected, so the attribution is an unmeasured post-hoc gloss presented inside a Results section. The consequence is not that the association is wrong; it is that the paper's stated positioning as an incremental data point comparable with prior work cannot hold, because the reader has no way to know whether this site's coefficient is low because perception matters little here or high because compliance pressure and perception happen to co-vary at this site. Either the contextual determinants are measured, or the comparability and cross-site framing must be narrowed to match what was actually observed.

- **Severity**: Major
- **Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4 "including course requirements and assessment schedules"`
- **Confidence**: 5 — daily operational knowledge of what drives access frequency on an institutional LMS.

### W4: Neither the platform nor the position of the survey window in the academic term is reported

Two facts the authors already possess would let any other institution judge applicability: which system this is (Canvas, Moodle, Blackboard, or in-house), and where the three-week window sat in the term. Access patterns differ sharply between platforms with different notification and mobile behaviour, and a window falling in an assessment-heavy fortnight produces a different use distribution from one in a teaching-only stretch. Without them, the single-institution limitation in §6 is not merely acknowledged but unremediable: I cannot decide whether this site resembles mine. The fix is two clauses in §3.1 and changes no core claim, which is why I score this Minor rather than higher, but its absence disproportionately reduces the paper's usefulness to precisely the readership the Discussion addresses.

- **Severity**: Minor
- **Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §3.1 Design and participants — expected the LMS platform identity and the position of the three-week window in the academic term; checked Abstract, §1, §3.1, §3.2, §3.3, §3.4, §4, §6`
- **Confidence**: 4 — I routinely assess whether other institutions' learning-analytics findings transfer to my own site.
