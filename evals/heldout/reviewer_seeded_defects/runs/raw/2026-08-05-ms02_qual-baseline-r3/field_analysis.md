# Field Analysis Report

## Paper Basic Information

- **Title**: Building Institutional Quality Culture: Administrator Perspectives on Quality Assurance Implementation in Universities
- **Abstract length**: ~155 words
- **Full text length**: ~2,300 words (Sections 1–6, excluding references); ~2,600 including reference list
- **Number of references**: 12
- **Language**: English (review to be conducted in English)

## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Quality in Higher Education** (Routledge) — The natural disciplinary home. Its readership is precisely the QA-scholar/practitioner hybrid audience this paper addresses, and the journal has published the "quality culture" debate for two decades. **Caveat**: this readership knows the canonical quality-culture literature intimately, so the paper's failure to engage foundational work (Harvey & Stensaker's quality-culture typology; Newton's work on ritual compliance and "feeling the squeeze"; the EUA Quality Culture Project; ESG 2015) will be the first thing a QiHE reviewer notices.

2. **Journal of Higher Education Policy and Management** — Explicitly welcomes administrator- and management-facing empirical work, and already publishes the closest prior study the paper itself cites (Silva & Tan, 2021). More tolerant of modest sample sizes if the practical contribution is sharpened. Best fit if the authors reposition the paper as a management-practice contribution rather than a theoretical one.

3. **Tertiary Education and Management** — Good fit for institutional-level administrative practice and multi-site comparative framing; likely the most realistic acceptance target given the current sample and analytic depth.

*Stretch option (not recommended in current form):* **Studies in Higher Education** — Q1, high visibility, but would require a substantially larger or more theoretically ambitious contribution and would not survive review with the present generalization and citation-fidelity problems.

## Reviewer Configuration Cards

### Reviewer Configuration Card #1

**Role**: EIC
**Display role**: Journal-Fit Reviewer

**Identity Description**: Associate Editor of *Quality in Higher Education*, a scholar of internal quality assurance systems in European and Asian higher education systems; previously served on a national QA agency's standards review panel and has handled roughly 200 submissions on quality culture, institutional self-evaluation, and accreditation impact. Reads first for desk-reject signals, then for whether the manuscript tells this journal's readers something they do not already know.

**Review Focus**:
1. **Originality claim vs. actual increment.** The Conclusion asserts "the first comprehensive account of how university administrators experience and enact QA implementation," while the Literature Review already concedes Pettersen (2022) and Rahman (2020) address distributed leadership and professional identity in the quality office, and Silva & Tan (2021) theorize the intermediary position. Assess whether the paper's stated novelty survives its own literature review, and what the defensible contribution claim actually is.
2. **Reference verifiability — editorial screening flag.** All 12 references carry DOIs in the `10.5555/1042001`–`10.5555/1042012` range (a reserved test/sandbox prefix, allocated sequentially), and several of the journal and publisher titles do not correspond to indexed outlets. Verify each reference against Crossref/Scopus before any substantive assessment proceeds; if the reference list cannot be verified, this is a pre-review integrity matter, not a revision item.
3. **Fit and readership value.** Whether a QA-specialist readership gains something from three themes (ritual compliance / distributed leadership / identity work) that are individually well-established in this literature, and whether the paper's real contribution is the *interaction* among them (which it gestures at in §4.3 but never develops).

**Will particularly care about**: Whether "quality culture" is given an operational definition the study can actually test, rather than being invoked as an atmospheric term — and whether the paper distinguishes quality culture from quality assurance, accreditation compliance, and institutional climate, which it currently conflates.

**Possible blind spots**: Will judge the paper against the journal's disciplinary conversation and may under-weight the technical statistical problems in §4.4 and the participant-protection issue in §4.1; will likely treat the sample-size contradiction as a copy-editing slip rather than probing which number is true.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 1

**Identity Description**: Mixed-methods researcher in educational research methodology, trained in qualitative inquiry (reflexive thematic analysis, interpretive phenomenological work) with a second competence in educational measurement; serves on the methods review board of a mixed-methods journal and routinely reviews QUAL→quan designs for integration quality. Known for insisting that "mixed methods" means integration, not adjacency.

**Review Focus**:
1. **Sample integrity and internal arithmetic.** The abstract reports fourteen administrators; §3.2 reports twelve (n=12). The survey reports n=48, but the only subgroup comparison uses n=9 and n=11, leaving 28 respondents unaccounted for and no per-institution breakdown. Establish which figures are correct and whether any reported result changes.
2. **The inferential claim in §4.4.** "A statistically significant difference (p<.05)" is reported with no named test, no test statistic, no degrees of freedom, no effect size, no confidence interval, and no normality/assumption check — on groups of 9 and 11. Assess whether this claim is reportable at all, and whether "points to institutional type as a possible moderator" is licensed by an n=20 contrast drawn from a convenience sample.
3. **Survey instrument, absent.** M=3.9 (SD=0.6) is reported without the response scale range, item wording, item count, provenance (adapted or original), or any reliability/validity evidence. A mean is uninterpretable without its scale.
4. **Thematic analysis rigor.** §3.4 describes coding in three sentences. No analytic framework is cited, no coder count or agreement procedure, no saturation criterion, no audit trail, no reflexivity or positionality statement. §4.1 additionally claims themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" while §3.1 specifies *semi*-structured interviews — and pairs the discrepant claim with a confidence assertion ("giving us confidence that the pattern was not an artifact").
5. **Case exclusion and the balance claim.** §4.3 states the study "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views," then discloses in the next sentence that dissenting participants "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure." This is disconfirming-case suppression justified by the analytic frame it should have challenged; the two sentences cannot both stand.
6. **Anonymization adequacy (deductive disclosure).** §3.5 claims data were "fully anonymized... so that no individual could be identified." §4.1 attributes a quotation to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and another to "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" — role + institution-type + superlative descriptors that, in a three-institution single-system design, identify individuals. Assess whether the ethics claim as written is accurate.
7. **Integration.** Whether the two strands are genuinely mixed. §4.4 says the survey "corroborated the qualitative picture," then immediately says it contradicted expectations ("had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture"). No joint display, no integration procedure, no meta-inference logic.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the qualitative claims are traceable — that is, whether a reader could reconstruct how twelve (or fourteen) transcripts became three themes, and whether the quantitative strand is doing analytic work or decorative work.

**Possible blind spots**: May be so occupied with design and reporting defects that they do not evaluate whether the three themes are substantively novel to the QA field, nor catch that the Discussion misrepresents a cited source's argument — that is a domain-knowledge catch, not a methods catch.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #3

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 2

**Identity Description**: Professor of higher education policy specializing in quality assurance regimes and institutional self-evaluation; has conducted comparative studies of internal QA across national systems, contributed to a European Standards and Guidelines consultation, and written extensively on the gap between accreditation compliance and educational improvement. Deeply read in the primary literature and reads citation-by-citation.

**Review Focus**:
1. **Citation fidelity — a substantive error, not a typo.** §2 states Delacroix (2018) "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual, and argues *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of a healthy quality culture," and the reference-list annotation confirms this. §5 then states: "our findings align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture. Consultation, on this reading, is the engine of internalization." The Discussion recruits Delacroix for the exact position he wrote a paper to refute, and the paper's third headline implication rests on this reversal. Assess how much of §5 survives correcting it — and whether Delacroix's actual argument is in fact a *challenge* to the paper's conclusion that the study has left unanswered.
2. **Missing canonical literature.** The manuscript engages no foundational quality-culture scholarship: no Harvey & Green on the multiple meanings of quality, no Harvey & Stensaker's quality-culture typology, no Newton on ritual compliance and academic responses to QA, no EUA Quality Culture Project, no reference to ESG 2015 or any equivalent national framework. Since §4.1's "ritual compliance" finding restates a claim established in this literature twenty years ago, the omission determines whether §4.1 is a finding or a rediscovery.
3. **Conceptual precision.** "Quality culture" is never defined operationally. The paper moves between quality culture, quality assurance, compliance, and institutional climate without marking the boundaries, then measures "quality culture" with an undescribed survey and reads a mean of 3.9 as a cultural property.
4. **The overgeneralization in §5.** "These findings demonstrate that universities across the sector treat QA as a negotiated accomplishment" and "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture: administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension" — from three institutions in one national system. Note that the Conclusion's own closing sentence ("Future research should test these dynamics across a wider range of institutional types and national systems") concedes the point the Discussion overrode.
5. **National context suppressed.** "Three universities in a single national system" — the system is never named, and neither is its QA regime (voluntary or mandatory? programme-level or institution-level? consequences attached?). Since the entire argument concerns how external requirement translates into internal meaning, the regime's design is not background detail; it is the independent variable.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the paper's claimed contribution is genuinely additive to a mature literature, or whether it restates well-known findings in new vocabulary without the citations that would expose the overlap.

**Possible blind spots**: May accept the survey and subgroup statistics at face value as "supplementary"; unlikely to interrogate the anonymization or the excluded-cases problem; may treat the numerical inconsistency as the methodologist's territory.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #4

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 3

**Identity Description**: Organizational sociologist working on accountability regimes, audit cultures, and institutional decoupling across public-sector fields (healthcare accreditation, school inspection, higher education); publishes in organization-studies venues on how organizations respond to external evaluation, and has advised two national evaluation agencies on why their standards produce documentation rather than change. Approaches the manuscript from *outside* higher education studies, and from the perspective of the external body the paper treats as a black box.

**Review Focus**:
1. **The paper's core findings already have names in another discipline — and the paper does not use them.** "QA as ritual compliance" is textbook decoupling (Meyer & Rowan; Bromley & Powell's distinction between policy–practice decoupling and means–ends decoupling, which would sharpen §4.1 considerably). "QA as identity work" is institutional work / identity work in the organization-studies sense (Lawrence & Suddaby; Alvesson & Willmott). "Administrators reconciling competing demands" is competing institutional logics (Thornton & Ocasio) or organizational hybridity. The paper's §4.3 finding — that administrators hold the compliance/meaning tension *open* rather than resolving it, treating the gap as where their identity lives — is genuinely interesting and is precisely what this literature calls sustained hybridity. Borrowing this vocabulary would give the paper the theoretical apparatus it currently lacks; not borrowing it means the paper reinvents established concepts and cannot be found by the readers most likely to cite it.
2. **The external body is never opened.** The paper's stated argument is that quality culture is "an ongoing negotiation among administrators, academics, and external bodies," yet external bodies appear only as an off-stage force generating requirements. No agency standards, review methodology, sanctions structure, or reviewer behaviour is described. A negotiation with an unexamined party is not a negotiation account.
3. **Level-of-analysis error in §5.** Data were collected from individuals; claims are made about institutions ("universities across the sector treat QA as...") and about a field ("the higher education sector as a whole"). Three organizations cannot characterize an organizational field, and twelve individuals cannot characterize three organizations. Ask whether the paper is studying administrators, institutions, or a field — it currently claims all three.
4. **The administrator/staff divergence is the paper's most valuable and least developed finding.** §4.4 and §5 note that administrators describe ritualism while staff report moderately positive sentiment, then set it aside as "a divergence worth pursuing in future work." From an organizational standpoint this asymmetry is not a loose end — it is diagnostic (does the QA burden concentrate in the quality office, insulating academics? is that a design feature or a failure?). The paper buries its most interesting result.
5. **Practical translation — what would anyone actually do differently?** The Conclusion recommends processes that "create room for meaning-making rather than merely demanding documentation." For an agency director or a quality office head, this is not actionable. What changes in the review cycle, the evidence requirement, or the panel's terms of reference? Note that the paper's one concrete recommendation (maximize participation) rests on the reversed Delacroix citation — remove that, and the practical contribution is currently empty.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether "quality culture" is doing explanatory work or functioning as a placeholder for "the part of the organization we did not measure" — and whether the authors would accept a decoupling framing that treats ritual compliance as a *rational organizational response* rather than a pathology.

**Possible blind spots**: Less familiar with higher-education-specific QA instruments and frameworks, so may propose organization-studies literature that duplicates work the HE field has already done in its own terms; will not scrutinize interview-count or statistical reporting detail; may under-weight journal-fit considerations.

**Three issues that must not be lost in synthesis:**

1. **Reference verifiability is a gate, not a revision item.** The `10.5555/` prefix is a reserved test range and the DOIs are sequential across all twelve entries. Confirm this before weighting any other critique — if the reference list is not verifiable, the substantive review is provisional.

2. **The Delacroix reversal is independently detectable.** Even without external verification, the manuscript contradicts *itself*: §2 and the reference-list annotation both state Delacroix argues against consultation-as-sufficient; §5 cites him as recommending consultation as the central mechanism. This is a self-contained internal contradiction and should be reported with high confidence regardless of what verification finds.

3. **Two claims in §4 are self-refuting within a single paragraph.** The "balanced representation / full range of views" claim is immediately followed by disclosure that dissenting participants were excluded. The "fully anonymized, no individual identifiable" claim is immediately followed by "the quality director of the largest private university in the region." Neither requires domain expertise to detect, and both bear directly on whether the findings and the ethics statement can be taken at face value.

**Anticipated tension between reviewers:** R2 will want the paper anchored in the higher education QA canon; R3 will want it anchored in organization studies. These pull in opposite directions and the paper cannot fully satisfy both in one revision. The synthesizer should frame this as a *positioning decision the authors must make* (field journal + QA canon, or organization-studies journal + institutional theory), not as two independent required additions. R2's route is the lower-risk path to publication; R3's route is the higher-ceiling contribution.

**Register:** This is a competent, well-written revised draft with a genuinely interesting under-developed finding (§4.3's held-open tension; §4.4's administrator/staff divergence). The problems are serious and several are disqualifying in their current form, but they are correctable. Reviewers should use a **developmental register** — specific, actionable, addressed to authors who can fix this. The register governs wording only; the recommendation itself must follow from the evidence against the criteria, and on the present evidence the numerical contradictions, the unsupported inferential claim, the citation reversal, and the anonymization problem do not support a positive verdict without major revision.
