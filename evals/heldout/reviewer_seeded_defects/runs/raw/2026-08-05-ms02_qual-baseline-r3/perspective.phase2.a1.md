## Review Body

I read this manuscript from outside higher education studies, as an organizational sociologist who works on accountability regimes and institutional decoupling across public-sector fields, and from the perspective of the party the paper treats as off-stage: the external body. Two things follow from that vantage. The first is that the paper is genuinely readable — I could reconstruct its argument without insider knowledge of any national accreditation regime, and that is not trivial in this subfield. The second is that readability and connectivity are different properties. This paper is legible to an adjacent-field reader and simultaneously invisible to one, because every one of its three headline findings already has a name in organization studies and the paper uses none of those names.

"QA as ritual compliance" is decoupling. More precisely, the material in §4.1 looks like what Bromley and Powell call means–ends decoupling — the activity is genuinely performed, at real cost, but its causal link to the espoused goal has been severed — rather than policy–practice decoupling, where the policy exists only on paper. The distinction is not pedantry; it changes the diagnosis and the remedy. The quotation about producing portfolios "that nobody reads except the panel" and leaving "nothing behind that a teacher would recognize as helping them teach" is means–ends decoupling stated almost verbatim. "QA as identity work" is identity work in the Alvesson and Willmott sense, embedded in institutional work; "administrators reconciling competing demands" is competing institutional logics, or organizational hybridity. And §4.3's observation that administrators hold the compliance/meaning tension open rather than resolving it is what that literature calls sustained hybridity — a real, non-obvious finding that the paper reports and then does not know what to do with. Borrowing this apparatus would give the paper the theory it currently lacks and, just as importantly, would make it findable by the readers most likely to cite it. Not borrowing it means the paper independently rediscovers established concepts and files them under headings that no organizational reader searches on.

I would also press the authors on a framing choice implicit throughout. The manuscript treats ritualism as a failure state — something to be recovered from, cured by "meaning-making." From a decoupling standpoint, ritual compliance is frequently a *rational* organizational response to an evaluation regime whose sole accepted evidence of quality is documentation. If the panel reads portfolios, producing portfolios is competent behaviour, not pathology. Whether the authors would accept that reframing matters, because it relocates the object of criticism from the university to the review instrument — and the review instrument is precisely what this paper never examines.

That absence is my central structural concern. The paper's own thesis, stated in the abstract and repeated in the Conclusion, is that quality culture is "an ongoing negotiation among administrators, academics, and external bodies." Two of those three parties are studied; the third appears only as an off-stage generator of requirements. No agency standards, no review methodology, no sanctions structure, no account of what reviewers actually attend to or reward. A negotiation account in which one party is never characterized is not a negotiation account, and it also removes the paper's ability to distinguish the two kinds of decoupling above, since that distinction turns on what the external instrument asks for.

On the practical side, I asked myself the question an agency director or quality-office head would ask: what would I do differently on Monday? The Conclusion recommends processes that "create room for meaning-making rather than merely demanding documentation," which is a description of a desirable state rather than an instruction. The single concrete recommendation — maximize genuine participation — rests entirely on a citation that the manuscript itself contradicts two sections earlier. Remove that, and the practical contribution is empty. Meanwhile the paper's most operationally useful result, the administrator/staff asymmetry, is set aside as future work.

On scoring: I considered block for cross-disciplinary relevance and chose warn deliberately. My Phase 1 block condition required either that core constructs be undefined and the argument unreconstructable without regime-specific insider knowledge, or that the paper claim generalizability *across disciplinary fields* without support. Neither holds on its own terms. "Quality culture" is given a working definition at first use, and the paper's overreach in §5 is a within-field, level-of-analysis error rather than a cross-disciplinary claim — which is why I record it below as a finding for the methodology and coherence seats rather than treating it as a block on my dimension. What does hold is the warn condition: the central construct is defined only nominally and never operationally, and the transferable implications are asserted rather than tied to reported evidence.

### S1: The §4.3 finding on sustained tension is a real contribution

The observation that administrators do not resolve the compliance/meaning tension but inhabit it, treating the gap as the location of their professional identity, is a genuinely interesting result that the paper reports cleanly and does not overstate at the point of reporting. In organization-studies terms this is sustained hybridity, and it is the sentence I would build the paper around.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4.3 — "Rather than resolving the tension between compliance and meaning, they held it open"`

### S2: The manuscript is accessible to adjacent-field readers at the prose level

Acronyms are expanded at first use, and the paper supplies a working definition of its central construct in the Introduction rather than assuming it. I could follow §1 through §4 without any prior exposure to higher education quality assurance instruments. Many papers in specialist policy subfields cannot claim this.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §1 — "the shared values, practices, and commitments through which an institution takes collective ownership of its own standards"`

### S3: The research question targets a boundary-spanning role that adjacent literatures also care about

Framing administrators as the actors who must daily reconcile external requirement and internal practice locates the study on terrain that street-level bureaucracy, boundary-spanning, and institutional-work scholarship all occupy. The question is well posed for travel even though the answer is not yet written for travel.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 — "They are neither the accreditors imposing standards nor the academics resisting them, but the actors who must reconcile the two daily"`

### W1: Level-of-analysis slippage from individuals to institutions to a field

Data were collected from individuals; §5 makes claims about institutions ("universities across the sector") and then about an organizational field ("the higher education sector as a whole"). Twelve administrators cannot characterize three organizations, and three organizations in one national system cannot characterize a field. The paper needs to decide what its unit of analysis is — administrators, institutions, or a field — because it currently claims all three, and the field-level claim in particular is unsupported by any field-level data (no agency population, no sector distribution, no comparison system). The repair is a narrowing of scope rather than new data, which is why I place this below the Critical band, but the claim as written cannot stand.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5 — "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture" and "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension"`
**Confidence**: 5 — level-of-analysis inference is a core competence of my field.

### W2: The external body, one of the paper's three named parties, is never opened

The manuscript's thesis is a three-party negotiation, but the external body is present only as an unexamined source of requirements. There is no description of the agency's standards, its review methodology, what evidence it accepts, what sanctions or consequences attach to outcomes, or how reviewers behave. This matters for more than completeness: without characterizing the instrument, the paper cannot tell whether the ritualism it documents reflects institutional evasion or competent adaptation to what the instrument rewards, and it cannot distinguish the two forms of decoupling that its own data appear to bear on. Either the third party enters the study, or the thesis is rescoped to a two-party account of how administrators interpret an external demand they do not directly observe.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §2 and §3.2 — expected any description of the external agency's standards, review methodology, sanctions, or reviewer conduct; checked §1, §2 strand one, §3.2 participants and setting, §4.1, and §6`
**Confidence**: 5 — I study external evaluation bodies and have advised two national agencies on this exact question.

### W3: The Delacroix citation is reversed, and it carries the paper's only actionable recommendation

Section 2 correctly reports that Delacroix (2018) warns participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual and argues against treating consultation as evidence of quality culture; the reference-list annotation says the same. Section 5 then attributes to the same source the opposite position — that consultation is the central mechanism for building quality culture — and uses it as the warrant for the paper's single concrete design recommendation. At least one of these two statements is a factual error about the published record, and the reader cannot tell which. Because the reversed version is load-bearing for the only recommendation a practitioner could act on, correcting it does not merely fix a citation: it leaves the practical contribution empty and requires the authors to build a recommendation that survives Delacroix's actual argument. As written, this cannot go to print.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 and §5 — "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual" versus "who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism"`
**Confidence**: 4 — direct comparison of two passages within the manuscript; I have not read the cited source.

### W4: Disconfirming cases were excluded while the paper claims full-range coverage

Section 4.3 asserts balanced representation and full coverage of administrator views, then states in the next sentence that participants with more skeptical or dissenting views about the QA enterprise were excluded because they fell outside the three-theme structure. This is self-refuting on its face, and the exclusion runs in the analytically worst direction: the dissenters are precisely the cases that would test whether the three-theme structure is a finding or an artifact of the coding frame. From my angle the loss is specific — skeptics are the informants who would reveal whether ritual compliance is experienced as rational adaptation or as institutional failure, which is the interpretive question the paper most needs answered. The themes cannot be credited until the excluded accounts are reported and their fit or misfit with the structure is shown.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4.3 — "the study achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views" and "these were excluded for space"`
**Confidence**: 4 — analytic-transparency norms for qualitative work are shared across social-science traditions; I am not assessing interview counts.

### W5: The findings reinvent established concepts without naming them

Ritual compliance, identity work, and the reconciliation of competing demands correspond to decoupling, institutional and identity work, and competing institutional logics respectively. None of these literatures appears in §2 or the reference list, which draws exclusively on higher education QA scholarship. The cost is double: the paper has no theoretical apparatus with which to develop §4.3 beyond description, and organizational readers searching on the concepts the paper actually studies will not find it. I note my own blind spot — it is possible the HE field has developed equivalent apparatus in its own terms, in which case the authors should name that apparatus instead. What is not tenable is the current position, where three findings are presented as emergent themes with no theoretical lineage at all.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §2 Literature Review and the References list — expected engagement with the decoupling and institutional-logics literatures that already name these findings; checked all three strands of §2, §4.1, §4.3, §5, and every reference entry`
**Confidence**: 5 — this is my home literature.

### W6: The administrator/staff divergence is the most valuable result and is set aside

Section 4.4 opens by declaring that the survey corroborated the qualitative picture and then reports something that complicates it: administrators describe theater while staff sentiment is moderately positive. Section 5 acknowledges the tension and defers it to future work. From an organizational standpoint this asymmetry is not a loose end; it is diagnostic. It suggests the QA burden may concentrate in the quality office, insulating academic staff from the documentation regime — and the interesting question is whether that concentration is a design feature (a buffer that protects teaching from audit) or a failure (a quality function decoupled from the practice it is meant to improve). Either answer would be a contribution. The paper should also stop describing the survey as corroborating when it does not, and should reconcile the moderately positive staff score with the ritualism claim rather than reporting both and moving on.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4.4 and §5 — "The survey corroborated the qualitative picture." and "a divergence worth pursuing in future work"`
**Confidence**: 4 — organizational-asymmetry reasoning; I am not evaluating the subgroup statistics.

### W7: "Quality culture" is not operationalized and functions as a residual category

The construct is defined once by citation and never again. The survey reports an institutional quality culture score of M=3.9 with no named instrument, no item wording, no response-scale anchors, and no source measure in the references, so an adjacent-field reader cannot say what was measured or what a 3.9 would mean. In the qualitative sections the term absorbs whatever the themes do not explain. This is the specific risk I watch for in audit-culture research: the construct becomes a placeholder for the part of the organization that was not measured, and its explanatory work is done by the reader's intuition rather than the paper's evidence. Either the construct earns its keep — defined, operationalized, and shown to discriminate — or the paper should describe what it observed without appealing to it.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §3.3 and §4.4 — expected the instrument, item wording, and response scale behind the reported quality culture score; checked §3.1, §3.3, §3.4, §4.4, and the References for a named source measure`
**Confidence**: 4 — construct operationalization; I am not assessing psychometric properties.

### W8: The abstract and methods report different participant counts

The abstract states fourteen administrators were interviewed; §3.2 states twelve. One of the two is wrong, and the discrepancy propagates into any reader's assessment of the evidence base. Trivially fixable, but it must be fixed.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Abstract and §3.2 — "Fourteen administrators were interviewed in depth" and "Twelve senior administrators (n=12) with direct QA responsibilities"`
**Confidence**: 5 — direct comparison of two stated counts.

### W9: The protocol is described inconsistently, and the warrant offered for the themes does not follow

Section 3.1 describes semi-structured interviews; §4.1 attributes the themes to a structured protocol and offers that as grounds for confidence that the pattern is not an institution-specific artifact. Protocol structure does not license that inference — ruling out an institutional artifact would require cross-institutional comparison, which the paper does not report. The description should be made consistent and the warrant replaced with whatever comparative evidence actually exists.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.1 and §4.1 — "semi-structured interviews" and "These themes emerged systematically from the structured protocol"`
**Confidence**: 3 — a textual inconsistency plus a warrant judgment; the methodology seat is better placed to weigh it.
