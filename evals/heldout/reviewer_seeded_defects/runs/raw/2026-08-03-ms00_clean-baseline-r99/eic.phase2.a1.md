## Review Body

I read this as the editor who would have to decide where it goes, so I will state the routing judgment first and then defend it.

**Routing.** As submitted, this is not a full research article; it is a brief report or research note that has been formatted as a full article. The delivered analytic content is one bivariate correlation with a non-parametric robustness check, presented without a single table or figure, supported by six references. The paper knows this about itself — the Introduction calls the question "deliberately narrow," and the Conclusion offers the work as "an incremental, design-bounded contribution." I have no objection to the genre. I object to the mismatch between the genre the paper occupies and the category it was submitted in, and more seriously to the fact that the brief-report genre carries an obligation the paper declines to meet.

**Reader value, which is where the block comes from.** Ask what a practitioner or researcher can do with r = .42 from one unnamed mid-sized public university. If this is a standalone finding, the answer is very little: the number is uninterpretable in isolation, because nothing in the paper tells the reader whether .42 is high, low, or unremarkable relative to what the field has already measured. If it is a replication data point — which is what the paper says it is — then the comparison to prior estimates is not optional decoration; it is the entire contribution. The manuscript performs the self-nomination and skips the work. It even supplies its own argument for why the work is necessary: it cites Song (2018) for the proposition that association strengths vary by institution and that any single-site estimate should be read as one point in a distribution. Having established that, the paper owes its reader the distribution. It gives none. This is an obligation the manuscript incurred itself, not one I have imposed from outside, and it is why I cannot pass D6 on rewriting alone.

**Framing coherence, and a distinction worth preserving.** My configuration flagged the risk that I would read the paper's pervasive modesty as either calibration or evasion. It is both, on different axes, and the revision must not collapse them. On the *inferential* axis the modesty is genuine calibration and should be protected in revision: the paper refuses causal language, names the reverse pathway explicitly, treats its use item as an indicator of perceived rather than actual engagement, and does not smuggle a mechanism into the Discussion. That discipline is rarer than it should be in single-site survey submissions, and I would resist any revision that traded it for stronger claims. On the *contribution* axis, however, the same modesty does substitute for missing work. Restricting what you claim about your number is not the same as telling the reader what your number is worth. The author appears to have treated the first as discharging the second.

**What would change my score.** Either (a) the manuscript reports the specific effect sizes from the studies it cites, states whether its instruments are commensurable with theirs, and locates .42 within or outside that range — at which point the increment is verifiable and the paper clears the bar; or (b) it is resubmitted to the short-paper or replication track with explicit replication framing and a benchmark table, and the full-article framing is dropped. Option (a) is the stronger paper. Option (b) is the faster route. Neither requires new data collection, which is why I score this repairable and expect a major revision rather than a rejection. The methods, ethics reporting, and analytic honesty are not what is holding this back.

I have not scored methodology, domain accuracy, argumentative coherence, or cross-disciplinary relevance; those belong to other seats. I will flag for the record that my configuration identifies two blind spots I could not fully close from the editorial seat — the measurement detail in §3.2 (a single ordinal item on the dependent side, correlated parametrically) and the verification status of the reference list — and I am relying on the methodology and domain seats for both. My D6 block does not depend on either.

### W1: The paper's self-declared status as a "comparable" data point is never discharged — no prior effect size appears in the manuscript

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 Literature Review, final paragraph "It is intended as an incremental data point, comparable with prior work, rather than as a test of a theoretical model."
**Confidence**: 5 — four years running a journal's replication and short-paper track; this is the standard I apply to every submission that claims replication value.

The manuscript claims comparability with prior work in the Literature Review, the Discussion ("consistent with prior technology-acceptance research"), the Abstract, and the Conclusion. In none of these places, nor anywhere else, does a prior effect size appear. The Literature Review states that "effect sizes vary across samples and instruments" without reporting any of them, and describes Song (2018) as having found that "association strengths varied by institution" without giving the range. The consequence is that the paper's only claim to reader value cannot be evaluated by the reader: r = .42 is presented without the comparison set that would make it informative, and the paper's own citation of Song is an argument that the comparison set is indispensable. Uncorrected, this alone makes the submission unacceptable at this venue, and it alone carries the D6 block. It is repairable without new data — the required work is locating comparator estimates, assessing instrument commensurability against a six-item adapted PU scale and a single-item frequency measure, and stating where .42 falls — but that is literature and analytic work, not rewriting, and if commensurability fails the framing itself must change.

### W2: Full-article submission delivering brief-report content

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 Introduction "It asks a deliberately narrow question: among undergraduates at one university, is perceived usefulness of the LMS associated with self-reported frequency of use?"
**Confidence**: 4 — routine triage judgment for this venue's article categories; another editor might set the category boundary marginally differently.

The paper's own characterisation of its scope is accurate, and the delivered content matches it: one Pearson correlation, one Spearman check, two descriptive statistics, no tables, no figures, six references. That content is appropriate to a research note or brief report. As a full research article it is under-built — not statistically, since the power statement in §3.4 is adequate, but substantively, in that the article category promises a treatment the manuscript does not attempt (no model test, no multivariate work, no subgroup analysis, no benchmarking). The remedy is restructuring and re-categorisation, which changes what the paper advertises rather than what it found, so the empirical core survives intact.

### W3: No figures or tables at all, including the scatterplot the Analysis section relies on

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.4 Analysis and §4 Results — expected a scatterplot figure or descriptive table supporting the reported linearity and symmetry inspection; checked all seven numbered sections, the abstract, and the reference list for any figure or table callout

Section 3.4 reports that "scatterplot inspection showed an approximately linear, monotonic association with no extreme bivariate outliers, and both distributions were approximately symmetric." ### W4: Abstract under-reports the paper's own reporting standard

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract "Perceived usefulness was positively and moderately associated with self-reported LMS use (r = .42, p < .001)."
**Confidence**: 4 — standard reporting-convention check; venue-specific abstract requirements may vary in detail.

The Results section reports the point estimate with a 95% confidence interval, a p value, n, and a non-parametric robustness check. The abstract drops the interval and the robustness check and keeps only r and p, which is a weaker standard than the paper actually meets. Since the interval [.30, .52] is precisely the information a reader scanning abstracts for comparable estimates would need, its omission is self-defeating given the paper's stated aim of being comparable with prior work.

### S1: Inferential calibration is sustained across every section, not just the limitations

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 Discussion "the reverse pathway, in which more frequent use raises perceived usefulness, is equally consistent with the data"
**Confidence**: 5 — direct reading; the correlational discipline is verifiable sentence by sentence.

The paper names the reverse causal pathway explicitly and attributes the caution to a cited source rather than burying it in a limitations list. ### S2: Reporting completeness and sensitivity are above the norm for single-site survey submissions

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.4 Analysis "With n = 214, the study had greater than .80 power to detect a correlation of r >= .19 at alpha = .05"
**Confidence**: 4 — comparative judgment against submissions I triage in this category.

The paper states its detectable-effect threshold, reports the correlation with a confidence interval alongside p and n, and supplies a Spearman coefficient as a robustness check against the ordinal dependent measure. Each of these is routinely missing from comparable submissions. Whether the power statement and the parametric choice fully hold is for the methodology seat, but the disclosure itself is what a reader needs and it is present.

### S4: The limitations section anticipates reviewer objections without special pleading

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 Limitations "Fourth, response was voluntary, so students who engage more with institutional channels may be overrepresented."
