## Review Body

Let me start with the strongest version of this manuscript, because it deserves one. A cross-sectional observational study in a single gateway statistics course, reporting a modest positive association between dashboard session counts and end-of-term completion, plus a descriptive self-report contrast on perceived control, is a legitimate and publishable contribution *if* it is stated as exactly that. The literature review is unusually self-aware: it names click-count proxies as rough indicators (Vandermeer, 2023), names the causal-language problem in the field (Ibarra, 2023), and commits the paper to "transparent about its correlational scope." Section 5, third paragraph, actually honours that commitment: "the modest size of the engagement-retention association counsels against overstatement." There is a coherent, falsifiable, modest thesis inside this paper. My objection is that the paper does not argue for that thesis. It argues for a different, much stronger one, and the two are stapled together without reconciliation.

The load-bearing break is inferential. Section 5 opens by declaring that "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and that "increasing dashboard engagement therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course"; Section 6 escalates to "raises, course retention," "dependable strategy," and "generalizable lever." Nothing in Section 4 licenses any of this. The design is explicitly cross-sectional and observational (§3.1), no manipulation of dashboard exposure occurred, no covariates were modelled, and the only reported statistics are a bivariate correlation and two t-tests. The manuscript therefore commits precisely the fallacy its own literature review indicts, and it does so while the Introduction claims the opposite of what the Discussion does: "We are careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story." One of those two passages must be withdrawn. This is not a copy-editing matter of softening a verb; the Conclusion's policy recommendation to institutions is *constituted* by the causal claim, so retracting the causal claim retracts the paper's stated contribution and forces a different Conclusion to be written.

The objection I would press hardest, however, is that even the associational claim may be an artifact of the measurement design rather than a fact about dashboards. Dashboard engagement is the count of sessions "during the term" (§3.3). Retention is coded not-retained for students "who withdrew before the final assessment" (§3.3). A student who withdrew in week 6 has, by construction, roughly six weeks in which sessions could accumulate; a student who completed has fifteen. The exposure variable is thus mechanically shortened by the outcome. An r of .24 between "how long you were enrolled, measured in clicks" and "whether you stayed enrolled" is closer to a tautology than to a finding, and the manuscript nowhere addresses this — no per-week rate, no exposure-window truncation, no censoring adjustment, no sensitivity analysis restricting sessions to a common early-term window. Until that is done, the paper's central empirical quantity has no interpretation, and neither the weak causal reading nor the modest correlational reading survives. This is repairable with data the authors already hold, which is one reason I stop short of fatal.

Second, the numbers cannot be reconciled with the sample the paper describes, and this is not one slip but a pattern. The headline correlation is r = .42 in the Abstract and r = .24 in §4.2, a 75 percent discrepancy in the paper's single most-read claim; the reported p = .004 is consistent with .24 at N = 142 and wildly inconsistent with .42, which suggests the Abstract figure is not merely a different rounding of the same analysis. The perceived-control t-test reports df = 156, which requires N = 158 in a study whose largest sample is 142 and whose perceived-control respondents number 87. The exam comparison reports t(140) = 1.31 with p = .008, when t = 1.31 at df = 140 yields p ≈ .19; the paper then describes this as failing to "reach a comparable level" while its own stated alpha of .05 would make p = .008 significant, so the text contradicts both arithmetic and its own decision rule. Table 2 sums to 127 against the sentence "All 142 students in the primary analytic sample were classified into engagement groups," and 127 is also incompatible with the df = 140 just reported. Two descriptive statistics are arithmetically unreachable: a mean of 3.847 from 87 integer responses would require a sum of 334.689, and a sample SD of 0.10 is impossible for ten integer responses on a 1–5 scale, where the smallest non-zero sample SD is 0.316. Individually these are errors; jointly they mean a reader cannot verify that any reported analysis was run on the described data, and every downstream claim inherits that doubt.

Third, the sampling account contradicts itself and then the contradiction is dropped rather than resolved. Section 3.2 states that participants were drawn "using a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" and, three sentences later, that "Students who chose to respond, and who consented to the survey, formed the study sample," with non-responders excluded. Those are different designs with different inferential warrants. The recruitment timing compounds it: an announcement posted midway through the term cannot reach students who had already withdrawn, so the analytic sample is conditioned on survival to mid-term — a selection mechanism that operates directly on the outcome variable. Neither the volunteer basis nor the mid-term truncation appears in §5.1, which lists three limitations and omits the two that bear most heavily on the central claim. This is the pattern I look for under this dimension: limitations acknowledged where they are cheap (session counts are coarse; self-report is biased) and unacknowledged where they would cost the conclusion.

Fourth, the scope of the Conclusion is unearned on the paper's own account. Section 5.1 concedes a single introductory statistics course and a dashboard whose "particular interface features may shape how students respond," and calls for future work across designs and disciplines. Section 6 then addresses "higher education institutions worldwide" and promises retention gains "across programs and disciplines." The bridge offered in §3.1 — that a required course serving many majors gives "some disciplinary breadth" — equivocates between the majors of enrolled students and the disciplinary contexts of courses; the study sampled one course, one instructor, one interface, one institution, one term. A limitation that is stated and then contradicted two pages later is worse than an unstated one, because it demonstrates the authors saw the boundary and crossed it anyway.

Fifth, the mechanism story is asserted rather than tested. The Discussion says the results "complement accounts emphasizing perceived control as a mediating construct," but no mediation analysis was performed, the mediator is a single item measured simultaneously with the predictor, and the reverse path — students who already feel in control open the dashboard more — is equally consistent with everything reported. Similarly, §2 promises to return to the equity/who-benefits question in the Discussion, and §5 claims the results "speak to an ongoing debate about who benefits from dashboards," yet no analysis stratifies by achievement, prior performance, or goal orientation. A debate about differential benefit cannot be addressed by an undifferentiated bivariate correlation.

On why this is `block` and not fatal: a coherent falsifiable thesis is identifiable in this manuscript, and a repair path exists that does not require a new study. Recompute the association with exposure held constant or censoring handled; correct or withdraw the irreconcilable statistics; state the sampling mechanism truthfully and carry volunteer plus survivorship selection into the limitations; delete every causal and policy-prescriptive sentence in §5 and §6 and replace them with the calibrated claim the paper already knows how to make in §5's third paragraph; drop the who-benefits and mediation claims or earn them with analysis. If the recomputed association survives exposure correction, a modest, honest paper remains. If it does not, the finding does not exist — which is why I regard the exposure artifact as the item the authors must resolve before anything else in this manuscript can be assessed.

Minor matters I record without escalating: nine of fifteen listed references (Ainsworth & Devi; Berange 2021; Delacroix & Ohno; Halloran; Kessler & Amadou; Montez; Prakash & Tolliver; Solberg & Whitfield; Wexler & Ojo) are never cited in the text, which inflates apparent grounding; §3.4 states that Pearson correlations were used "between continuous measures" while the retention outcome is dichotomous, so the reported r is a point-biserial coefficient under a mislabelled procedure; the enrollment denominator is given only as "several hundred students," leaving the reader unable to compute participation; no confidence intervals or standardised effect sizes accompany either t-test; and the median split is candidly described as "a coarse simplification" in §3.3 yet its output is then treated in §5 as if it identified two substantively distinct populations. I found no attempt in the manuscript to address or influence reviewers directly, and nothing in the text purported to instruct a reader on how to evaluate it.
