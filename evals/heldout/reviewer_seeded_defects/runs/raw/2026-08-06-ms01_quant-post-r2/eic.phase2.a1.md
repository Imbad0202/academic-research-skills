## Review Body

I read this submission in the seat where the journal's dashboard-effectiveness stream lands, and I have handled enough single-course deployment audits to know that the question at intake is not "is this competent?" but "what does this add that the last twelve did not?" I want to be plain about where I came out, and equally plain about which parts of my judgement are mine to make and which belong to the methodology and domain seats.

Start with what the manuscript does well, because it matters for how I read the rest. The Literature Review is genuinely even-handed: it surfaces the demotivation counter-evidence, the measurement critique, and the causal-language critique rather than assembling a supportive-citation wall. The Methods section volunteers its own design constraints instead of burying them, and §3.3 labels the median split as the compromise it is. These are the habits of authors who understand the field's epistemic problem. That is exactly why the Discussion and Conclusion are so difficult to excuse: the manuscript states the correct standard in §1 — that it will keep the pattern separate from the causal story, citing Ibarra (2023) on precisely this failure — and then, four pages later, writes that engagement "improved" retention and that dashboards are a "dependable" and "generalizable" lever for institutions worldwide. The paper indicts itself against its own declared standard and its own chosen authority. This is not a wording slip that a copy-edit repairs; it is a positioning decision, and it is the single most common reason submissions in this stream do not survive intake here.

On D6 I have scored a fatal block, and I want to justify that rather than assert it, because it carries the heaviest consequence available to me. The sub-literature that this manuscript joins — dashboard engagement correlates with an outcome in one course at one institution — is saturated. To clear this journal's bar, a paper in that space needs at least one of: a new or validated measure, a genuine mechanism test, a disconfirming result, or a dataset of a scale that changes what the field can ask. This manuscript offers none. It states in §2 that most dashboard studies infer engagement from coarse proxies "including the present one," which is a candid concession that the measurement contribution is nil. The perceived-control component is a single survey item analysed by median-split t-test; that is a descriptive crosstab, not a mechanism test, and no mediation is attempted. The result is confirmatory rather than disconfirming. And nowhere — not in the Abstract, Introduction, Discussion, or Conclusion — does any sentence state what this study adds relative to a named prior study. The decisive point is the one my fatal criterion turns on: the deficit is not repairable within the current scope. Correcting every statistic, deleting every causal verb, and rewriting the Conclusion to say "we observed r = .24 in one introductory statistics course" leaves a local audit. Repositioning cannot manufacture an increment that the data collection did not produce.

On D5 I have scored a block, and here the problem is that the manuscript cannot be reconstructed by a careful reader on its own numbers. Table 2's subgroup counts sum to 127 while the adjacent text states that all 142 students were classified and reports df = 140. The Abstract's headline correlation is r = .42 and §4.2's is r = .24. §3.2 describes a random sample in one paragraph and self-selected survey volunteers in the next. §4.3 reports df = 156 for a comparison on an item that §4.1 says 87 students answered. I am not, in this seat, recomputing test statistics — that is Reviewers 1 and 3's work, and I defer to them on whether the reported SD of 0.10 for the ten-student clarity item (N=10, M=3.00, integer 1–5 scale) is attainable, which on its face warrants their check. But an editor does not need to recompute anything to see that four separate sample-size and effect-size statements in this manuscript contradict other statements in the same manuscript. Until the authors reconcile them, no reader can tell which analysis was actually run.

On apparatus, I report what I checked and what I could not. Verified by direct inspection: all fifteen reference entries carry DOIs under the single prefix 10.5555, which is a reserved test and documentation prefix rather than a registered publisher prefix; and nine of the fifteen listed references are never cited anywhere in the text. Also verified: the in-text claim attributed to Ferro & Nakamura (2021) is the opposite of what that entry's own listed title conveys. Not verified, and I make no assertion either way: whether these sources exist. That check requires the editorial office to resolve the DOIs and the journal names against an index, and I am flagging it as a required pre-decision step rather than as an accusation. Separately, the manuscript carries no ethics approval statement, no data availability statement, and no funding or competing-interests declaration, while §3.2 discloses that students were not told their dashboard activity would be analysed. Whether that non-notification is a substantive harm rather than a paperwork gap is a question I leave to the seats that own it; the missing approval statement is mine, and it is a hold condition regardless.

### W2: Causal and generalizability claims violate the manuscript's own declared standard
**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 "We are careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story that might explain it"; §5 "The central finding of this study is that dashboard engagement improved course retention"; §6 "investing in student-facing dashboards and encouraging students to engage with them is a dependable strategy for improving retention across programs and disciplines"
**Confidence**: 5 — claim–evidence calibration is the screen I apply most often in this seat.

A cross-sectional correlation of r = .24 in one course licenses neither "improved," nor "raises," nor "dependable," nor "generalizable," nor an inference to "institutions worldwide." Uncorrected, the manuscript's stated core claim is not the claim its Results support.

### W3: Abstract's headline correlation does not match the Results section
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract "Dashboard engagement correlated positively with retention (r = .42)"; §4.2 "Dashboard engagement was positively associated with course retention (r = .24, p = .004)"

The abstract's figure is roughly 75% larger than the reported result. Whether this is a transcription error or a different analysis, the discrepancy must be resolved and the source of each number stated before any assessment of the finding is possible.

### W9: Degrees of freedom for the perceived-control comparison exceed every reported sample size
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "Students in the high-engagement group reported significantly greater perceived control than students in the low-engagement group, t(156) = 3.02, p = .003."
**Confidence**: 4 — comparison against §4.1's stated 87 item respondents and §3.2's 142-student analytic sample; formal recomputation is the methodology seat's.

df = 156 implies N = 158, larger than both the 87 respondents who answered the item and the 142-student log sample. The paper's second headline finding is therefore unreconstructable as reported.

### W10: Prose characterisation of the exam comparison contradicts the statistic printed beside it
**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "A parallel comparison on final exam performance did not reach a comparable level."; "but the difference was small, t(140) = 1.31, p = .008"
**Confidence**: 4 — internal inconsistency is plain; the t/p compatibility check belongs to the methodology seat.

At the stated alpha of .05, p = .008 is significant, which is not what the surrounding prose says, and the pairing of t = 1.31 with p = .008 at df = 140 warrants recomputation. This is a secondary comparison, so the core retention claim is untouched by it.

### W11: Reporting precision and effect-size conventions do not meet venue expectations
**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: table: Table 1, Descriptive statistics for primary measures, Perceived control row reporting M = 3.847 for a single-item 1-5 integer scale
**Confidence**: 4 — routine venue-convention check.

Three-decimal precision on a single-item ordinal measure is spurious, and no confidence intervals or standardised effect sizes accompany any reported test. Both are expected in this journal's reporting format.
