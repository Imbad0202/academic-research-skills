### D5: writing_and_structure

score: block
trigger: "numbers appearing in text that no table or figure supports"

The manuscript's organisation follows venue convention and its prose is generally clear, so this is not a block on style. It is a block because the reported quantities cannot be reconciled with one another or with the exhibits that are supposed to carry them: the Abstract's headline correlation is not the one the Results report, the perceived-control comparison is reported on degrees of freedom that no described sample can produce, Table 2's group sizes contradict both the text's stated N and the reported degrees of freedom of the same test, and Table 1 carries a measure that Section 3.3 never defines. D5 is normal priority; on my Phase 1 reading, a block here is a revision requirement, not a ground for rejection.

### D6: venue_fit_and_contribution

score: block
block_class: repairable
trigger: "generalised claims resting on a single narrow deployment"

The topic is squarely in this journal's scope, and the Introduction's promise to "distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story" is exactly the discipline this venue asks for. That promise does not survive Section 5. The Discussion opens by asserting that engagement "improved" retention and that increasing engagement "raises the probability" of completion; the Conclusion escalates to a "dependable strategy" for institutions "worldwide" and a "generalizable lever," from one term, one course, one self-selected sample. That is the block trigger as written. I withhold `fatal`: my fatal condition concerns scope and absence of any increment, and a correctly reframed descriptive report of a modest association with transparent sampling would retain a small but real increment for this readership. The consent defect recorded as W3 is a separate matter that may bar publication independently of the contribution judgement; that weighing belongs to the panel, not to this dimension's trigger.

## Review Body

I read this submission as the handling editor. My question is not whether the study is interesting but whether this journal could put its name to the claims as stated, and whether the manuscript meets the submission requirements our readers assume we enforce.

The paper is well constructed up to the end of Section 4.1 and then comes apart in two places at once. First, the epistemic discipline the Introduction and Section 2 promise — including a citation to our own published audit of causal language in correlational learning analytics — is abandoned in the Discussion and Conclusion, where an r of .24 in one course becomes a dependable, worldwide, generalizable retention lever. If we published this, it would be cited as evidence that dashboards cause retention. That is the precise inferential slippage our critical literature exists to resist, and it is why D6 blocks.

Second, the numbers do not reconcile. I do not treat effect-size verification as my seat's primary work, and I expect the methodology reviewer to go deeper, but several of these discrepancies are visible to any attentive reader: the Abstract and Section 4.2 report different correlations for the same central result; a t-test is reported at df = 156 when the largest described sample is 142 and the item's respondent pool is 87; Table 2's group sizes sum to 127 against a stated 142 and df = 140; and a t of 1.31 on 140 df is reported at p = .008 while the surrounding sentence calls the difference not reliable. A copy-editor would miss these. A reader will not.

On novelty, the manuscript never states its increment. Our readership already treats "dashboard use covaries with persistence" as a working assumption, and the perceived-control component as executed — one item, cross-sectional, split at the median, no mediation model — restates the self-regulated learning premise the paper begins from rather than testing it. There is a publishable descriptive contribution here if the claims are pulled back to the design and the increment is argued against the named prior studies, but that is substantial reframing, not copy-editing.

Finally, and against my own instinct to read it as a disclosure omission: Section 3.2 states that students were not informed their dashboard activity data would be analysed. Consent covered the survey only, while the log data carry the paper's central finding. I do not consider this repairable by adding a sentence, and I flag it for the panel as potentially dispositive regardless of how the contribution question resolves. The absence of ethics approval, data availability, funding, and conflict-of-interest statements compounds it.

### S1: Literature review engages the counter-evidence, including against itself

The review does not curate a supportive literature. It reports the demotivation findings, the click-proxy critique, and the causal-language audit, and applies the proxy critique to the present study by name. This is the register the rest of the manuscript should have sustained.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 — "Most dashboard studies, including the present one, infer engagement from coarse behavioral proxies such as page views or session counts."
**Confidence**: 5 — routine editorial assessment of literature framing.

### S2: Measurement decisions are stated rather than hidden

The sessionization rule, the dichotomous retention coding, and the median split are each specified, and the median split is explicitly labelled a coarse simplification adopted for interpretability. Self-flagging a weak analytic choice is more than most submissions in this area do.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.3 — "This median split is a coarse simplification of a continuous measure and was adopted for interpretability rather than statistical efficiency."
**Confidence**: 4 — based on comparison with submissions I handle in this area.

### S3: Conventional structure with a locatable research question

Section order matches venue convention, section lengths are proportionate to the work reported, and the research question is stated plainly at the close of the Introduction rather than left for the reader to reconstruct.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 — "Our central question is whether students who engage more with a learning analytics dashboard are more likely to persist"
**Confidence**: 5 — direct structural inspection.

### W1: The Abstract and the Results report different values for the paper's central quantity

The Abstract reports r = .42; Section 4.2 reports r = .24, p = .004. No table or figure supports either value, and nothing in the manuscript indicates which is correct. The Abstract is the part of a published paper that circulates, and .42 versus .24 is the difference between a moderate association and a small one. Uncorrected, the paper publishes a false central quantity, so I band this by that consequence rather than by the ease of the fix.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract and §4.2 — "Dashboard engagement correlated positively with retention (r = .42)" versus "positively associated with course retention (r = .24, p = .004)"
**Confidence**: 5 — direct textual comparison, no inference required.

### W2: Causal and generalization claims in Sections 5 and 6 exceed the design and contradict the paper's own stated care

Section 5 opens with "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and states that increasing engagement "raises the probability" of completion. Section 6 escalates to institutions "worldwide," a "dependable strategy," and a "generalizable lever." The design is cross-sectional, single-course, single-term, and self-selected, with no comparison condition and no adjustment for the obvious confound that students already on track to complete are the students who open a progress dashboard. This is the claim–evidence calibration failure that governs my venue-fit judgement, and on its own it is rejection-level as submitted.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 and §6 — "dashboard engagement improved course retention" and "a dependable strategy for improving retention across programs and disciplines"
**Confidence**: 5 — claim-to-design comparison is core editorial competence.

### W3: Behavioral log data analysed without informing the students it describes

Section 3.2 states that consent was obtained for the survey and that students were not informed their dashboard activity data would be analysed for the study. The log data are the source of the paper's primary finding. This is a substantive consent problem, not a reporting gap: it cannot be cured retrospectively by adding a statement, and it bears on whether the primary analytic dataset is publishable at all in this venue.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 — "Students were not informed that their dashboard activity data would be analyzed for this study."
**Confidence**: 4 — confident on the editorial-policy implication; the ethics-committee status of the deployment is not recoverable from the text.

### W4: The perceived-control comparison is reported on a sample that cannot exist

Section 4.3 reports t(156) = 3.02, p = .003 for the high- versus low-engagement contrast on perceived control. Degrees of freedom of 156 imply 158 cases in an independent-samples test, while the primary analytic sample is 142 and Section 4.1 states that 87 respondents answered the item. Group sizes for this comparison are never reported and no table carries it. Either the test statistic or the sample description is wrong; until that is resolved, the paper's second core claim cannot be checked. I band this Major rather than Critical because a correctly reported test on the correct 87 respondents could still sustain the claim.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 and §4.1 — "t(156) = 3.02, p = .003" against "the 87 survey respondents who answered the item"
**Confidence**: 4 — arithmetic is elementary; the correct denominator is a matter for the methodology seat.

### W5: Required submission statements are absent

The manuscript carries no ethics or IRB approval statement, no data availability statement, and no funding or conflict-of-interest declarations. For a study built on institutional LMS trace data these are not formalities; the ethics statement is the document that would ordinarily resolve W3 one way or the other, and its absence is why W3 has to be read at face value.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: manuscript front and back matter — expected ethics/IRB approval, data availability, funding, and conflict-of-interest statements; checked §1-§6, the §3.2 recruitment paragraph, table captions, and References
**Confidence**: 5 — checked against this journal's submission requirements.

### W6: Table 2's group sizes contradict the text and the reported test

Section 4.3 states that all 142 students in the primary analytic sample were classified into engagement groups and reports t(140), which implies 142 cases. Table 2 lists 66 and 61, totalling 127. The exhibit that is supposed to document subgroup composition instead disagrees with the analysis it documents, leaving the reader unable to determine who was compared.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: table: Table 2, n column (66 + 61 = 127) versus §4.3 "All 142 students in the primary analytic sample" and t(140)
**Confidence**: 5 — arithmetic on the printed table.

### W7: The exam-comparison p-value is inconsistent with its own test statistic and with the surrounding prose

Section 4.3 reports t(140) = 1.31, p = .008 while describing the difference as small and as not reaching a comparable level of reliability. A t of 1.31 on 140 df is nowhere near p = .008, and the value as printed would be the paper's most significant result — contradicting the sentence that contains it and the moderating reading offered in Section 5. Whichever number is wrong, the exam comparison must be re-reported before the Discussion's calibration language can be assessed.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 — "the difference was small, t(140) = 1.31, p = .008"
**Confidence**: 4 — the inconsistency is unambiguous; the correct value is the methodology seat's determination.

### W8: The sampling account describes two mutually exclusive samples

Section 3.2 first states that participants were drawn using a random sample of enrolled students, then describes an LMS announcement to which students chose to respond, with non-respondents excluded. These are not compatible descriptions, and the difference is decisive for every claim in Sections 5 and 6: a volunteer sample of students who answered a mid-term survey about their study habits is selected on close to the same disposition the study treats as its outcome. As written, the reader cannot establish who was studied.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.2 — "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" versus "Students who chose to respond, and who consented to the survey, formed the study sample"
**Confidence**: 5 — direct reading of the recruitment paragraph.

### W9: No stated increment over the prior work the paper itself cites

The Introduction says the study "contributes to this literature by examining the association" and never says what is new relative to Calloway, Ferro and Nakamura, Rutledge and Berange, Vandermeer, or Ibarra. The perceived-control component is offered as the distinguishing element, but as executed — one item, cross-sectional, median split, no mediation test — it re-describes the self-regulated learning premise the paper starts from rather than putting it at risk. For this readership, "dashboard use covaries with persistence" is not itself news; the increment has to be argued, and currently the reader is left to infer it.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §1 and §5 — expected an explicit statement of the increment over named prior studies; checked the §1 contribution paragraph, §2 Literature Review, §5 Discussion, and §6 Conclusion
**Confidence**: 4 — based on the current state of this journal's published dashboard literature.

### W10: Table 1 reports a measure the Methods never define

Table 1 includes final exam score (0-100), and Section 4.3 builds a group comparison on it, but Section 3.3 defines only dashboard engagement, retention, perceived control, and the secondary clarity item. Retention is defined as completing the final assessment, which is not the same variable as a score on it. An exhibit carrying a variable the Measures section does not introduce forces the reader to reconstruct the operationalization.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: table: Table 1, row "Final exam score (0-100)" with no corresponding definition in §3.3 Measures
**Confidence**: 5 — direct cross-check of Table 1 against §3.3.

### W11: The secondary clarity item is presented as an unedited data note, and its dispersion is not attainable

Section 4.1 reports the secondary item as a run of raw values rather than as prose or a table, its analytic purpose is never stated, and the reported combination cannot occur: with ten integer responses on a 1-5 scale and a mean of exactly 3.00, any nonzero dispersion is far above 0.10. I band this Minor because no claim in the paper rests on the item; the methodology seat may reasonably weigh it more heavily as part of a wider pattern of unchecked reporting, which is a judgement for that seat rather than this finding.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.1 — "the reported secondary-item values were N=10; M=3.00; sample SD=0.10; integer scale=1-5"
**Confidence**: 4 — elementary bound on the standard deviation of integer scores.
