## Review Body

Taking the editorial chair on this submission, my first judgement is the easy one: the topic sits squarely inside this journal's remit. Internal QA implementation and the quality-culture construct are core business here, and the manuscript's own citation of Iversen (2021) — published in this journal — confirms the author knows the house conversation. The administrator-centred angle is also genuinely under-served: the literature review is right that Silva and Tan (2021) leave the intermediary position conceptual, and a grounded account of the people who actually run review cycles is something a QA-practitioner readership could use. The interview material is good. "It's theater, and everyone knows it's theater" and "The forms are the easy part" are the kind of lines that get quoted in later work, and the identity-work theme is more than a restatement of the tired "QA is ritualistic" argument — it treats the compliance–meaning gap as productive rather than merely lamentable. There is a publishable paper in here.

That said, the manuscript as submitted does not deliver what it advertises, and the gap is not rhetorical trimming. Section 6 claims "the first comprehensive account" of administrator experience of QA implementation. Section 2 concedes, two pages earlier, that Pettersen (2022) and Rahman (2020) are "closest in spirit" and that they already examine distributed leadership and professional identity in the quality office — which are two of this paper's three themes. The manuscript never states what it adds to those two studies, never compares its findings against theirs, and never distinguishes its account from Silva and Tan's. A twelve-interview, three-site, single-system study is in no sense comprehensive. This is why D6 blocks rather than warns: the contribution is real, but the novelty claim currently rests on assertion where positioning is required, and a reader of this journal who has already read Pettersen and Rahman will ask what is new and find no answer in the text. The fix is available within one revision cycle — drop "first" and "comprehensive", state the specific increment over the three adjacent studies, and let the identity-work reading carry the originality — which is why I classify the block as repairable rather than fatal. The paper is inside the remit and does have an identifiable original contribution.

On my own turf of exposition, the manuscript reads well at sentence level and its section architecture is conventional and signposted, but it fails on reconstruction. The abstract says fourteen administrators were interviewed; section 3.2 says twelve. A reader cannot determine the study's basic sample size from the paper itself. The survey is described as 48 respondents, yet the only reported analysis compares 9 against 11, and the remaining 28 are never accounted for or attributed to the third site. There is no instrument, no scale range, no anchors, and no item count, so M=3.9 is a number without units. There is not a single table or figure in a paper that reports a mean, a standard deviation, and a significance test. Section 3.1 promises semi-structured interviews; section 4.1 attributes systematic theme emergence to "the structured protocol". Individually these are copy-editing matters; together they mean the manuscript cannot be audited by the reader, which is the condition my block trigger describes.

I want to be explicit about which shortfalls are gate-blocking and which are developmental, since that is what determines whether this returns for one revision cycle or not at all. Gate-blocking, in my judgement: the excluded dissenting participants paired with the claim of balanced representation (W3); the re-identifiable participant descriptions set against a stated guarantee of full anonymisation (W4); the reversal of Delacroix's argument in the Discussion (W2); and the sample-count contradiction (W1). None of these can be resolved by argument in a response letter — they require the data, the ethics position, and the source to be reconciled. Developmental: the reference-list annotation, the analysis-plan mismatch, and the protocol-description inconsistency.

Two of those deserve a further word from the chair. Section 4.3 states that the study "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views" and then, in the next sentence, that dissenting participants were excluded because they "fell outside the three-theme structure". Removing disconfirming cases in order to preserve a thematic frame, and then claiming full coverage, is not a presentational slip; it inverts the relationship between data and analysis. Separately, section 3.5's anonymisation guarantee is broken in section 4.1 by "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" — within a three-site design that names each site's type, that phrase identifies one individual. I would not send this to production in its present form regardless of the other scores.

Finally, the construct that gives the paper its title is never operationalised. "Quality culture" is defined discursively in section 2 as shared values, practices, and commitments, then measured in section 4.4 as a single mean with no instrument behind it. What that mean captures is staff sentiment, and this readership will read the paper as conflating culture with satisfaction unless the author either supplies the instrument with evidence of its construct coverage or reframes the quantitative strand explicitly as a sentiment check that cannot speak to culture. I do not accept the "supplementary" label as covering this: the survey is used in the Discussion to make a substantive claim about level-dependent variation in quality culture, so it must meet reporting standards.
