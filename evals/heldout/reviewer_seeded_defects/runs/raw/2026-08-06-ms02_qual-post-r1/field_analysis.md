# Field Analysis Report

- **Abstract length**: ~150 words
- **Full text length**: ~2,600 words (excluding references) — unusually short for a mixed-methods empirical paper aimed at a mainstream journal
- **Number of references**: 12

| **Target Journal Tier** | **Aspiration: Q1–Q2.** The framing, citation targets (*Higher Education Quarterly*, *Quality in Higher Education*, *Teaching in Higher Education*) and conceptual ambition are pitched at mainstream international field journals. **Current execution: Q3 or desk-reject at Q1–Q2.** Rationale: methods section is roughly 350 words with no analytic framework named, no trustworthiness/rigour procedures, no survey instrument description, no sampling logic; the reference base is thin (12 sources) and omits the canonical quality-culture literature; and there are several internal contradictions and at least one apparent participant re-identification. These are gate-level, not cosmetic, problems. |
| **Paper Maturity** | **Revised draft.** Structure is complete, prose is polished, references are consistently formatted with DOIs, and the argument has a clear arc — this is not a first draft. But the self-consistency failures (participant count, subgroup arithmetic, a source cited for the opposite of what the same paper's literature review attributes to it) and the missing methodological substrate mean it is not pre-submission ready. |

**Configuration-relevant observations** (raw signals for the review team; not review verdicts):
- Participant *n* differs between abstract (14) and §3.2 (12).
- §4.1 attributes a quote to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" — a potentially re-identifying descriptor in a 3-institution study claiming full anonymization (§3.5).
- §2 states Delacroix (2018) argues *against* consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture; §5 cites Delacroix (2018) as *recommending* consultation as the central mechanism. The annotated reference entry supports §2.
- §4.1 says themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol"; §3.1 describes the protocol as semi-structured.
- §4.3 claims "balanced representation … capturing the full range of administrator views" in the same paragraph that reports excluding dissenting participants.
- §4.4 reports p<.05 from n=9 vs n=11; the three institutional subgroups are not reconciled to the total of 48.
- §5 and §6 generalize from three institutions to "universities across the sector" / "the higher education sector as a whole," and claim "the first comprehensive account."
- All 12 DOIs share the 10.5555 test/placeholder prefix and a sequential suffix pattern (1042001–1042012). The domain reviewer should verify whether these references are real and retrievable before treating the literature base as evidence.

## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Quality in Higher Education** — The closest topical match. The journal's core readership is exactly the QA-practitioner/scholar audience this paper addresses, and it publishes qualitative multi-site institutional studies. Also the most likely to detect the paper's misalignment with the established quality-culture canon (Harvey & Stensaker, EUA Quality Culture project lineage), which the manuscript does not engage.
2. **Journal of Higher Education Policy and Management** — Strong fit for the administrator-as-intermediary framing (the paper's own Silva & Tan reference sits here). Receptive to practitioner-facing governance work and tolerant of modest sample sizes if the analytic logic is transparent. Would demand far more national/regulatory context than the manuscript currently supplies.
3. **Higher Education Quarterly** — Plausible if the theoretical contribution is sharpened; HEQ rewards conceptual payoff over sample scale. Currently the paper's theoretical claim ("negotiation, not output") is asserted rather than developed against a named framework, which is the main barrier here.

*Fallback if the empirical base cannot be strengthened*: **Tertiary Education and Management** or a regional/practitioner QA outlet, reframed honestly as an exploratory three-site study.

## Reviewer Configuration Cards

### Reviewer Configuration Card #1

**Role**: EIC
**Display role**: Journal-Fit Reviewer
**Identity Description**: Senior Associate Editor of *Quality in Higher Education*, specializing in internal quality assurance systems and the quality-culture construct; formerly coordinated a multi-country institutional review project on internal QA under a European quality-assurance network, and has handled roughly 200 submissions on quality culture over eight years.
**Review Focus**:
1. **Fit and audience**: Does the paper deliver something a QA-practitioner readership can use, or is it a generic "QA is ritualistic" restatement? Assess whether the administrator-centred angle is genuinely under-served in this journal's recent output, given that Pettersen (2022), Rahman (2020) and Silva & Tan (2021) — all cited — already occupy adjacent ground.
2. **Novelty claims versus delivered contribution**: Interrogate "the first comprehensive account" (§6) and "first" framing against the paper's own literature review, which concedes that Pettersen and Rahman are "closest in spirit." A three-site, 12–14-participant study is not a comprehensive account.
3. **Publishability trajectory**: Is this a major-revision paper or a desk reject? Judge whether the shortfalls are fixable within one revision cycle, and state explicitly which shortfalls are gate-blocking (participant count, source misattribution, re-identification) versus developmental.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether "quality culture" is given a defensible operational definition rather than being used interchangeably with staff sentiment — the survey collapses "institutional quality culture" into a single mean (M=3.9) with no instrument, which the journal's readership will read as conflating culture with satisfaction.
**Possible blind spots**: May under-scrutinize the statistical subgroup claim and the survey's psychometric absence, treating the quantitative strand as decorative because the paper labels it "supplementary." May also accept the three-theme structure as adequate because it is familiar within the QA field.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Identity Description**: Mixed-methods researcher in education, trained in qualitative rigour traditions (Lincoln & Guba trustworthiness criteria, reflexive thematic analysis à la Braun & Clarke) and competent in applied inferential statistics; teaches a doctoral seminar on integration in mixed-methods designs and regularly reviews for *Journal of Mixed Methods Research*.
**Review Focus**:
1. **Sample and data integrity**: Reconcile n=14 (abstract) against n=12 (§3.2); reconcile the survey subgroups (n=9 + n=11 + third institution) against the stated total of 48. Ask why participant counts are not reported by institution and role, and whether saturation was assessed or merely asserted ("until a stable structure was reached").
2. **Analytic transparency and case exclusion**: Which thematic analysis tradition? Who coded, with what codebook, at what level of agreement? Then the central issue: §4.3 reports excluding dissenting participants "for space" because they "fell outside the three-theme structure," while simultaneously claiming the analysis "captur[ed] the full range of administrator views." Negative-case analysis is not optional in thematic work; discarding disconfirming data to preserve a theme structure inverts the logic of qualitative inference and invalidates the claim that themes "emerged." Also flag the §4.1/§3.1 contradiction (structured vs semi-structured protocol) and the illegitimate inference that a fixed protocol rules out site-specific artefact.
3. **Quantitative strand and integration**: The survey has no described instrument, no item set, no reliability estimate, no response rate, no scale anchors — yet a mean and SD are reported to one decimal and a p<.05 claim is made from n=9 vs n=11. Demand the test used, effect size, assumption checks, and a defensible position on whether any inferential test is warranted at that cell size. Separately: there is no integration mechanism (no joint display, no explicit sequence logic), so the design is currently two parallel studies stapled together, not mixed methods. Note also that the survey is described as "corroborat[ing]" the qualitative picture in §4.4 while the same paragraph says it contradicted expectations — corroboration and disconfirmation cannot both be claimed.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the data as reported can bear the weight of the conclusions at all — specifically whether "quality culture" was measured or merely scored, and whether any causal or moderating language ("institutional type as a possible moderator") is licensed by a cross-sectional descriptive survey with tiny cells.
**Possible blind spots**: May be indifferent to whether the quality-culture literature is correctly represented, and may not notice the Delacroix misattribution or the missing canonical field sources. May also treat the re-identification issue as an ethics-committee matter outside methodological remit — the synthesizer should ensure it is not dropped.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #3

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Identity Description**: Professor of higher education policy and governance, twenty years working on external QA regimes and internal quality systems across European and East Asian jurisdictions; has served on national accreditation panels and published comparative work on how regulatory design shapes institutional QA behaviour.
**Review Focus**:
1. **Source fidelity**: Systematically check whether cited sources are used as their arguments actually run. The clearest failure: §2 correctly reports Delacroix (2018) as arguing *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture — consistent with the annotated reference entry — yet §5 recruits Delacroix as *recommending* consultation as "the engine of internalization." The paper's own third contribution therefore rests on an inverted citation, and the tension the introduction promised to animate the study is silently resolved by misreading the dissenting source. Also verify whether the reference list is real: the uniform 10.5555 DOI prefix and sequential numbering are consistent with placeholder rather than retrievable sources, and if so the entire literature foundation must be rebuilt.
2. **Literature coverage and theoretical framing**: A quality-culture paper with 12 references omits the field's foundational work on quality culture as a dual structural/cultural construct and the European Quality Culture project lineage, as well as the audit-culture scholarship that underwrites the "ritual" theme. Assess whether "quality culture" is theorized at all, or used as a label. The paper announces the concept is "under-theorized at the level of everyday administrative practice" and then does not theorize it either.
3. **Context and generalization**: The national system, regulatory regime, and QA cycle are never specified, which makes the findings uninterpretable for policy readers and untestable by others — while §5 leaps from three unnamed institutions to "universities across the sector" and "the higher education sector as a whole." Require either the contextual detail or the retraction of the sector-level claim; note that the two demands interact, since anonymity is being used to avoid specification while generality is being claimed without warrant.

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the paper distinguishes external QA compliance from internal quality management from quality culture, or lets all three float together — the conflation is what allows a documentation complaint to be presented as a cultural finding.
**Possible blind spots**: May accept the three-theme structure and the interview quotes at face value without probing how themes were derived or which cases were dropped. May also be untroubled by the statistical subgroup claim, dismissing the survey as peripheral.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #4

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Identity Description**: Organizational sociologist studying institutions and professions — decoupling and ceremonial conformity in regulated organizations, institutional logics, and identity work among professional staff; also chairs a university research ethics committee and has published on confidentiality risk in small-N elite interviewing. Reads higher education as one field among several regulated sectors.
**Review Focus**:
1. **The unnamed theory**: All three findings are well-established organizational phenomena that the paper rediscovers without naming. "Ritual compliance" is textbook institutional decoupling and ceremonial conformity; "distributed leadership" is coalition-building across institutional logics; "identity work" has a mature literature on how professionals sustain commitment inside contradictory demands. Because the paper does not engage this work, it cannot say what is *specific* to higher education QA versus generic to any audited profession — and it therefore cannot deliver the theoretical contribution §5–§6 claim. Ask the authors what a hospital accreditation officer or a financial compliance officer would find surprising here.
2. **Participant confidentiality as a live harm, not a formality**: §3.5 asserts full anonymization such that "no individual could be identified." §4.1 then identifies a speaker as "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" — in a study with exactly one private university — and a second as "the associate dean for quality at the public research university." In institutions with a single such post, these descriptors are identifying, and the attributed quotes are critical of the participants' own employers ("It's theater, and everyone knows it's theater"). This is a concrete employment risk to named-by-description individuals and a direct contradiction of the consent basis. Require removal of institution-linked role descriptors and a statement of how quote-level disclosure risk was assessed.
3. **Whose account is missing, and who can use this**: The paper argues quality culture is negotiated among administrators, academics, and external bodies, then evidences only administrators, with academics present solely as an object of persuasion. Assess whether the negotiation claim can stand on one party's testimony. Then the practice question: the recommendations ("processes that create room for meaning-making") are unactionable at the level of any actual QA process, cycle, or instrument — what would a quality director change on Monday?

**Will particularly care about**: Whether the study's own analytic move — excluding dissenters to preserve a tidy three-theme narrative, then reporting a favourable survey mean as corroboration — enacts the very performativity it critiques. If so, that is worth naming plainly, because it bears on the credibility of the whole account.
**Possible blind spots**: Unfamiliar with the internal debates and canonical citations of the QA field, so may propose organizational-theory framings that the higher education literature has already domesticated; may also underweight journal-fit and audience considerations.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

**Special characteristics requiring attention**

- **This paper's failures are load-bearing, not cosmetic.** Three problems each independently undermine a stated contribution: the Delacroix inversion voids the paper's third discussion claim; the exclusion of dissenting cases voids the claim that themes emerged and that representation was balanced; and the sector-wide generalization from three anonymous institutions voids the second discussion claim. Reviewers should be directed to trace each contribution claim back to its evidence rather than listing surface defects.
- **Register: developmental, verdict: unchanged.** Given the manuscript's maturity (revised draft with a real argument and clean prose), reviewers should write constructively and specify what a successful revision looks like. Tone adjusts the wording; it must not soften the recommendation. On the evidence available, the honest recommendation is major revision at best, and the Journal-Fit Reviewer should be free to argue for reject-and-resubmit at Q1–Q2 targets.
- **Reference verifiability is a threshold question.** Every DOI shares the 10.5555 placeholder prefix with sequential suffixes. Reviewer 2 must resolve whether the literature base is real before any judgment about literature coverage is meaningful. If the sources are placeholders, that finding dominates all others and should be reported as unverified-pending-check rather than asserted.
- **Ethics has a specific victim, not an abstract issue.** The re-identification problem is not a missing-boilerplate complaint; it is a contradiction between §3.5's consent claim and §4.1's disclosures, attached to critical quotes. Route it to Reviewer 3 with the confidentiality expertise, and ensure the synthesizer keeps it visible even if Reviewer 1 treats it as out of scope.

**Complementarity and tension across the team**

- **Designed non-overlap**: Journal-Fit = fit, novelty claims, publishability trajectory. R1 = design, data integrity, analytic transparency, inference. R2 = source fidelity, literature canon, context and generalization. R3 = theoretical framing from outside the field, participant confidentiality, missing voices, practice utility.
- **Expected productive tension 1**: R2 will want more contextual specification (national system, regulatory regime, institutional detail); R3 will want *less* identifying detail to protect participants. This is a genuine design conflict, not a disagreement to be averaged away. The synthesizer should present it as a decision the authors must resolve — likely by specifying the regulatory regime while stripping institution-linked role descriptors — rather than picking a side.
- **Expected productive tension 2**: R3 will argue the findings are generic to audited professions; the Journal-Fit Reviewer will judge them from within the QA field's own conversation. Preserve both; the gap between them is precisely the "so what is specific to higher education?" question the paper needs to answer.
- **Coverage risk to monitor**: the p<.05 subgroup claim sits only with R1, and the Delacroix inversion sits only with R2. If either reviewer produces a thin report, the synthesizer must pick these up directly rather than letting them fall through.
