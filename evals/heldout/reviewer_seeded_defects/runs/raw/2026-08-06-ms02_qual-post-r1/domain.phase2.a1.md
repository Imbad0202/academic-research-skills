## Review Body

I read this manuscript as a higher education policy and governance specialist, checking whether its sources are used as their arguments actually run, whether the field's core constructs are used with their established meanings, and whether its factual claims about quality assurance regimes are correct and checkable. My scope is D2 only.

The paper has a real subject. Administrators occupying the hinge between an external review regime and departmental practice are genuinely under-studied, and the identity-work material in §4.3 is the kind of evidence the field lacks. That makes the accuracy failures more, not less, consequential: the substantive contribution is being carried on a literature apparatus and a construct definition that do not hold.

Three problems are load-bearing. First, the manuscript's twelve references all carry DOIs on the 10.5555 prefix — the reserved test prefix used in DOI documentation examples, not a registrant prefix — and the suffixes run in a strict sequence, 1042001 through 1042012, matching the alphabetical order of the reference list. Twelve articles issued by ten different journals and one monograph publisher cannot receive consecutively numbered DOIs from a single prefix. No entry in this list resolves to a retrievable record, and no author-title-journal combination in it corresponds to work I recognise in a field I have worked in for twenty years. Every domain claim in §2 — and therefore the paper's entire positioning — rests on this apparatus. Second, the manuscript inverts the argument of the one source whose dissent it says animates the study: §2 and the reference annotation both state that Delacroix (2018) argues against treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture, and §5 then recruits Delacroix as recommending consultation as the central mechanism. Third, "quality culture" is defined in §1 in a form that drops half the construct as the field has developed it, and the paper's headline claim is an artefact of that truncation.

On terminology: the manuscript lets external QA compliance, internal quality management, and quality culture float together as a single object called "QA." That conflation is what allows a complaint about evidence portfolios produced for a review panel to be reported as a finding about institutional culture. These are three distinct constructs with distinct referents in this literature, and the paper's central inference depends on the slide between them.

On context: the national system, the regulatory regime type, the responsible agency, and the position in the review cycle are never stated. For a policy readership this is not a stylistic omission — accreditation regimes, audit regimes, and evaluative-state regimes generate materially different administrator behaviour, and without knowing which is in play a reader cannot judge whether the "theatre" account is a general phenomenon or an artefact of one regime design. The paper then compounds the omission by generalising from three unnamed institutions to the sector as a whole. Anonymity is doing double duty here: it excuses specification while generality is claimed without warrant.

Three matters fall outside my dimension and I hand them to the seats that own them rather than scoring them. The abstract reports fourteen interviewees and §3.2 reports twelve; §4.1 says themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" while §3.1 describes semi-structured interviews and §3.4 describes iterative inductive coding; §4.3 claims "balanced representation" and "the full range of administrator views" in the sentence before it reports that dissenting participants were excluded. Those are for the methodology and discourse seats. I also note without scoring that §3.5 asserts data were anonymised "so that no individual could be identified," while §4.1 identifies a speaker as the quality director of the largest private university in the region — an ethics-conduct question for the methodology seat. The p<.05 subgroup contrast on n=9 versus n=11 is likewise not mine, though I record that I did not treat the survey as peripheral: it is the only quantitative warrant offered for institutional type as a moderator.

### S1: Accurate and precise reading of the dissenting source in the literature review
The §2 characterisation of the participation-ritual critique is exactly right as that argument runs in this literature: consultation can be performed and documented without any transfer of ownership, and the consultation record then becomes one more artefact for the panel. Setting that against the participatory-cultivation strand is the correct framing of the field's live disagreement, and it is the framing a paper on this topic should adopt.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "an institution can hold elaborate consultations and still lack any genuine ownership of standards"
**Confidence**: 5 — this debate is the core of my own comparative work on QA regime design.

### S2: Identity work as a coping resource is a genuine and under-evidenced domain observation
The reading in §4.3 — that vocational framing lets administrators hold the compliance/meaning gap open rather than resolving it, and that professional identity is located in the gap itself — is consistent with what the professionalisation-of-QA literature predicts but rarely evidences in practitioners' own words. This is the part of the paper that would be worth publishing if the foundations were sound.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "The paperwork is the price of admission, but it's not the point."
**Confidence**: 4 — based on familiarity with quality-professional role literature and national panel work.

### S3: Correct identification of the intermediary actor position
The paper is right that QA administrators are analytically distinct from both the accrediting body and the resisting academic, and that this position has been described conceptually more often than it has been evidenced. That is an accurate statement about where the field's gap actually sits.
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "They are neither the accreditors imposing standards nor the academics resisting them, but the actors who must reconcile the two daily."
**Confidence**: 5 — direct knowledge of the intermediary-role literature and of national accreditation practice.

### W1: §5 inverts the argument of Delacroix (2018), and the paper's third contribution rests on the inversion
The manuscript states the source correctly twice — in §2 and in the annotated reference entry, both of which report Delacroix as arguing against treating consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture — and then, in §5, cites the same source as recommending consultation as the central mechanism for building quality culture and calls consultation "the engine of internalization." This is not a debatable reading; it is the opposite of the argument the manuscript itself reproduces. Two consequences follow. The paper's third design recommendation has no source support and, if the source were read as written, would point the other way: a QA process that maximises consultation is precisely what Delacroix identifies as the sophisticated form of ritual. And the tension the introduction promises to animate the study is not resolved by the evidence but dissolved by misreading the dissenting party, which removes the paper's stated reason for existing. A related and smaller error sits in the same source's treatment: §2 also lists Delacroix in the governance-technology strand alongside Harlow, which does not match the participation-ritual argument attributed to him three paragraphs later.
**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture"; §2 "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual"
**Confidence**: 5 — the contradiction is internal to the manuscript and requires no external verification.

### W2: The entire reference apparatus carries reserved test-prefix DOIs in sequential order and cannot be verified
All twelve entries use the 10.5555 prefix, which is the DOI Foundation's test prefix used in handbook examples rather than a prefix assigned to any publisher, and the suffixes increment by one in alphabetical order of first author. Real DOIs for works published by Meridian Academic Press, *Higher Education Quarterly*, *Quality in Higher Education*, *Teaching in Higher Education*, and *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* would carry different registrant prefixes and non-sequential suffixes. Several of the journals named exist; the author-title combinations attributed to them do not correspond to work I can identify, and two of the outlets — *Journal of Higher Education Governance* and *Studies in Quality Assurance* — I cannot place at all. The reference list also carries an inline editorial annotation on the Delacroix entry, which is not a reference-list element in any style this venue would use and suggests the apparatus is a working placeholder rather than a finished citation set. The consequence is that no claim in §2 is checkable, and §2 is where every domain positioning claim in the paper is made. This is the basis for my fatal designation, and it is falsifiable in one step: if the authors supply resolvable DOIs or verifiable bibliographic records for all twelve works, this finding retracts, and the remaining findings fall back to a repairable block.
**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: References "https://doi.org/10.5555/1042001" (Aoki 2019) through "https://doi.org/10.5555/1042012" (Silva & Tan 2021)
**Confidence**: 4 — DOI registration practice is verifiable from the manuscript text; I cannot resolve identifiers from within this review.

### W3: "Quality culture" is defined with its structural component removed, and the headline claim depends on that removal
The §1 definition reduces quality culture to shared values, practices, and commitments. As the construct has been developed in this field since the European quality culture work of the early 2000s, it is explicitly dual: a cultural-psychological element of shared values and commitment, and a structural-managerial element of processes, tools, and coordination, with the claim being that neither alone constitutes a quality culture. Deleting the structural half lets the paper set "culture" against "management output" as opposites — the abstract's "not a management output but an ongoing negotiation," repeated in §6. In the canonical formulation the managerial element is constitutive of quality culture, not its antithesis, so the paper's central contrast is manufactured by its own truncated definition rather than discovered in the data. The paper also announces in §1 that the concept is "under-theorized at the level of everyday administrative practice" and then supplies no theorisation of its own; "quality culture" functions throughout as a label for the residual after documentation is subtracted. Repairing this means re-specifying the construct and re-reading all three themes against it.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §1 "the shared values, practices, and commitments through which an institution takes collective ownership of its own standards"
**Confidence**: 5 — the dual-construct formulation is standard in the quality-culture literature I work in.

### W4: The field's foundational literature is absent, and established concepts are attributed to unverifiable recent sources
A quality-culture paper with twelve references omits the European quality culture project lineage that established the construct, the responsive/reactive/regenerative/reproductive typology that is the field's standard analytic tool for exactly the compliance-versus-ownership distinction this paper draws, and the standards framework that created the internal-QA obligation whose translation problem the paper says it is studying. The "ritual" theme, which is the paper's most developed finding, sits on the audit-culture and audit-society literature without citing it, reaching that literature only at second hand through a 2019 article. Similarly, "steering at a distance" is attributed at length to Aoki (2019) although the concept and the evaluative-state formulation predate it by decades. The effect is that the paper's most confident claim — that this territory is under-theorised — is an artefact of not having read the theory. This omission would warrant a warn on its own; it becomes material here because the twelve unverifiable entries are the only literature the paper has.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §2 Literature Review — expected engagement with the European Quality Culture Project lineage, the standard quality-culture typology, the ESG Part 1 internal QA standards, and the audit-culture literature underwriting the ritual theme; checked §1 framing, all three strands of §2, §5 discussion, and the 12-entry reference list
**Confidence**: 5 — comparative QA scholarship and national accreditation panel practice.

### W5: External QA compliance, internal quality management, and quality culture are used interchangeably
These are three distinct constructs. External QA compliance concerns what an institution must demonstrate to an agency; internal quality management concerns the institution's own processes for monitoring and improving provision; quality culture concerns the values and commitment that determine whether those processes are owned. The manuscript labels all three "QA" and moves between them without marking the shift. The §4.1 evidence is administrators reporting that portfolio production for an external panel is disconnected from teaching — a finding about external review documentation, and a well-documented one. It is reported as a finding about institutional quality culture. The same slide runs through the abstract's framing of the problem as translating external requirements into internal culture, which presupposes the linear relation the paper elsewhere denies. Until the three constructs are separated, the paper cannot show that its data bear on the construct in its title, and the reader cannot tell which of the three the "moderately positive" survey score measures.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.1 "We produce beautiful evidence portfolios that nobody reads except the panel. It's theater, and everyone knows it's theater."
**Confidence**: 5 — this three-way distinction is the working vocabulary of external QA practice.

### W6: The sector-universality claim contradicts the comparative evidence base
§5 states that the findings demonstrate how universities across the sector behave and that the study can be read as a general account of the higher education sector as a whole, with administrators everywhere facing the same tension. Comparative work on QA regimes points the other way: administrator behaviour varies systematically with regulatory design — programme accreditation versus institutional audit versus evaluative-state monitoring, agency-led versus buffer-body arrangements, high-stakes versus developmental consequences — and the ritual response is strongest where review is high-stakes and standardised. Asserting cross-system uniformity is not merely an over-reach from three cases; it is a claim the comparative literature already contradicts. It also cannot be tested here, because the manuscript never says which regime it observed (W7). The two demands interact: either the contextual detail is supplied and the claim is tested, or the sector-level claim is withdrawn.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension between external requirement and internal meaning"
**Confidence**: 4 — based on comparative QA regime scholarship across European and East Asian systems.

### W7: The national system, regime type, agency, and cycle position are never specified, making the domain claims uncheckable
"Review cycles," "evidence portfolios," "action plans," and "the panel" all denote different objects under different regimes: an evidence portfolio in a programme accreditation system with pass/fail consequences is not the same artefact as a self-evaluation report in a developmental institutional audit, and the seasonality described in §4.1 is a property of some cycle designs and not others. Without the regime specification a policy reader cannot map the findings onto any system, no other team can attempt replication, and I cannot verify whether the paper's implicit factual picture of how QA operates is correct for the setting studied. Anonymity does not require this omission — naming the national system and regime type while withholding institution names is standard practice in this literature and is the minimum a policy venue should accept.
**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.2 Participants and setting — expected identification of the national system, the external QA regime type (accreditation, audit, or evaluation), the responsible agency, and the position in the review cycle; checked §1, §3.2, §3.3, §4 findings, and §5 discussion
**Confidence**: 5 — routine reporting requirement for comparative QA research.

### W8: The priority claim in §6 is contradicted by the paper's own literature review
§6 claims the first comprehensive account of how administrators experience and enact QA implementation. §2 cites two studies on distributed leadership and professional identity in the quality office and a third on administrators as intermediaries, and describes the last as conceptual rather than grounded — a real and defensible gap, but a narrow one. Twelve interviews in three institutions in one unspecified system is not a comprehensive account of anything, and the misstatement of what prior work has already established is itself a domain-accuracy error. The accurate claim is available in the manuscript: the first interview-based account of administrator meaning-making in this setting. That is what should be written.
**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §6 "This paper has offered the first comprehensive account of how university administrators experience and enact QA implementation"
**Confidence**: 4 — the counter-evidence is the manuscript's own §2.
