## Review Body

I read this manuscript as a defendant and asked, at each load-bearing step, whether the conclusion drawn is the conclusion the presented material can carry. The paper's central thesis — that quality culture is an ongoing negotiation rather than a management output — is a plausible and potentially valuable claim, and the interview material quoted is vivid. But the argument that connects that material to the thesis fails at three points that are internal to the manuscript, meaning I do not need any outside evidence to establish the defect: the paper contradicts itself in each case.

The most serious is §4.3. The paper states that it "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views on QA implementation," and then, in the very next sentence, reports that participants who were skeptical of the entire QA enterprise "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis." These two sentences cannot both be true. Worse, the excluded accounts are precisely the disconfirming cases for the thesis: the paper's argument is that administrators recover meaning from ritual through identity work and distributed leadership, and the participants who did not recover meaning were removed on the grounds that they did not fit the structure their exclusion then appears to validate. The warrant is circular — the three-theme structure is evidenced by a corpus from which non-conforming accounts were deleted — and the resulting framework is unfalsifiable as stated, since an administrator who endorses QA becomes theme three and an administrator who rejects it becomes out of scope. §4.1's assurance that the themes "emerged systematically" and so are not an artifact is exactly the inference the exclusion forbids.

The second failure is the Delacroix citation. §2 accurately reports Delacroix (2018) as cautioning that "participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual" and as arguing against treating consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture; the reference list's own annotation confirms this reading. §5 then claims the paper's findings "align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture." That is an inversion of the cited position and a contradiction of the manuscript's own literature review, and it is not decorative: it is the sole citational support for the paper's practical contribution, the design implication promised in the abstract. Strip the mis-citation and no evidence remains for "processes that maximize genuine participation should be favored," because no participation intervention, no variation in participatory design, and no outcome measure of culture strength was studied. There is a sharper adversarial point available: the paper's strongest and most pervasive finding — QA as theater that "everyone knows" is theater — is evidence *for* Delacroix's warning that added consultation becomes one more documented artifact. The paper's own theme one argues against the paper's own recommendation, and §2 had announced this tension as the study's animating problem before §5 quietly dissolves it.

The third failure is scope. §5 asserts that the findings "demonstrate that universities across the sector treat QA as a negotiated accomplishment" and that "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension." Twelve purposively recruited administrators at three institutions in one national system, reached through the QA offices whose work is under study, cannot demonstrate anything about the sector or about everywhere; and §6 concedes the point by calling for research "across a wider range of institutional types and national systems," which is unintelligible if the general account has already been delivered. This is a rewording problem rather than a data problem, so I treat it as Major rather than Critical, but it is the same defect class as the abstract's tripartite framing: a negotiation "among administrators, academics, and external bodies" is a claim about three parties evidenced by one party's accounts plus a perception score from a fourth group, with no acknowledgement of that asymmetry.

I score D3 `block` with class `repairable` rather than `fatal` for a specific reason. The dissenting interviews were collected; the citation error is a correction; the generalisations are re-scopable. A revision that re-analyses the full corpus including negative cases, states honestly what the retained and excluded accounts jointly show, repairs the Delacroix attribution, and confines its claims to three institutions could yield a coherent paper — possibly a better one, since the co-existence of meaning-recovery and outright rejection is more interesting than the tidy three-theme story. What cannot survive is the current inference chain. I note also that the revised paper's conclusion may not be the present one, so authors should not treat this as a wording exercise.

Several findings I report belong to other seats' scoring remits and I hand them over without scoring them. The abstract/methods participant-count discrepancy (fourteen versus twelve), the unreported survey instrument and scale range behind "M=3.9," the unnamed test and absent group means behind "p<.05," and the identifying participant descriptors set against a claim of full anonymisation are D1 material for the methodology reviewer, and the anonymisation contradiction in particular requires editorial verification of what participants consented to. The Delacroix inversion is D2 material for the domain reviewer, who can also check whether the §2 characterisations of Aoki (2019), Montero (2021), Nakamura (2020), Iversen (2021), and Okonkwo (2018) survive the same test. The "first comprehensive account" claim in §6 is D6 material, since the paper's own §2 credits Pettersen (2022), Rahman (2020), and Silva and Tan (2021) with occupying this ground already. One further point I rate as Minor and therefore do not table: "quality culture" is never defined operationally and shifts referent across the paper — shared values and commitments in §1, a numeric perception score in §4.4, a sector-level property in §5 — and it is that elasticity that permits the survey to be described as corroborating the interviews. Fixing the definition once, early, is a clarity repair, but it is the mechanism behind two of the tabled Majors and the perspective reviewer may reasonably read it as an accessibility problem as well. I also note as Minor that §4.2's claim that distributed leadership "recurred across every interview" is a frequency assertion with no coding counts anywhere in the manuscript.
