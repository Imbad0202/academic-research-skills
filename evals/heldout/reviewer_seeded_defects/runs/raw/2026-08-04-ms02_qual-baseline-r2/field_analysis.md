- **Title**: Building Institutional Quality Culture: Administrator Perspectives on Quality Assurance Implementation in Universities
- **Abstract length**: ~155 words
- **Full text length**: ~2,500–2,700 words (excluding abstract and references)
- **Number of references**: 12 (all dated 2018–2022; all DOIs in the `10.5555/104200x` placeholder range, sequentially numbered)

### Internal contradictions identified during triage (to be routed to reviewers, not resolved here)

These are recorded so that no reviewer has to rediscover them, and so that each is assigned to exactly one reviewer:

1. Abstract "Fourteen administrators" vs §3.2 "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)".
2. §3.1 "semi-structured interviews" vs §4.1 "emerged systematically from the **structured** protocol".
3. §4.1 "emerged" (inductive) vs "structured protocol" (deductive) — paradigm conflation.
4. §4.2 "recurred across **every** interview" vs §4.3 "A minority of participants expressed more skeptical or dissenting views... these were excluded".
5. §4.3 "achieved balanced representation... capturing the **full range**" vs the exclusion of dissenting cases in the very next sentence.
6. §3.5 "all data were fully anonymized... no individual could be identified" vs §4.1 identifying "the quality director of the **largest private university in the region**" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" — in a three-institution sample, both are singular positions.
7. §4.4 "The survey **corroborated** the qualitative picture" vs "the interview accounts had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture" — the survey *diverged*; divergence is being reported as convergence.
8. §3.4 analysis plan (subgroups by **role**) vs §4.4 reported analysis (subgroups by **institution type**); also 9+11=20 of 48 respondents, with the remaining 28 unaccounted for.
9. §5 "Here our findings align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism" vs §2 and the reference-list annotation, both of which state Delacroix argues *against* exactly this. The paper's own reference list falsifies its own Discussion.
10. §5 "confirms **long-standing** concerns" with no source older than 2018.

## Recommended Target Journals (Top 3)

1. **Quality in Higher Education** (Taylor & Francis) — Closest topical fit by a wide margin: the journal is the field's dedicated home for internal QA, quality culture, and implementation studies, and the paper already cites Iversen (2021) from it. Its readership will, however, know the canonical quality-culture literature cold, which is precisely what this manuscript omits.
2. **Journal of Higher Education Policy and Management** — Strong fit for the paper's actual contribution: senior administrators and professional staff as intermediary actors. The paper cites Silva and Tan (2021) from this journal, and the manuscript is best read as a grounded extension of that conceptual piece. More tolerant of modest samples if the institutional-management implications are sharp.
3. **Higher Education Quarterly** — Plausible fit (Ferreira & Lund, 2022 is cited from it) and receptive to qualitative institutional studies, but it will demand a theoretical frame the paper currently lacks. *Fallback*: **Tertiary Education and Management**. *Stretch, not currently viable*: **Studies in Higher Education** — would require substantially stronger theorization, a larger or better-justified sample, and removal of the sector-level generalization.

## Reviewer Configuration Cards

### Reviewer Configuration Card #1

**Role**: EIC
**Display role**: Journal-Fit Reviewer
**Identity Description**: Senior Associate Editor of *Quality in Higher Education*; comparative specialist in internal quality assurance systems, with two decades of work on ESG Part 1 (internal QA) implementation and a long involvement in the European quality-culture agenda from the EUA Quality Culture Project onward. Has handled several hundred submissions on "quality culture" and can recite from memory which claims of novelty the journal has already published.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **Fit and novelty against the journal's own back catalogue.** Assess the Conclusion's claim to be "the first comprehensive account of how university administrators experience and enact QA implementation" against the existing corpus — including the paper's own citations of Pettersen (2022) on distributed leadership in the quality office, Rahman (2020) on identity and vocation among quality professionals, and Silva and Tan (2021) on administrators as intermediaries. Determine whether the paper's actual increment is "grounded empirical extension of Silva and Tan" rather than "first comprehensive account", and whether that smaller, defensible claim still clears the journal's contribution bar.
  2. **International readability and comparative value.** The national system is never named (§3.2, "three universities in a single national system"), no QA regime, agency, or regulatory cycle is described, and no data collection period is given. Judge whether an international readership can situate the findings at all, or whether the anonymization of the system has stripped the paper of the contextual specificity that makes QA research travel.
  3. **Whether the empirical base is sufficient for this journal at this length.** 12 (or 14) interviews across three sites plus a 48-person survey, reported in ~2,600 words with no interview protocol, no participant table, no coding examples, and no limitations section. Decide whether this is a Major Revision candidate or below threshold for the journal's empirical standards, and state which.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether "quality culture" is defined and operationalized precisely enough to be distinguished from quality assurance, accreditation, and audit — the journal's readers treat conflation of these as a disqualifying error, and the manuscript uses "quality culture" as both an outcome, a survey construct, and a negotiated process without ever defining it.
**Possible blind spots**: May accept a compelling narrative and well-turned quotations as adequate evidence; is unlikely to interrogate the statistical claim in §4.4 in technical detail; may treat the placeholder DOIs as a copy-editing matter rather than as an integrity flag requiring verification before any recommendation is issued.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 1
**Identity Description**: Mixed-methods design methodologist based in an education research methods unit; qualitative-primary background, publishes on QUAL→quan integration quality and on the reporting standards for thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke lineage, COREQ/SRQR); also routinely serves as the statistical screener for small-N educational surveys and has written on the misuse of significance testing in subgroup analyses.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **Sample integrity and reporting consistency.** Reconcile the abstract's "Fourteen administrators" with §3.2's "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)" and require the authors to state which is correct and why the error occurred. Then interrogate the survey: no sampling frame, no response rate, no per-institution breakdown, and §4.4's subgroups (n=9, n=11) account for only 20 of the stated 48 respondents. Also flag the mismatch between the analysis plan in §3.4 (subgroups "where role categories permitted") and the analysis actually reported in §4.4 (comparison by institution type) — the reported test was not the planned test.
  2. **Thematic analysis rigor and the exclusion of dissenting cases.** §3.4 reports iterative coding "until a stable structure was reached" with no coding framework, no coder count, no intercoder or member-checking procedure, no reflexivity or positionality statement, and no saturation argument. Most seriously, §4.3 states that a minority of participants held dissenting views about the entire QA enterprise and that "these were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis" — this is the elimination of disconfirming evidence to protect a coding structure, and it directly falsifies both the "balanced representation... full range" claim in the same paragraph and the "recurred across every interview" claim in §4.2. Require the negative cases to be analyzed and reported, or the universality claims withdrawn. Separately, challenge §4.1's non sequitur that themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol, giving us confidence that the pattern was not an artifact of any single institution's local circumstances" — protocol structure cannot rule out site effects, and no per-institution theme distribution is reported; note also that "emerged" and "structured protocol" describe incompatible designs, and that §3.1 says *semi*-structured.
  3. **The inferential claim in §4.4.** A "statistically significant difference (p<.05)" is asserted between two subgroups of 9 and 11 with no named test, no effect size, no confidence interval, no assumption checks, no correction for the fact that this is one comparison among several possible, and no reported per-group means or SDs. Additionally, M=3.9 / SD=0.6 is reported with no scale anchors, no instrument source, no item count, and no reliability estimate — the reader cannot tell whether 3.9 is out of 5, 6, or 7. Assess whether the sentence "points to institutional type as a possible moderator" is supportable at all, or must be deleted.
  4. **Mixed-methods integration and the mischaracterized divergence.** No integration strategy, joint display, or design typology is stated. §4.4 claims the survey "corroborated the qualitative picture" and then, two sentences later, concedes the interviews "had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture" — a divergent finding is being narrated as convergence. Divergence is legitimate and interesting in mixed-methods work; misreporting it as corroboration is not. Finally, evaluate external validity: the study has three sites, and §5 claims the findings "demonstrate that universities across the sector" behave this way and "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture" — plus "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension." Also note the complete absence of a limitations section.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether the data as described can bear the weight of the claims as written — and specifically whether the authors will accept that the honest version of this paper is smaller than the one they have drafted.
**Possible blind spots**: Will likely not detect the reversed characterization of Delacroix (2018) in §5, since that requires reading the reference-list annotation against the Discussion; may under-weight the missing canonical quality-culture literature; may treat the identifiability problem in §4.1 as a minor reporting slip rather than a substantive participant-protection failure. Both gaps are covered by Reviewers 2 and 3 respectively.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #3

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 2
**Identity Description**: Professor of higher education studies specializing in quality culture and institutional self-evaluation; comparative work across European and Asian QA systems; deeply versed in the foundational strand the manuscript does not cite — Newton's studies of academics "feeding the beast" and the ritualization of quality systems, Harvey and Stensaker's quality-culture typology, Ehlers on quality literacy, Sursock and the EUA *Examining Quality Culture* reports, and the audit-society framing that preceded all of them. Also serves as a reference-verification screener for a journal that has had two retractions for fabricated citations.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **Citation integrity — the Delacroix reversal.** §5 states: "our findings align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture. Consultation, on this reading, is the engine of internalization." §2 of the same manuscript states that Delacroix "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual, and argues *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of a healthy quality culture," and the reference list annotation confirms this. The Discussion attributes to a cited author the exact position that author wrote against, and the paper's own front matter proves it. Establish whether this is a misreading, a misremembering, or a citation inserted to manufacture support — and require the third of three "findings that stand out" to be rebuilt, since it currently rests on this reversal. Then audit whether any other citation is used to support a claim its source does not make.
  2. **Reference verifiability and the missing canon.** All 12 references carry DOIs in the `10.5555/1042001`–`10.5555/1042012` range — a sequential block in a prefix not assigned to real publishers. Attempt verification of each and report which resolve. Independently: every reference is from 2018–2022, no work is cited for the foundational quality-culture literature, and §5 claims the findings "confirm long-standing concerns" while citing nothing long-standing. Assess whether §2's premise — that quality culture "remains under-theorized at the level of everyday administrative practice" — survives contact with the literature that actually exists, or whether the gap claim is an artifact of an incomplete search. Ask for the search strategy.
  3. **Novelty claims against the field.** Evaluate "the first comprehensive account" (§6) and the framing of a three-site study as "a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture" (§5) against what the field has already established. Specify what remains genuinely new once the overclaims are stripped out.
  4. **Conceptual precision and unearned theoretical labels.** "Quality culture" is never defined, yet functions simultaneously as a process, an institutional property, and a survey score. "Distributed leadership" (§4.2) is deployed as a theme label with no engagement with the distributed-leadership literature — not even Pettersen (2022), which the paper itself cites for exactly this. "Identity work" (§4.3) is likewise used as a term of art without its provenance. Determine whether these are analytic concepts doing work or descriptive labels borrowed for authority.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether the manuscript's central gap claim is real or manufactured by a thin literature search — because if the canonical literature already contains the ritual-compliance finding, the paper's contribution collapses from "first account" to "confirmation in an unnamed system," and every downstream claim must be rewritten.
**Possible blind spots**: May over-invest in literature and citation matters and give the §4.4 statistics only cursory attention; may accept the qualitative findings as adequately warranted because they align with what the field already believes — a confirmation risk the synthesizer should watch, since agreement with prior literature is not evidence of methodological soundness.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #4

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Display role**: Peer Reviewer 3
**Identity Description**: Organizational sociologist working on audit cultures, accountability infrastructures, and institutional decoupling — the tradition running from ceremonial conformity and institutionalized myth through the audit society to reactivity effects and policy–practice decoupling in organizations. Studies universities as one case among hospitals, courts, and firms, and has no stake in higher education's internal debates. Long-serving member of a university research ethics committee with specific responsibility for qualitative de-identification review. Comes to this manuscript from outside the QA field entirely.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **The paper has discovered decoupling and does not know it.** "QA as ritual compliance" — elaborate documentation whose connection to the underlying activity is tenuous, produced for an external audience, with the record displacing the thing recorded — is a textbook case of ceremonial conformity and policy–practice decoupling, described by organizational sociology for nearly fifty years and extended in the audit-culture and reactivity literatures. The manuscript instead treats it as a novel empirical finding within higher education. Recommend that the authors either engage this tradition (which would let them ask the far more interesting question — *why does decoupling persist even where administrators can see it and name it as theater?*) or explicitly justify why it does not apply. The same applies to "identity work," which is a specific construct from organizational studies used here as a bare label. Note also that the strongest observation in the paper is arguably underdeveloped: §4.3's finding that administrators "held the tension open, treating the gap itself as the space in which their professional identity was located" is a genuinely non-obvious claim about how actors inhabit decoupled systems, and it is given one paragraph.
  2. **Participant identifiability — the anonymization guarantee is broken in the text.** §3.5 promises that "all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis so that no individual could be identified in reported findings." §4.1 then attributes a quotation to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and another to "the associate dean for quality at the public research university." With three named institution types and one such post per institution, both speakers are effectively named. The quoted content is institutionally damaging ("It's theater, and everyone knows it's theater"; the effort leaves "nothing behind that a teacher would recognize as helping them teach"), which converts a de-identification lapse into a live risk to identifiable senior staff who consented on the basis of a promise the manuscript does not keep. Assess this as a research-governance matter requiring editorial attention, not a copy-edit: it needs stripped role descriptors, a re-examination of whether consent covered attributable institutional criticism, and possibly a statement from the approving ethics committee. Note that the disclosure risk scales *inversely* with sample size — the small-N design that limits the paper's claims simultaneously amplifies its identifiability exposure.
  3. **Whether anything here is actionable for the people who run QA systems.** §5 concludes that "processes that maximize genuine participation should be favored" — a recommendation that (a) rests on the reversed Delacroix citation, and (b) is the precise position the manuscript's own literature review flags as vulnerable to becoming another compliance ritual. From a practice standpoint, ask what a quality office or a national QA agency could actually do differently on Monday morning. If the finding is that administrators recover meaning through identity work and coalition-building rather than through documentation, the practical implication concerns workload allocation, professional development, and how agencies design evidence requirements — none of which the paper addresses.
  4. **Unexamined structural assumptions.** The study treats administrators as the analytically privileged vantage point on quality culture, but they are the actors with the strongest occupational interest in QA being meaningful — §4.3 documents this directly, then reads the vocational framing as insight rather than interrogating it as motivated account. Meanwhile academics and students, whose experience the QA system nominally exists to serve, are absent except as objects of administrators' persuasion, and the survey samples "mid-level staff" of unspecified composition. Assess whether "institutional quality culture" can be characterized from this single vantage point, and what the design's silence about power relations within the institution does to the findings.
**Will particularly care about**: That the manuscript's most defensible contribution — administrators as actors who inhabit a decoupled system knowingly and build professional identity in the gap — is buried under overclaims about being first and about the whole sector, while the theoretical resources that would make it publishable in any discipline sit uncited.
**Possible blind spots**: Unfamiliar with higher education QA conventions and may propose theoretical reframing that overreaches what the data can support, or may undervalue the field-specific literature Reviewer 2 covers. Being an outsider, may also misjudge which of the paper's claims are genuinely novel *within* higher education — Reviewer 2 must arbitrate that.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

**Paper characteristics requiring particular attention**

- **Fluent prose over unstable evidence.** This manuscript is well-written, and its polish will create a favorable first impression that its evidentiary base does not support. Reviewers must be instructed to quote and cite specific text for every finding rather than forming a global impression, because the errors here are local, numerous, and individually checkable — and every single one is visible from the manuscript alone, without external verification.
- **Two integrity-class issues must not be softened into "minor revisions."** (1) The Delacroix (2018) reversal in §5, which the paper's own reference-list annotation falsifies, and which supports one of three headline findings. (2) The identifiability of two quoted senior administrators in §4.1 against the explicit anonymization guarantee in §3.5. A third item — the sequential `10.5555` placeholder DOIs across all 12 references — requires verification before any recommendation is finalized and should be reported to the handling editor regardless of the verdict. Per the developmental-register rule, tone may be constructive; the verdict must follow the evidence. These three items are what set the recommendation ceiling.
- **The paper's honest contribution is smaller than its stated one, and that is survivable.** All four reviewers should be pointed toward the same constructive path: the grounded extension of Silva and Tan (2021) with the identity-in-the-gap finding from §4.3 is publishable in a Q2 outlet. "First comprehensive account" and "the higher education sector as a whole" are not, and removing them costs the paper nothing it can currently defend.
- **Missing wholesale, and worth stating as a single package**: limitations section, interview protocol, participant table, coding examples, survey instrument and its provenance, scale anchors, response rate, data collection period, named national system, reflexivity/positionality statement, and any treatment of the excluded dissenting cases.

**Complementarity and tension between reviewers**

- **Deliberate non-overlap on the internal contradictions.** Each of the ten triaged contradictions is assigned to exactly one reviewer: numeric, design, sampling, statistical, integration, and generalization inconsistencies → R1; citation, literature, novelty, and conceptual inconsistencies → R2; theoretical omission, ethics/identifiability, practice, and standpoint issues → R3. The Journal-Fit Reviewer stays at the level of fit, contribution, and threshold and should not re-litigate the details. If two reviewers converge on the same item independently, that convergence is signal and the synthesizer should treat it as high-confidence rather than as redundancy to be trimmed.
- **Live tension 1 — thin vs. thinner.** R2 will demand substantial added literature; the Journal-Fit Reviewer will observe that the paper is already only ~2,600 words with an under-reported methods section. These pull in the same direction on length but opposite directions on emphasis: the resolution is not "cite more" but re-anchoring the gap claim, which may *shrink* the introduction while expanding methods and findings. The synthesizer should say so explicitly rather than passing both demands through.
- **Live tension 2 — generalize less vs. matter more.** R1 will require the sector-level claims in §5 to be withdrawn as unsupported by three sites. The Journal-Fit Reviewer will simultaneously ask why an international readership should care about an unnamed system. Satisfying R1 makes the Journal-Fit concern worse. The genuine resolution — naming the national system and describing its QA regime so that readers can judge transferability themselves — trades against the confidentiality problem R3 raises, since naming the system narrows the pool within which two identifiable senior administrators sit. This three-way constraint is the hardest editorial judgment in the review and must be surfaced for the authors as a decision they need to make, not buried as three separate comments.
- **Live tension 3 — R3's theoretical ambition vs. R1's evidentiary limits.** R3 will propose an organizational-sociology reframing that is intellectually the strongest available move for this paper; R1 will note that 12 interviews with no negative-case analysis cannot carry a theoretical contribution of that scale. Both are right. The synthesizer should present the reframing as the paper's medium-term trajectory while holding the current revision to what the data support.
- **Watch for R2's confirmation risk.** R2 will find that the ritual-compliance theme matches the field's established literature and may accept the qualitative strand as sound on that basis. Agreement with prior findings is not evidence of methodological adequacy, and R1's objections to the coding process apply with full force regardless of whether the conclusions happen to be correct.
