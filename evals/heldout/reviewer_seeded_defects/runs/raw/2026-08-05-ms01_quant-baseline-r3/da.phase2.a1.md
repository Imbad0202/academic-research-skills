### D3: argumentative_coherence
score: block
trigger: "a causal or effectiveness conclusion drawn from a design that can only license association, an equivalence claim resting on a null result, or a stated finding that conflicts with the numbers reported elsewhere in the manuscript"
block_class: repairable

The manuscript triggers this condition twice over and in the most literal way available. Section 5 opens with "The central finding of this study is that dashboard engagement improved course retention" and then states that "increasing dashboard engagement therefore raises the probability that a student completes the course"; Section 6 repeats that engagement "is associated with, and raises, course retention." The design described in §3.1 is explicitly "observational, cross-sectional," with all measures drawn from a single term. A cross-sectional observational design licenses association and nothing more. Separately, the Abstract reports the headline association as r = .42 while §4.2 reports r = .24 — a stated finding that conflicts with the numbers reported elsewhere in the manuscript, on the single number the paper's contribution rests upon.

I classify this as repairable rather than fatal. A coherent argument survives underneath the overreach: the correlational observation, honestly framed, is the paper the Introduction and Literature Review promise to deliver. Repair requires a rewritten claim set (abstract, discussion, conclusion), reconciliation of the contradictory statistics, and a re-analysis that addresses the exposure-time confound — but not a different study. It therefore does not meet the fatal bar, which requires that no revision short of a different study and a rewritten claim set could reconcile the conclusions with the evidence.

## Review Body

This manuscript is unusual in that it diagnoses its own central failure and then commits it. The Introduction states: "We are careful throughout to distinguish the pattern in the data from the causal story that might explain it, a distinction that the dashboard literature has not always maintained." The Literature Review reinforces this, citing Ibarra (2023) on causal language outrunning evidence and asserting that "We designed the present study to be transparent about its correlational scope." Two sections later, the Discussion abandons the distinction entirely — engagement "improved" retention, and increasing it "therefore raises the probability" of completion — and the Conclusion escalates further, telling institutions "worldwide" that dashboards are "a dependable strategy." This is not a hedging lapse at the edges of the argument. It is a motte-and-bailey structure with the motte written into §1 and §2 and the bailey written into §5 and §6, and a reader who stops at either end will come away with a materially different paper.

Grant the authors everything they ask for and the associational claim still does not survive unassisted. Retention is coded as remaining enrolled through the final assessment; dashboard engagement is the cumulative count of sessions "during the term." A student who withdraws in week four has, by construction, eleven fewer weeks in which to open the dashboard. The reported r = .24 is therefore partly definitional: some unknown share of it is the arithmetic of exposure duration, not any behavioral or regulatory process. The manuscript never raises this possibility — not in §3.3, not in §3.4, not in §5.1 — even though its own Limitations section finds room to worry about the depth of engagement and the generalizability of the interface. That asymmetry in what gets flagged is itself telling: the limitations acknowledged are the ones that leave the headline intact.

The numeric substrate will not bear the weight placed on it. The Abstract's r = .42 and the Results' r = .24 cannot both be right. The perceived-control comparison is reported as t(156) = 3.02, implying 158 observations, when the item was answered by 87 respondents drawn from an analytic sample of 142; there is no sample described anywhere in the manuscript that produces 156 degrees of freedom. The exam comparison is reported as t(140) = 1.31, p = .008, a pairing the t-distribution does not permit — t = 1.31 at 140 df corresponds to p ≈ .19 — and the prose then narrates this p = .008 result as one that "did not reach a comparable level," which under the paper's own alpha of .05 is backwards. Table 2's group sizes sum to 127, contradicting the immediately preceding sentence that "All 142 students in the primary analytic sample were classified into engagement groups for this comparison." Individually each of these could be a typographic accident; together they mean a reader cannot determine which reported quantity, if any, is the real one.

Construct stability fails at the paper's outermost surface. The Abstract states that "we measured dashboard engagement, self-regulated learning behavior, and course persistence." Section 3.3 describes no self-regulated-learning behavioral measure at all — only a single-item rating of perceived control. The Discussion then converts that single item into a mediator ("accounts emphasizing perceived control as a mediating construct") without any mediation analysis, and without the temporal ordering a mediation claim requires. Each step is small; the cumulative drift is from one click-count and one Likert item to a validated SRL mechanism.

The sampling narrative contains a flat self-contradiction that bears directly on every inference drawn: §3.2 asserts "a random sample of students enrolled in the course section" and then describes, three sentences later, a mid-term LMS announcement to which "Students who chose to respond ... formed the study sample." Volunteer self-selection is the opposite of random sampling, and the students who answer a mid-term survey about study habits are plausibly the students already engaging with the dashboard and already likely to persist. This confound is not mentioned in §5.1.

Finally, the literature is enlisted against itself. Section 2 states that "Dashboards have been shown to reliably improve outcomes for lower-achieving students," citing Ferro & Nakamura (2021) — a paper the reference list titles "When dashboards demotivate: Peer comparison and the lower-achieving student." The Discussion then leans on this inverted reading when positioning the results within the "debate in the literature about who benefits from dashboards." An argument built on a source that says the opposite of what it is cited for is not a strong argument that happens to have a citation error; it is a weak argument wearing borrowed authority.

What I would want to see: the causal and worldwide-generalization language removed rather than softened; the exposure-time confound modeled explicitly (e.g., sessions per enrolled week, or an analysis window closing before the earliest withdrawal); every reported statistic reconciled against a stated denominator; the sampling frame described once, accurately; and the Ferro & Nakamura characterization corrected. The correlational paper underneath is publishable. The paper as submitted is not the paper underneath.
