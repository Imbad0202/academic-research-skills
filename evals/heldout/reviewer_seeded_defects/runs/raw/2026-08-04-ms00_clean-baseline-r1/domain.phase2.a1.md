## Review Body

I read this manuscript solely for domain accuracy: whether its substantive assertions about LMS acceptance align with what the field's evidence currently supports, whether its constructs and instruments carry their accepted meanings, whether cited work is characterised as those sources actually stand, and whether the numbers and interpretive labels inside the paper are internally correct.

The verdict is not that the paper says false things. It is that the paper's one interpretive claim about the field — that r = .42 is "consistent with prior technology-acceptance research" — is stated in a form that cannot be checked, and that the manuscript's core construct arrives without its provenance while its outcome variable arrives under three non-equivalent labels. The empirical content survives every finding below; the positioning does not. That combination is what a warn is for, and I want to be explicit that I did not find grounds for a block: no reported statistic is wrong, no cited caution is misdescribed, and the substantive conclusion (a moderate positive association in this sample) is not overturned by anything I found.

On construct provenance. Perceived usefulness is not a loose descriptor in this literature; it is Davis's (1989) TAM construct, defined as the degree to which a person believes using a system would enhance performance, and it carries an established measurement tradition through TAM2 and UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003). The manuscript gives essentially that definition and attributes it to Costa & Wren (2019) and Delgado (2020). The definition is right; the lineage is missing. Neither Davis, nor the UTAUT line, nor any of the meta-analyses that have pooled precisely this association in educational settings appears anywhere. That absence is what makes the consistency claim untestable rather than merely under-cited: the paper never states what value would have been *inconsistent*.

On the state of the evidence, stated with the caution I'd want from any reviewer: my recollection of the pooled literature (Šumak, Heričko & Pušnik, 2011; Abdullah & Ward, 2016; Scherer, Siddiq & Tondeur, 2019, the last on teachers rather than students) is that PU→behavioural-intention associations run considerably higher than PU→use associations, and that the PU→self-reported-use band is broadly where this paper's estimate sits. I am confident about that pattern and deliberately not confident about specific pooled coefficients from memory. The authors, not I, must do this placement — but the fact that placement is *possible* is exactly why its absence is a domain-accuracy defect rather than a stylistic gap. The paper characterises the evidence base as qualitatively variable ("effect sizes vary across samples and instruments") when quantitative synthesis exists.

I also want to record what the paper gets right, because it is not trivial. The correlational discipline is genuine and consistently maintained; the reverse-causation pathway is named and correctly attributed; the self-report/log divergence is named and the outcome is explicitly downgraded to an indicator of perceived use. Papers in this area routinely fail all three of those tests. The statistics reconcile. My criticism is that the paper's honesty is doing work that analysis should be doing — naming a weakness is not addressing it, and here the naming of "one point in a distribution" (via Song, 2018) is followed by no distribution.

On my own likely bias: I am not scoring this down for being small or single-site. A bounded local estimate has real utility for the institution that produced it, and I accept that premise. What I cannot accept is a claimed *comparability* that the manuscript's own reporting makes impossible — the site is undescribed, the metric is not the metric the field reports, and the benchmark is absent. Those are fixable without making the study bigger.

Finally, one item outside my seat: the tension between citing Song (2018) for the insufficiency of single-site estimates and then delivering a single-site estimate is, in my reading, primarily an internal-coherence matter and belongs to the seat that owns D3. I flag its domain consequence only — that the consistency claim is left unsupported — and leave the coherence adjudication there.

### W1: The load-bearing "consistent with prior research" claim is unfalsifiable, and the canonical evidence base is absent

The Discussion, Abstract, and Conclusion all rest on the assertion that r = .42 is consistent with prior technology-acceptance research, supported by two primary studies. No pooled estimate, range, or prediction interval is given, so no reader can determine whether .42 is consistent, high, or low. The Introduction compounds this by citing a single instrument-development paper (Costa & Wren, 2019) for a "substantial body of work" claim. The remedy is specific: locate .42 against the meta-analytic distribution for PU and LMS/e-learning use, cite the canonical construct source (Davis, 1989) and at least one synthesis, and state what value would have contradicted the claim.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5 Discussion "consistent with prior technology-acceptance research (Costa & Wren, 2019; Ibarra & Poll, 2021)"`
**Confidence**: 5 — a decade working on the TAM→UTAUT lineage and the meta-analytic evidence on LMS acceptance.

### W2: "Engagement" is used as a synonym for a single self-reported access-frequency item

The Abstract's closing claim and the Literature Review both speak of "engagement," and the Abstract's opening sentence frames the whole study around engagement varying widely. What was measured is one five-point item on weekly access frequency, which §3.2 and §2 explicitly downgrade to an indicator of *perceived* use rather than behaviour. Student engagement is an established multidimensional construct in this field (behavioural, emotional, cognitive; Fredricks, Blumenfeld & Paris, 2004); self-reported access frequency is not a measure of it, and is at best a weak proxy for even the behavioural facet. The manuscript therefore contradicts its own measurement stance at exactly the two places most readers stop: abstract and conclusion. Relabelling to "self-reported access frequency" throughout, and revising the abstract's terminal claim, resolves it — at the cost of stating a visibly narrower finding, which is the correct finding.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Abstract "perceived usefulness tracks with LMS engagement among undergraduates"`
**Confidence**: 4 — direct familiarity with the engagement-construct literature and its misuse in LMS studies.

### W3: A zero-order correlation is not commensurable with the estimates the field reports, and extracting one TAM path is never justified

The paper declines to test an acceptance model while adopting that model's central construct, and never says why. This is a defensible design choice, but it has a measurement consequence the manuscript does not address: most estimates in the technology-acceptance literature for this relationship are standardised path coefficients from structural models that condition on perceived ease of use and behavioural intention, or are PU→intention rather than PU→use associations. A raw bivariate r is on a different footing. So even if the authors supply the benchmark requested in W1, they must state which quantity they are comparing to which. Relatedly, PU's meaning in this framework is partly relational — it is specified as an antecedent within a joint structure — so isolating a single path requires an explicit statement of what is gained (partial replication? benchmarking? atheoretical description?) and what is given up.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 Literature Review "an incremental data point, comparable with prior work, rather than as a test of a theoretical model"`
**Confidence**: 4 — based on how estimates are conventionally reported and pooled in this literature.

### W4: The institutional context that the paper's own cited source calls decisive is not reported

§3.1 tells us the site is one mid-sized public university and nothing else that bears on the finding. Which LMS? Was use mandated or optional? Were assessments submitted through it, and were grades or announcements available only there? Ibarra and Poll (2021) are invoked in §2 precisely to establish that course design, instructor expectations, and assessment structure shape both perception and use — and then none of the three is described. If assessment ran through the platform, the frequency item is substantially measuring course requirements, which the Results section itself concedes as an unmeasured influence. Without these descriptors the estimate cannot be placed against any other site, which is the sole function of an incremental data point. This is the finding I would most want addressed, because the information almost certainly already exists.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `absence: §3.1 Design and participants — expected named LMS platform, mandated-versus-optional use status, and assessment integration; checked §3.1, §3.2, §4, §5, §6`
**Confidence**: 4 — comparative reading of single-site LMS studies where these moderators drive between-site variation.

### W5: No cited source can be verified from the manuscript as submitted

Every one of the six references carries a DOI in the 10.5555 prefix, which is a reserved test/documentation range rather than a registered publisher prefix, and the DOI suffixes run in strict sequence. Several journal titles are near-misses for real venues (for instance, the *British Journal of Educational Technology* exists; *British Journal of Educational Technology Studies* does not, to my knowledge). I therefore could not execute the central check my dimension requires — whether prior work is characterised as those sources actually stand. I record two readings and decline to choose between them on the evidence available: either these are placeholders or anonymisation artefacts, in which case the fix is clerical, or they are not resolvable at all, in which case the entire literature framing is unassessable and my score would not survive. Confirmation of the reference list is a precondition for any acceptance decision, and I flag to the panel that my D2 warn is conditional on the good-faith reading.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: References "https://doi.org/10.5555/2050001" and "https://doi.org/10.5555/2050006"`
**Confidence**: 3 — I can characterise the DOI prefix but cannot resolve these records from the manuscript alone.

### W6: "Previously validated" overstates what is established for the adapted instrument

The Abstract claims measurement with "an adapted, previously validated instrument," and §3.2 reports that the source instrument showed strong internal consistency without giving a value, then reports α = .88 for the present six items. Two precise problems: validation does not transfer automatically to an adapted version, and internal consistency is a reliability property, not evidence of validity. In the acceptance literature PU scales are ordinarily supported with dimensionality evidence (factor structure, convergent and discriminant validity), none of which appears here. Report what was changed in adapting the items, give the original's reliability figure, and either supply dimensionality evidence or soften the claim to "adapted from a previously validated instrument; reliability in the present sample was α = .88."

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: Abstract "measured with an adapted, previously validated instrument"`
**Confidence**: 4 — standard instrument-validation practice in this subfield.

### W7: The reported median category is a label the scale description never defines

§3.2 gives only the two endpoint anchors, while §4 reports a median category worded as an intermediate frequency that appears nowhere in the scale definition. A reader cannot tell whether this is category 3 or 4, which blocks any comparison of use levels against another site — the same comparability problem as W4, in miniature. Print all five category labels with their numeric codes and the response distribution across them.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §3.2 and §4 "1 = rarely or never to 5 = several times daily" and "a median category of"`
**Confidence**: 5 — direct textual comparison of the two passages.

### S1: Reported statistics are internally correct and mutually consistent

I recomputed what could be recomputed. The 95% confidence interval matches r = .42 at n = 214 under Fisher's z transformation to two decimal places; the sample accounting reconciles exactly (233 received, 14 incomplete and 5 duplicates removed, 214 analysed); the Spearman robustness coefficient sits where a monotone-but-ordinal outcome would put it relative to the Pearson value; and "moderate" is the conventionally correct verbal label for a coefficient of this magnitude. Nothing in the numeric reporting misleads.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §4 Results "r = .42, 95% CI [.30, .52], p < .001, n = 214"`

### S2: The field's methodological cautions are represented accurately, not decoratively

The reverse-causation pathway is stated in its correct form and attributed to the source that raises it; the self-report/behavioural-log divergence is stated in its correct direction and used to constrain the outcome's interpretation rather than merely acknowledged; the between-site variability point is attributed to a multi-campus source. Each of the three cited cautions is characterised in a way consistent with how that argument actually runs in this literature. My complaint (W1, W4) is that the cautions are named and then not acted on — but the naming itself is accurate, and that is more than many submissions in this area manage.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §5 Discussion "the reverse pathway, in which more frequent use raises perceived usefulness, is equally consistent with the data"`

### S3: The core construct's operational meaning is faithful to field usage

Notwithstanding the missing provenance recorded in W1, the definition given is substantively the field-standard one — belief that the technology improves the user's performance — rather than a drift toward satisfaction, liking, or ease of use, which is the most common terminological error I encounter in this space. The six-item length is also consistent with established PU instrumentation. The construct is under-cited, not distorted.

**Evidence Anchor**: `text: §2 Literature Review "the degree to which a person believes a technology will help them perform better"`
