## Review Body

The adversarial case against this manuscript has to start from an accurate picture, so let me concede what is genuinely well done. The paper is unusually disciplined about causality. Correlational language holds across abstract, results, discussion, and conclusion; the reverse pathway is conceded explicitly rather than buried; the limitations section names real constraints rather than decorative ones; and the reported confidence interval is arithmetically consistent with the stated *r* and *n*. My concern is not that the authors sneak in causal language. It is that their modesty is applied almost exclusively to the *direction* of the association, while three other things travel unpoliced: the identity of the outcome construct, the composition of the sample, and the comparative claim that supplies the paper's entire stated contribution.

Start with the outcome construct. What was measured is one Likert item about typical-week access frequency. Section 2 concedes, via Vasquez (2020), that self-report captures perceived rather than actual engagement, and §3.2 commits to treating the item as an indicator of perceived use "rather than a behavioral count." The abstract's closing sentence then reports the finding as perceived usefulness tracking "LMS engagement," and §5 restates the outcome as "engagement" again. Under the paper's own stated commitment, both sentences name a construct the study did not measure. This is not pedantry about word choice, because engagement is precisely the construct the practical implication requires; the paper's discipline lapses exactly where the lapse buys something.

Second, and more seriously for D3: both variables come from one instrument, one respondent, one sitting. The obvious competing account of *r* = .42 is shared-method variance — acquiescence, consistency motif, self-presentation — and it is never named anywhere in the manuscript. The self-report limitation in §6 is a different point: it concerns the fidelity of one variable against logs, not the dependence between two variables elicited together. The design cannot distinguish "students who find the LMS useful use it more" from "students who answer agreeably answer agreeably twice." Because the paper's only inferential claim *is* a claim about an association, an unaddressed rival account of that association sits at the argument's spine rather than at its margins. That is the specific defect driving the warn.

Third, recruitment. If the "course-announcement channel" of §3.1 is the LMS announcement facility — and §1 lists announcements among the LMS's functions, so that is the natural reading — then respondents were recruited through the very behaviour being measured, and students at the bottom of the frequency scale were systematically unreachable. That would restrict the range of the outcome, make the coefficient non-comparable with samples drawn from a student registry, and convert "among undergraduates at one university" into "among undergraduates who read LMS announcements." The fourth limitation gestures at this but does not notice that the overrepresented trait *is* the dependent variable. No response rate is reported against the eligible population, and no frequency distribution for the use item is given, so a reader cannot check whether the lowest category is nearly empty. If the channel was not the LMS, one sentence resolves this; as written, the paper leaves its own sampling frame ambiguous on the one dimension that matters most.

Fourth, the comparability claim. "Consistent with prior technology-acceptance research" appears in the abstract, §5, and §7, and §2 positions the work as "an incremental data point, comparable with prior work." No prior coefficient appears anywhere in the manuscript. Meanwhile §2 concedes that effect sizes "vary across samples and instruments," and Song (2018) is invoked specifically to argue that any single-site estimate is one point in a distribution. On the paper's own account, then, almost any coefficient in the plausible range would have been "consistent." A comparative claim that no obtainable result could have contradicted is not a finding, and since comparability is the declared contribution, the contribution is asserted rather than performed.

Fifth, the practical implication. Section 5 states that the reverse pathway is "equally consistent with the data" and then, two sentences later, recommends onboarding designed to help students see concrete usefulness. That recommendation has content only if the forward pathway operates. The double hedge ("may be worth," "suggested by, not proven by") does not repair the problem, because the problem is not strength but direction: under the authors' own symmetry statement, the data supply no directional evidence at all, so there is nothing for a hedge to attenuate. Whitfield (2019), a practitioner account, cannot supply the missing warrant. Either the symmetry concession or the recommendation has to go.

There is also a flat internal contradiction in the data-handling account: §3.1 removes five duplicate entries while §3.3 states that responses could not be linked back to individual students. Deduplication requires linking responses to each other, so at least one of these statements is imprecise, and the anonymity assurance is the one that matters ethically. Several smaller items round this out and I raise them without tabling them: the abstract's "previously validated instrument" rests on a Methods sentence that reports only the source's internal consistency, with the nature of the adaptation undocumented, so validity is inherited rather than shown; *r*² is never stated, leaving "moderate" to do the framing work when the number (roughly 18% shared, 82% unaccounted) would discipline it better; the five frequency categories are never enumerated, so the reported median category cannot be located on the scale; and the claim of greater-than-.80 power for *r* ≥ .19 at *n* = 214 sits essentially on the boundary rather than comfortably above it, which is the methodology seat's call rather than mine.

Net position on D3: warn, and repairable. The narrow claim survives — a correlation of .42 between two self-reports in this sample, reported with an interval consistent with its *n*. What does not survive as written is the outcome construct in the abstract and discussion, the onboarding implication, and the comparability framing that carries the paper's claimed contribution. None of these requires a different study; all of them require the authors to extend the discipline they already show about causation to the constructs, the sample, and the comparison.
