## Review Strategy Recommendations

- **This paper's central risk is internal inconsistency, not surface quality.** The prose is polished enough to pass a casual read, which makes the substantive defects easy to miss. The panel should be steered to close-read for self-contradiction first, quality of argument second. The synthesizer should expect the sharpest findings to be **consistency** findings rather than stylistic ones.
- **The Delacroix reversal (§2/annotation vs. §5) is the single most serious issue and is deliberately owned by R2 (domain), because judging which characterization is correct requires knowing the source.** It is not a typo: the paper's own recommendation ("consultation is the engine of internalization") is built on a citation the paper elsewhere reports as arguing the opposite. Treat as potentially disqualifying until the authors resolve which reading they intend.
- **The numeric/site discrepancies are distributed on purpose so they are caught, not assumed.** R1 owns the survey-side numbers (n=48 vs. 9+11; unnamed significance test on n=20), R4 owns the cross-manuscript audit (14 vs. 12 administrators; "fourteen"/"three universities" in the abstract vs. the body; the identifiable-private-university quote). If any reviewer treats a count as settled, the synthesizer should notice the un-reconciled remainder.
- **Non-overlap is engineered along the axis of *why* each reviewer would catch a problem**, not merely *what*: R1 catches the "balanced representation / full range" claim as a **methodological** bias signal; R4 catches the same paragraph's dissent-exclusion as a **reporting-integrity** signal; R2 catches literature **fidelity**. The claim in §4.1 that themes "emerged systematically… giving us confidence" and the claim in §4 of "balanced representation" are both epistemic over-reaches that the design cannot license — R1 and R4 should independently converge on that from different premises, and that convergence is a signal, not redundancy.
- **Complementarity/tension to watch:** R2 (domain) is likely to be the most sympathetic to the paper's face-value narrative and may rate the contribution generously; R1 and R4 are structurally more skeptical. The EIC must hold the line on scope: a genuinely valuable, modest paper on administrator meaning-making is being oversold as a general theory of the sector. The productive editorial outcome is *major revision toward honesty and internal consistency*, not rejection — the underlying qualitative material appears real and interesting; the manuscript's problem is that its claims, counts, and citations have not been reconciled with each other.
- **Verification flag for the whole panel:** every reference resolves to a placeholder-pattern DOI (`10.5555/104200x`). Reviewers should treat citation existence and correct attribution (especially Delacroix) as an open verification item rather than assume the sources are as described.
- **Tone:** given "revised draft" maturity with real substance beneath fixable defects, reviewers should be firm on the contradictions but developmental in register — the goal is a publishable paper, and the fixes (reconcile counts, correct the Delacroix reading, scope the claims, honor the anonymity promise or drop the identifying descriptors, name and justify the survey test) are concrete and achievable.

### Summary Assessment

This manuscript studies how senior administrators experience and enact QA implementation across three universities, using interviews as the primary strand and a supplementary staff survey. The underlying material is genuinely interesting: the three themes (QA as ritual compliance, distributed leadership, and identity work) are recognizable, well-voiced, and sit squarely on this journal's home turf, and the "quality culture as ongoing negotiation" framing is a real contribution to the compliance-versus-commitment conversation. The prose is polished and the structure is complete. But the manuscript has not been reconciled against itself, and the defects are substantive rather than cosmetic. Three failures are, from the editorial chair, decisive: the discussion attributes to Delacroix (2018) a recommendation that the paper's own literature review and reference annotation say Delacroix argues *against* — and the paper's headline recommendation is built on that reversed citation; the contribution claims ("first comprehensive account," a general account of "the higher education sector as a whole") are wildly out of scale with a 12-interview, single-system, three-institution design; and the empirical spine carries un-reconciled numeric contradictions (fourteen versus twelve administrators; n=48 versus subgroups summing to 20; an unnamed significance test on n=20). None of these is fatal to the study; all are fatal to the manuscript as it stands. The path forward is a substantive consistency-and-claims revision toward honesty and internal coherence, not a polish and not a rejection.

### Strengths (4 items)

1. **Journal-core topic, competently framed**: The compliance-versus-commitment tension and the "quality culture as negotiation" thesis (§5, §6) are precisely the conversation this journal exists to advance. The administrator-centred angle is a legitimate and under-occupied vantage point.
2. **Vivid, credible qualitative material**: The interview voice is strong and rings true — "We produce beautiful evidence portfolios that nobody reads except the panel. It's theater" (§4.1); "Half my job is convincing people this matters. The forms are the easy part" (§4.2). The identity-work theme (§4.3), where administrators hold the compliance-meaning gap open as the space their professional identity occupies, is the freshest observation in the paper.
3. **Coherent three-theme architecture**: Ritual compliance → distributed leadership → identity work is a legible progression, and the discussion's move from these themes to a negotiation model is a sound analytic step (before it over-reaches on scope).
4. **Literate positioning against the field**: The literature review (§2) organizes the QA scholarship into three strands cleanly and situates the study in the emergent quality-culture strand, correctly identifying the administrator-hinge gap (Silva & Tan, 2021).

### Weaknesses (4 items)

1. **The headline recommendation rests on a reversed citation (disqualifying until resolved)**: §2 and the reference annotation both report Delacroix (2018) as arguing *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of quality culture. Yet §5 states the findings "align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism," calling consultation "the engine of internalization." The paper's own central design recommendation is therefore anchored to a source it elsewhere reports as holding the opposite view. *Improvement*: decide which reading of Delacroix is correct, verify it against the source, and rebuild the §5 recommendation on a citation that actually supports it — or, if the finding genuinely contrasts with Delacroix, frame it as a contrast, not an alignment.
2. **Contribution claims out of scale with the design**: "the first comprehensive account" (§6) and the leap from three institutions to "the higher education sector as a whole" and "administrators everywhere" (§5) are not proportionate to twelve interviews in a single national system. This is exactly the over-claim an editor cannot let stand: a strong, modest paper on administrator meaning-making is publishable here; a "general account of the sector" is not. *Improvement*: scope the claims to what the design supports — a rich, three-institution, single-system account that generates hypotheses about a broader pattern, not a verified account of the sector.
3. **The manuscript has not been reconciled against itself (empirical spine)**: The abstract says "Fourteen administrators" while §3.2 says "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)"; the survey total is "48 mid-level staff" (§3.2) but the only named subgroups sum to 20 (n=9 + n=11, §4.4); §4.4 reports a "statistically significant difference (p<.05)" with no test named, no effect size, and no assumption check. *Improvement*: reconcile every count across abstract, methods, and findings; name and justify the survey test or drop the inferential claim; report the instrument and scale range behind M=3.9/SD=0.6.
4. **Confidence language unearned by stated procedures; and a self-contradiction on representativeness**: §4.1 claims themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol, giving us confidence that the pattern was not an artifact," yet the Methods report no coder count, no intercoder or consensus procedure, no saturation logic, and no reflexivity. Worse, §4 asserts "balanced representation... capturing the full range of administrator views" in one sentence and, in the next, states dissenting participants "were excluded for space." *Improvement*: either supply the trustworthiness procedures that would license the confidence language, or soften the language to match the design; and reintegrate the dissenting views rather than excluding them, since their exclusion directly contradicts the "full range" claim and reads as analytic cherry-picking.

### Detailed Comments

#### Originality
The originality is real but narrower than the paper claims. Centering administrators' own accounts of the compliance-meaning gap, and theorizing identity work as the space where that gap is held open, is a genuine incremental advance on Silva & Tan (2021)'s more conceptual treatment. Recalibrate the novelty claim to "an interview-grounded extension of the administrator-intermediary line."

#### Significance
Moderate and worth publishing at proportionate scope. The negotiation model is a useful reframing for how QA processes might be designed to create room for meaning-making. Its significance is undercut, not enhanced, by the sector-wide generalization: the honest, bounded claim is more persuasive to this readership than the inflated one.
