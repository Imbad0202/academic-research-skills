### Reviewer Configuration Card #1

**Role**: EIC
**Identity Description**: Editor-in-Chief of *Quality in Higher Education*, a comparative higher education scholar who has spent fifteen years studying how national QA agencies translate the European Standards and Guidelines (ESG) into institutional practice; served on two national accreditation review panels and previously chaired an ENQA working group on internal quality systems. **Review Focus**:

Assess whether §5's leap to "universities across the sector" and "a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture" is a fatal over-claim or a fixable framing problem, and whether the paper's ~2,700 words are simply too thin to constitute a full research article at this journal.

**Possible blind spots**: An EIC operating at desk-review altitude may stop at "major revision, fix the contradictions" without pursuing *why* the contradictions arose — for instance whether the excluded dissenting cases would have overturned the three-theme structure rather than merely supplementing it. May also under-weight the statistical defect in §4.4 (p<.05 on n=9 vs. n=11) as "a minor reporting issue for the methods reviewer," when it is closer to an uninterpretable inference. The synthesizer should ensure R1's verdict on the quantitative strand is not softened by the EIC's framing.

### Reviewer Configuration Card #2

**Role**: Peer Reviewer 1 — Methodology
**Identity Description**: Professor of research methodology in a school of education, specializing in mixed-methods design in institutional and policy research; co-author of a widely used text on integration and joint displays in QUAL-quan designs, trained originally in survey measurement and psychometrics before moving into qualitative and mixed work. Regularly serves as the designated methods reviewer for two education journals and has published on the misuse of significance testing in small-sample educational research.
**Review Focus**:
  1. **Whether this is a mixed-methods study or a qualitative study with numbers attached** — the design is declared "mixed-methods" with the qualitative strand "primary" and the survey "supplementary," but no typology (convergent, explanatory sequential, embedded) is named, no integration point is specified, no joint display is offered, and §5 treats the survey finding as an unexplained "note of complication" rather than integrating it. Assess whether the two strands are genuinely integrated or merely co-located, and whether the "mixed methods" label in the title/abstract/keywords is warranted.
  2. **The quantitative strand's total under-specification, and the inferential claim built on it** — §3.3 says only that the survey "captured respondents' perceptions of institutional quality culture." There is no instrument named or reproduced, no item count, no response scale (M=3.9 on *what* range?), no scale provenance or adaptation history, no reliability estimate, no factor structure, no missing-data or response-rate reporting, no sampling frame. On top of this the paper reports "a statistically significant difference (p<.05)" between n=9 and n=11 subgroups without naming the test, reporting a test statistic, degrees of freedom, exact p, effect size, or any assumption check — and without acknowledging that 9+11=20 leaves 28 of the 48 respondents unaccounted for across three institutions. Determine whether this inference is salvageable or must be withdrawn.
  3. **Qualitative rigor: analytic procedure, sample adequacy, and the disclosed exclusion** — §3.4 describes thematic analysis in three sentences ("coding proceeded iteratively... until a stable structure was reached") with no analytic tradition named (Braun & Clarke? Framework? template analysis?), no coding-team or reflexivity account, no audit trail, no negative-case procedure. §4.1 then asserts themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" — but §3.1 describes *semi-structured* interviews, and thematic *emergence* is in tension with protocol-driven structure; these two claims cannot both stand. Most seriously, §4.4 states dissenting participants "were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure" — an explicit, self-reported deletion of disconfirming evidence, which inverts the standard qualitative obligation to *seek* negative cases. Assess what this does to the credibility of all three themes.
**Will particularly care about**: Whether the conclusions the paper draws are the conclusions this design can support. The methods reviewer's central question is not "is the sample too small" — 12 interviews can be plenty — but "did the analysis have any procedure capable of being wrong?" A three-theme structure that excludes the cases that don't fit it is unfalsifiable by construction, and a p-value with no test behind it is not evidence.
**Possible blind spots**: Likely to treat the §5/§6 over-generalization as downstream of the design flaws and hand it to the EIC, and may not register the *substantive* significance of what the excluded dissenters believed (that the QA enterprise as a whole lacks value) — which is a field-level finding, not merely a sampling issue. Also unlikely to catch the Delacroix (2018) citation reversal in §5, since that is a literature-integrity matter outside the methods remit. Both gaps are deliberately covered by R2 and R3.

## Review Strategy Recommendations

**Special characteristics of the paper requiring particular attention**

- **The polish is the trap.** This manuscript reads well. Prose is clean, structure is orthodox, quotations are vivid, the references are consistently formatted. Every one of its serious defects is *internal to the text* and visible only on a careful cross-section read — abstract against methods (14 vs. 12), ethics statement against findings (anonymity vs. re-identifying descriptors), §4.4's first sentence against its second (balanced representation vs. disclosed exclusion), §2 against §5 (Delacroix's argument against its inversion). Every reviewer should be instructed to read *across* sections rather than sequentially, and to treat surface fluency as no evidence of substrate reliability.

- **Self-disclosed defects deserve the heaviest weight, not the lightest.** Two of the most serious problems are things the authors state outright: that dissenting cases "were excluded for space," and that the study "achieved balanced representation... the full range of administrator views." These sit one sentence apart. Reviewers sometimes discount an admitted limitation as good faith; here the admission is embedded in a claim it directly falsifies. The disclosure is not mitigation — it is the evidence.

- **The Delacroix reversal is a single-point integrity failure with wide blast radius.** Because the manuscript itself states Delacroix's actual position in §2 *and* annotates it in the reference list, the §5 inversion is internally verifiable with no external lookup. It should be checked first, because it is the only support for the paper's principal recommendation, and because a confirmed reversal changes the prior for every other citation in the paper. Note also that all 12 DOIs fall in a single `10.5555/10420xx` block — a pattern warranting an independent existence check before any reviewer treats the bibliography as verified.

- **Unreconciled numbers should be enumerated, not narrated.** Interviews: abstract 14 vs. methods 12. Survey subgroups: 9 + 11 = 20 against a stated N = 48, with three institutions and only two named. Scale: M = 3.9 with no range given. Inference: p < .05 with no test, statistic, df, exact p, or effect size. Each is small; together they indicate the manuscript's numbers were not reconciled against each other before submission, which bears on whether the analysis was reconciled either.

- **Absence of methodological citation is itself a finding.** Twelve references, zero on method — no thematic-analysis authority, no mixed-methods design source, no sampling or saturation reference, no measurement citation for the survey. For a paper claiming a mixed-methods design, this suggests the design language may be post-hoc labeling rather than an executed plan.

**Potential complementarity or tension between reviewers**

- **Designed non-overlap.** R1 owns *procedure* (design integrity, statistics, analytic rigor, the exclusion as a methodological act). R2 owns *literature and contribution* (citation fidelity, coverage, construct definition, novelty against the paper's own bibliography). R3 owns *theory-outside-the-field, ethics, and consequence* (the unnamed decoupling frame, the re-identification breach, the single-party sampling of a three-party phenomenon, practitioner usability). The EIC owns *proportionality* — whether claim size matches evidence size, and whether the paper is reviewable at all. No focus area is shared.

- **Expected convergence — treat as a strength signal, not redundancy.** All four will independently reach the §5–§6 over-claim ("universities across the sector," "the higher education sector as a whole," "the first comprehensive account"), arriving from four different directions: proportionality (EIC), design warrant (R1), bibliography (R2), and sampling frame (R3). Four independent routes to the same conclusion is the strongest possible signal that this is the paper's central defect. The synthesizer should preserve all four framings rather than collapsing them into one comment.

- **Predicted live disagreement #1 — is the paper salvageable?** R2 will likely see a real if incremental contribution recoverable through honest reframing (drop "first comprehensive," define quality culture, fix the citations, reinstate the dissenters, position against Pettersen/Rahman/Silva & Tan). R1 will likely hold that the quantitative strand is unrecoverable as reported and that the disclosed exclusion undermines the qualitative strand at its foundation — pointing toward major revision at minimum, possibly reject. This disagreement is productive and should be surfaced in the panel matrix, not averaged into a middle verdict.

- **Predicted live disagreement #2 — how bad is the anonymity breach?** R3 will treat it as a publication blocker independent of scientific merit: identifiable individuals quoted saying professionally damaging things about their own institutions' compliance theater, in a study whose institutions have live regulatory relationships. R1 and R2 may register it as a revision item. R3's severity assessment should not be diluted by majority sentiment — this is a human-subjects matter where a single reviewer's expert judgment should carry, and R3 is the only panelist with IRB standing.

- **Coverage gap to watch — the excluded dissenters.** Both R1 (as a methodological act) and R2 (as a literature-contribution matter: what if the dissenters held the field-level view that QA lacks value altogether?) have partial claim to this issue, which creates the classic risk that each defers to the other and it lands underweighted. The synthesizer should confirm the exclusion is scored explicitly by at least one reviewer, and should note that the substantive content of the excluded views — not merely the fact of exclusion — is unrecoverable from the manuscript as written.

- **Tone calibration.** Maturity is "revised draft," not "first draft," so developmental hand-holding is not warranted — but neither is dismissal. The underlying study is legitimate and the interview material is genuinely interesting; the failures are of reporting discipline, citation fidelity, ethical follow-through, and claim proportionality. Reviewers should be direct and specific about each defect while making clear that a defensible paper is recoverable from this material at a right-sized claim. R3 in particular should frame the decoupling literature as an offer that would strengthen the analysis, not as a demand that the authors write a different paper.

## Contract Paraphrase

**D1 — methodology_rigor (mandatory).** From the editorial chair, this dimension asks whether the study's design, data handling, and reporting reach the threshold at which a journal in higher education studies would send the work out as credible evidence rather than as an opinion piece. My oversight interest is not the technical audit — that belongs to the methodology seat — but whether the manuscript's evidentiary base is visible and proportionate to the claims it wants to make about administrator perspectives on quality assurance implementation. For an interview- or survey-based administrator-perspective study, that means an identifiable design, an accounted-for sample and site set, a stated analytic procedure, and enough procedural detail that a reader could judge whether the findings are traceable to data at all. A manuscript of roughly 2,249 words is short for an empirical paper, so the editorially relevant question is whether the method is compressed but present, or effectively absent.

**D2 — domain_accuracy (mandatory).** This dimension asks whether the manuscript's use of the quality assurance and quality culture literature is faithful. In this specific field that means the core constructs — quality culture, compliance versus improvement orientation, internal versus external quality assurance, accreditation, accountability, enhancement — are used with their established meanings and are not silently collapsed into one another; and that where prior work is invoked (the EUA quality-culture tradition, Harvey and Green's conceptions of quality, national agency frameworks), it is represented as its authors actually argued it. As EIC I am the seat that notices when a paper announces engagement with a literature it does not actually cite, when attributions are vague or unattributable, or when domain terminology drifts. The specialist seat owns fine-grained factual verification; I own the question of whether the paper's stated relationship to the field's evidence base is honest.

**D3 — argumentative_coherence (mandatory).** This is the dimension most native to my seat: whether the paper holds together from title through research question, evidence, and conclusion. The title promises an account of *building* institutional quality culture through administrator perspectives, which sets an expectation the whole manuscript must honour — a stated question about administrators and quality culture, findings that speak to that question, and conclusions that neither exceed nor fall short of what the reported evidence licenses. My characteristic catch here is over-promising and under-delivering: recommendations for institutional practice or policy resting on an evidentiary base too thin to carry them, causal language attached to cross-sectional perceptual data, or a conclusion that answers a different question than the introduction posed.

**D4 — cross_disciplinary_relevance (high).** Higher education studies is read by a mixed community — institutional researchers, quality assurance practitioners, policy actors, and organisational scholars from outside the field. This dimension asks whether the framing, definitions, and implications are legible to that adjacent readership, and whether any claim reaching beyond the immediate sub-field (to organisational culture theory, to policy design, to management studies) is actually substantiated rather than gestured at. It also carries the internationalisation question that is squarely editorial: quality assurance systems are nationally specific, so a paper's reach depends on whether its setting is stated and its transferability argued rather than assumed. This is a high-priority, not mandatory, dimension — real deficiency matters, but it does not carry the same terminal weight as the three mandatory ones.

**D5 — writing_and_structure (normal).** This dimension covers organisation, clarity, the quality of any tables or figures, and conformity to venue conventions — whether the manuscript reads as a finished scholarly article. At 2,249 words the structural question is acute: does the paper allocate its limited space in the proportions a research article requires, or does it read as an extended commentary, a conference abstract, or a truncated draft? Length compliance and section completeness are ordinary editorial-desk concerns and sit with me. This is the lowest-priority dimension in the contract; it shapes the revision letter more than it shapes the accept/reject boundary.

## Scoring Plan

### D1: methodology_rigor

- `what_to_look_for` — Whether a research design is named and matched to the question (qualitative interview study, survey, mixed, document analysis, case study). Presence and description of participants: how many administrators, at how many institutions, in what national or systemic setting, selected how. Data collection specifics: instrument, protocol, duration, timeframe. Analytic procedure: named approach to coding or analysis, who analysed, any trustworthiness or reliability provision. Ethics and consent handling for human participants. Whether reported results are traceable to that stated procedure — quotes attributed to identified participant categories, counts consistent with the stated sample. Reproducibility affordances proportionate to the design: instrument availability, coding scheme, data-availability statement. Whether the manuscript's brevity is an honest compression of a real method or a substitute for one.
- `what_triggers_block` — The manuscript reports empirical findings about administrator perspectives with no identifiable method: no design named, or a sample and data collection so unspecified that a reader cannot tell how many people were consulted, where, or how the data were analysed. Also blocking: an analytic procedure asserted but wholly unexplained while conclusions rest on it; internally inconsistent methodological reporting (sample size, sites, or timeframe that contradict themselves or the results); results presented as findings with no evidentiary basis stated anywhere in the manuscript. Any of these means the evidentiary base is unassessable, and unassessable at the mandatory level is a block regardless of how reasonable the conclusions sound.
- `what_triggers_warn` — Method is present and identifiable but materially underspecified: design named and sample described, yet key procedural detail thin (sampling rationale unstated, analytic steps summarised in a sentence, no trustworthiness provision, ethics unmentioned). Reproducibility affordances absent (no instrument, no coding frame, no data-availability statement) where the field would expect them. Setting stated but sample composition given only in aggregate. These are gaps a revision can close, so they warn rather than block.

### D2: domain_accuracy

- `what_to_look_for` — Whether quality assurance and quality culture terminology is used with the meanings established in the field, and whether the paper distinguishes constructs it needs to distinguish: quality culture versus quality assurance system, internal versus external QA, compliance/accountability versus enhancement/improvement, accreditation versus audit versus evaluation. Whether prior work invoked in the framing is actually cited and correctly characterised — attributions to named traditions, frameworks, or agencies should be checkable. Whether the national or regional QA system the study sits in is described accurately, including how its regulatory instruments actually function. Whether stated empirical regularities about quality assurance in universities are presented at their real evidentiary strength rather than upgraded into settled fact. Whether the reference base is present at all and current enough for a field whose policy landscape moves.
- `what_triggers_block` — Substantive misrepresentation of the domain: a foundational construct defined contrary to its established meaning in a way that the paper's argument then depends on; a named framework, agency, or prior study attributed a position it does not hold; a description of a quality assurance system or regulatory mechanism that is factually wrong on a point load-bearing for the findings. Also blocking: claims about prior work made with no citations at all, so that the paper's stated engagement with the literature cannot be verified in any part — an unverifiable relationship to the evidence base is an accuracy failure, not merely a completeness one.
- `what_traggers_warn` — placeholder-free correction: see `what_triggers_warn` below.
- `what_triggers_warn` — Terminology used loosely but not wrongly: quality culture and quality assurance treated as near-synonyms in places, compliance/enhancement distinction implied but never drawn. Citations present but thin, dated, or narrow relative to the claims (for instance, a quality-culture framing carrying only a couple of supporting references). Prior work characterised at a level of generality that is defensible but unspecific. National context asserted rather than sourced. Individually correctable in revision, so these warn.

### D3: argumentative_coherence

- `what_to_look_for` — A stated research question or purpose, and whether the conclusion answers that question rather than an adjacent one. Consistency of the chain title → abstract → introduction → findings → discussion → conclusion. Whether each substantive claim in the discussion is anchored to a specific reported finding, and whether findings are anchored to reported data. **D1 — methodology_rigor (mandatory).** As Peer Reviewer 1 this is my primary charge: does the study's design actually license the inferences it draws? For a 2,249-word higher-education paper on administrator perspectives about quality assurance implementation, the operative question is whether the paper names a research design at all (qualitative interview study, survey, case study, mixed), and whether that design is matched to what "administrator perspectives" requires. The field's peer-review bar for qualitative institutional-QA work is trustworthiness (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability), not statistical power; for survey work it is instrument validity, response rate, and non-response bias. Ethics/IRB record and researcher positionality both live here, since administrator interviews inside universities carry a hierarchy-of-access problem. The word count matters: at ~2,249 words a paper cannot describe a method in reproducible depth, so I will judge whether the compression is honest (bounded claims scaled to a thin method) or concealing (strong claims on an undescribed method).

**D2 — domain_accuracy (mandatory).** From my seat this is not a literature-completeness audit (Reviewer 2's job) but a narrower methodological-truth question: are the domain constructs the study measures or codes defined the way the quality-assurance field defines them, and are prior findings represented in a way that survives contact with their original methods? "Quality culture" is a construct with contested operationalizations (EUA's structural/cultural distinction, Harvey & Stensaker's typology, Ehlers' dimensions); "quality assurance implementation" spans internal QA systems, external accreditation compliance, and AoL-style closing-the-loop. I read this dimension as firing when the paper conflates these — treating accreditation compliance data as evidence about culture, importing an instrument validated for one construct to claim another, or citing a source's conclusion while dropping the design limits that conclusion depended on. Terminological precision counts as evidence here, not as style.

**D3 — argumentative_coherence (mandatory).** My reading is the inference-chain audit: does each claim in the paper trace back to data the described method could have produced, at the level of analysis the data supports? The characteristic failure modes for this paper type are inferential rather than statistical — treating administrator self-report as evidence about institutional behavior or student outcomes (a level-of-analysis jump), reading cross-sectional interview data as a causal or developmental account of how quality culture "builds," selecting confirming voices while unreported cases stay invisible, and circular definition (quality culture defined by the practices that are then offered as evidence of it). ### Summary Assessment

Third, the participant count contradicts itself (abstract "Fourteen administrators" vs. §3.2 "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)"), and §3.5's "fully anonymized… no individual could be identified" is contradicted by §4.1's re-identifying role descriptors. The "mixed-methods" label is not earned: no typology, no integration point, no joint display, and §5 files the survey as a "note of complication." ### Strengths

1. **Research question is well-posed and method-appropriate.** §1 asks "How do senior university administrators experience and enact QA implementation, and how do their accounts illuminate the formation of institutional quality culture?" This is an experiential-meaning question, and semi-structured interviewing with thematic analysis is a defensible design for it. The alignment between question type and data type is one thing the paper gets right, and it is the foundation a revision can be built on.

2. **Multi-site sampling with deliberate institutional variation.** §3.2 samples across one large public research university, one mid-sized public teaching-focused university, and one private university. Deliberate variation in institutional type is a purposive logic that supports transferability better than a single-site convenience sample would, even though the paper never names the logic or states its selection criteria.

3. **Role-targeted recruitment matched to the phenomenon.** Recruiting "quality directors, associate deans, and registry heads" (§3.2) — actors with direct QA responsibility — matches the sampling frame to the research question at the individual level. The people interviewed are the people who do the thing being studied.

4. **Adequate basic interview procedure reporting.** §3.3 reports modality (in person or video call), duration (45–70 minutes), and that interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. This is thin but real procedural detail, and 12 interviews of that length is a defensible corpus for a thematic study — sample size is not this paper's problem.

5. **Ethics infrastructure exists in principle.** §3.5 reports institutional ethical approval and informed consent. The record exists; the problem is that the anonymization claim attached to it is falsified by §4.1, which is a fixable reporting-and-redaction failure rather than an absent-ethics failure.

### Weaknesses

1. **Self-disclosed exclusion of disconfirming cases, adjacent to a claim it falsifies (§4.3).** The manuscript states: "the study achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views… A minority of participants expressed more skeptical or dissenting views about the value of the entire QA enterprise; these were excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis." Why this is a problem: qualitative credibility rests on negative-case analysis — the obligation is to *seek* cases that strain the emerging structure and let them reshape it. Excluding cases *because* they fall outside the structure inverts that obligation and makes the three-theme finding unfalsifiable by construction; the reader cannot know whether the themes are what the data showed or what survived the filter. The stated reason ("for space") does not hold in a ~2,700-word manuscript, and the exclusion is not a limitation disclosed after the fact — it directly contradicts the "full range" claim one sentence earlier. How to improve: reinstate the dissenting participants, report how many there were, present their accounts, and either revise the theme structure to accommodate them or explain analytically why a distinct dissenting position coexists with the three themes. If they cannot be reinstated, the claim of balanced representation must be withdrawn and the themes reframed as a partial account of a screened subset.

2. **Quantitative strand under-specified to the point of being uninterpretable (§3.3, §4.4).** §3.3 says only that the survey "captured respondents' perceptions of institutional quality culture along with basic role and tenure information." There is no named instrument, no item count, no response scale (M=3.9 on what range? 1–5? 1–7?), no scale provenance or adaptation history, no reliability estimate, no dimensionality evidence, no response rate, no sampling frame, and no missing-data reporting. Why this is a problem: without a range, M=3.9 has no meaning; without reliability, SD=0.6 cannot be interpreted as measurement rather than noise; without a response rate or frame, the 48 respondents have no defined population of inference — so the abstract's "moderately positive institutional quality culture" is not a finding, it is an unanchored number. How to improve: either report the instrument in full (items, scale, provenance, reliability, response rate, frame, missing data) with the questionnaire as an appendix, or remove the quantitative strand and the "mixed methods" label with it. Both are honest; the current state is not.

3. **An inferential claim with no test behind it, on subgroups that do not reconcile (§4.4).** The paper reports "a statistically significant difference (p<.05) in perceived quality culture" between staff at the research university (n=9) and the teaching-focused university (n=11), then reads institutional type as "a possible moderator." No test is named, no test statistic, no degrees of freedom, no exact p, no effect size, no CI, and no assumption check (normality, variance homogeneity, or the ordinal nature of Likert data). Separately, 9+11=20 leaves 28 of the stated 48 respondents unaccounted for, and the third institution — a full arm of the design — is not compared at all, with no explanation of whether it was surveyed, whether respondents were unclassifiable, or whether the comparison was chosen after inspection. Why this is a problem: a p-value with no procedure behind it is not evidence a reader can evaluate, replicate, or dispute; at these subgroup sizes any point estimate carries an interval wide enough to include no effect, and an undisclosed selection of which two of three groups to compare is a live multiple-comparison and selective-reporting risk. "Moderator" is also the wrong construct for a two-group cross-sectional contrast with no moderation model. How to improve: withdraw the inferential claim as written. If retained, name the test, report statistic/df/exact p/effect size with 95% CI, state assumption checks, reconcile all 48 respondents by institution, report the third institution, and state whether this comparison was planned. Given n=9 vs. n=11, the defensible reporting is descriptive, with the difference framed as a hypothesis for future work.

4. **"Mixed methods" is a label, not an executed design (§3.1, §5).** §3.1 declares a mixed-methods design with the qualitative component "primary" and the survey supplementary, but names no typology (convergent, explanatory sequential, embedded), specifies no integration point, offers no joint display, and gives no timing or priority rationale beyond the word "supplementary." §5 then treats the survey as "a note of complication" — the two strands are co-located, not integrated. Compounding this, §4.4 states the survey "corroborated the qualitative picture" and then, in the same paragraph, that "the interview accounts had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture," which is divergence, not corroboration. Why this is a problem: the meta-inference is where a mixed design earns its label; without an integration procedure the reader cannot tell whether the divergence between administrator ritualism and positive staff sentiment is a substantive finding about levels of the institution or an artifact of two unrelated instruments measuring different things in different populations. How to improve: name the typology and the integration point, present a joint display mapping each qualitative theme against the relevant survey evidence, and treat the QUAL–quan divergence as an explicit meta-inference — or drop the label and present a qualitative study with descriptive contextual data.

5. **Analytic procedure not recoverable, and §4.1 contradicts §3.1 on how themes arose.** §3.4 describes the analysis in two sentences: "Coding proceeded iteratively, moving between transcripts and an emerging set of themes until a stable structure was reached." No analytic tradition is named, no coding-team or single-coder disclosure, no codebook, no audit trail, no reflexivity or positionality statement, no member checking, no peer debriefing, no negative-case procedure (consistent with §4.3, there was none), and no operational definition of "stable structure." §4.1 then claims the themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol," while §3.1 describes *semi-structured* interviews — and thematic emergence and protocol-driven structure are different claims about where the themes came from. Both cannot stand. Why this is a problem: with no procedural detail and no trustworthiness work, a second analyst could not approach this corpus and check whether the three themes are what the data support; "confidence that the pattern was not an artifact" (§4.1) is asserted rather than evidenced. How to improve: name the analytic approach and follow its reporting conventions; state who coded and how disagreements were handled; provide the codebook and interview protocol as appendices; add at least one trustworthiness procedure (member checking, peer debriefing, or audit trail) and report its outcome; reconcile the semi-structured/structured contradiction; and replace "emerged systematically" with an accurate account of how themes were derived.

### Detailed Comments

#### Research Questions & Hypotheses

The research question in §1 is clear, answerable, and appropriately experiential; no hypotheses are stated, which is correct for an interpretive design. The problem is not the question but its downstream drift. §5's second finding — "these findings demonstrate that universities across the sector treat QA as a negotiated accomplishment," and "what began as an inquiry into three institutions can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture" — answers a question the study did not ask and its design cannot address. The survey strand has no stated question or hypothesis at all, which is why its analysis has no plan: the subgroup comparison in §4.4 appears with no prior statement that institutional type was a variable of interest.

#### Research Design

The design is named ("mixed-methods") but not specified. Per the mixed-methods reporting conventions the label invokes, a reader needs the typology, the priority, the timing, and — most importantly — the point and procedure of integration. None is present. What is described is a qualitative interview study with a survey attached. The qualitative component is well matched to the question; the internal/external validity trade-off is unaddressed, which matters because §5 spends the paper's external-validity budget on a claim the design cannot fund. Alternative designs the authors could consider: an explanatory sequential design in which the survey's divergence drives a second qualitative round, or a straightforwardly bounded qualitative multi-site study with the numbers dropped. The second is achievable from the material already in hand.

#### Sampling Strategy

Twelve senior administrators across three institutions is a defensible corpus for thematic work — small samples in qualitative research are not a defect per se, and I do not score this as one. What is missing is the justification: no sampling logic is named (purposive? maximum variation? convenience via the QA offices that granted access?), no selection criteria are stated, and no adequacy argument is made — no saturation claim, no information-power argument, nothing beyond the count. Recruitment "through institutional QA offices" (§3.2) introduces a specific and unaddressed access-mediated selection risk: the office that runs QA selected who would speak about QA. That is precisely the condition under which the most critical voices are least likely to reach the interviewer — and it compounds Weakness 1, because the dissenters who did reach the corpus were then excluded. The survey has no sampling description at all: no frame, no invitation count, no response rate, no eligibility definition beyond "mid-level staff," and no institutional breakdown that reconciles to 48. The abstract's 14 versus §3.2's 12 is not resolvable from the text; a reader cannot tell which corpus produced the findings.

#### Data Collection

Interview modality, duration, recording, and transcription are reported (§3.3) — adequate for a compressed methods section. Not reported: the interview protocol or its domains (so the reader cannot judge how far the themes were seeded by the questions — directly relevant to the §4.1 "structured protocol" claim), who conducted the interviews, whether in-person and video interviews differed in any way that matters, transcription conventions, and any field-note or memo practice. For the survey, "captured respondents' perceptions of institutional quality culture" is the entirety of the instrument description. This is not a thin description; it is the absence of one.

#### Analysis Methods

Qualitative: see Weakness 5. The two-sentence account in §3.4 does not distinguish the procedure followed from any other iterative coding procedure, and "until a stable structure was reached" is a stopping rule with no operational content.

Quantitative — Step 4a, statistical reporting adequacy:

1. **Effect size** — absent. No d, no rank-biserial, no difference in means with CI. The magnitude of the reported difference is unknown to the reader.
2. **Confidence intervals** — absent throughout. No CI on M=3.9, none on the subgroup difference.
3. **Statistical power** — no a priori power analysis; at n=9 vs. n=11 the study is powered only for large effects, and a "significant" result at this size is as consistent with an inflated estimate as with a real difference. Not discussed.
4. **Assumption testing** — none reported. With Likert-derived scores at these subgroup sizes, normality and variance homogeneity are live questions and a nonparametric alternative would ordinarily be justified or ruled out. Neither occurs.
5. **Missing data** — not reported. The 28 respondents unaccounted for between the subgroups (9+11=20 vs. N=48) may be the third institution, non-respondents to the classifying item, or excluded cases; the manuscript does not say, so denominators are not reconstructable.
6. **APA format compliance** — non-conformant. "M=3.9, SD=0.6" lacks italicization and spacing; the scale range is unreported so the mean is uninterpretable; "p<.05" is a threshold statement, not the required exact p with test statistic and df.
7. **Red flags** — three. (a) Selective reporting: two of three institutional arms compared, with no account of the third and no statement that the comparison was planned. (b) Undisclosed analytic flexibility: "subgroup comparisons were examined where role categories permitted" (§3.4) describes exploration over an unstated number of subgroup splits, yet only one contrast is reported and it is the significant one — this is the multiple-comparison pattern, uncorrected and undisclosed. (c) HARKing risk: institutional type is introduced as a "possible moderator" in the results with no prior statement that it was a variable of interest.

**Statistical reporting completeness: Unacceptable.** The inferential claim in §4.4 should be withdrawn; the descriptive statistics can be retained only once the scale range, reliability, response rate, and institutional denominators are supplied.

#### Results Presentation

Qualitative results are presented as three themes with illustrative quotation, which is a recognizable and reasonable form. Two presentation problems bear on methodology rather than style. First, no participant-identification scheme exists: quotations are attributed by role descriptor ("one registry head," "one associate dean," "the quality director of the largest private university in the region"), so a reader cannot tell how many of the 12 (or 14) participants are actually represented, or whether one articulate participant carries a theme. #### Methodological Fallacies Detected

- **Confirmation bias / selective reporting (§4.3)** — disconfirming cases excluded by the author's own disclosure, precisely because they did not fit the analytic structure. Self-reported, therefore confirmed rather than suspected.
- **Multiple comparisons, uncorrected and undisclosed (§3.4, §4.4)** — subgroup comparisons "examined where role categories permitted," one significant contrast reported, no correction, no denominator of tests conducted.
- **HARKing (§4.4)** — institutional type presented as a "possible moderator" with no prior specification as a variable of interest.
- **Ecological / level-of-analysis error (§5)** — administrator self-report at three institutions generalized to "universities across the sector" and "the higher education sector as a whole." Individual accounts from a purposive sample cannot license a sector-level claim; the level of inference exceeds the level of measurement.
- **Reverse causation / cross-sectional causal inference (§5)** — "if quality culture is cultivated rather than mandated, then processes that maximize genuine participation should be favored" is a causal-mechanism recommendation drawn from one-shot cross-sectional self-report with no temporal or comparative structure.
- **Sampling frame mismatch (§3.2 vs. abstract/§6)** — the thesis is that quality culture is "an ongoing negotiation among administrators, academics, and external bodies," but only administrators were sampled. The design collects one party's account of a three-party process and reports it as a finding about the process.

2. **The core tension in §2 is correctly identified and correctly stated**: The Ferreira & Lund / Nakamura position (quality culture as cultivated through participation and trust) set against Delacroix's caution (participation itself becoming ritual) is a real and live axis in quality-culture scholarship, and §2 renders both sides accurately. That this is the section the paper later contradicts makes it more, not less, valuable — it establishes that the authors understood the literature at the point of writing it.

3. **The ritual-compliance theme is substantively plausible and well-evidenced in the interview material**: The "theater" and "feeding the machine" accounts are consonant with what the documentation strand (Iversen, 2021; Okonkwo, 2018) reports, and the quotations do real analytic work rather than decorating a pre-formed claim.

4. **Identity work is read with unusual care**: §4.3's observation that administrators hold the compliance/meaning tension open rather than resolving it — treating the gap as the site of professional identity — is a finer reading than "coping strategy," and is the closest thing in the paper to a genuine advance on Rahman (2020).

### Weaknesses

1. **W1 — Citation reversal on the paper's load-bearing recommendation (Delacroix, 2018)**: §2 states that Delacroix "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual, and argues *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of a healthy quality culture," and the reference-list annotation repeats this ("argues against relying on stakeholder consultation as evidence of quality culture"). §5 then writes that "our findings align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture," and adds "Consultation, on this reading, is the engine of internalization." The source's position is inverted, and Delacroix is the *only* citation supporting §5's practical recommendation. The consequence is worse than a bad citation: if Delacroix is right, the recommendation to "maximize genuine participation" may reproduce the pathology the paper diagnoses in §4.1 — consultation becoming one more artifact for the panel. **Fix**: rewrite §5's third point so it argues *with* Delacroix, not against him — i.e. specify the conditions distinguishing participation that deepens ownership from participation that becomes ceremony, which is a question the paper's own data could speak to. Then audit every other citation in §5 and §6 against §2. One reversal of this kind licenses doubt about all of them; the authors should demonstrate the others hold. *(This severity rests on no field norm — it is an internal contradiction verifiable within the manuscript's own text.)*

2. **W2 — The central construct is never defined**: "Quality culture" is the paper's object, its title concept, and its claimed contribution, yet it is only gestured at: §1 offers "the shared values, practices, and commitments through which an institution takes collective ownership of its own standards" as attributed paraphrase of Brennan & Osei (2020), and the paper never converts this into anything with observable purchase. Nothing in the manuscript states what would count as a quality culture being present, absent, strong, or weak. This has direct downstream costs. It is why §4.4's survey score ("M=3.9") cannot be interpreted as measuring *quality culture* rather than general staff sentiment; it is why the three themes cannot be assessed as evidence *about* quality culture rather than about QA work generally; and it is why §5's claim that quality culture is "a negotiated accomplishment" cannot be tested against any alternative. **Fix**: define the construct operationally in §2 — minimally, state the observable markers by which you would distinguish an institution that has one from an institution that does not — and then show which of your findings bear on which markers.

3. **W3 — The novelty claim is refuted by the paper's own bibliography**: §2 asserts that "sustained attention to the administrators who occupy the hinge between external requirement and internal practice" is "missing from this body of work," and §6 escalates to "the first comprehensive account of how university administrators experience and enact QA implementation." But §2 itself cites Pettersen (2022) on distributed leadership in the quality office, Rahman (2020) on identity and vocation among quality professionals, and Silva & Tan (2021) on administrators as quality intermediaries. The three findings map onto these one-to-one: ritual compliance → Iversen/Okonkwo, distributed leadership → Pettersen, identity work → Rahman. The paper does not say what it adds beyond confirming them in a new setting. **Fix**: drop "first comprehensive account" entirely; state the contribution as what it defensibly is — the first study (within your searched literature, and say so) to ground the Silva & Tan intermediary position in administrators' own accounts, and to show that the three previously separate strands co-occur in the same actors. That co-occurrence claim is real and is currently buried. Then say explicitly, for each theme, what your data show that Pettersen / Rahman / Iversen do not.

4. **W4 — Literature coverage has field-recognisable absences, and the paper's own explanatory vocabulary is uncited**: Twelve references, none before 2018, none after 2022, none methodological. Two absences matter domain-side. First, the paper's "ritual compliance," "theater," and "feeding the machine" language is the vocabulary of a specific and well-developed tradition — organisational decoupling and audit-culture scholarship — which the manuscript reinvents inductively from twelve interviews and presents as a finding. It is cited nowhere. Second, the quality-culture construct the paper builds on has an institutional lineage in European sector work (the EUA quality-culture project strand associated with Vettori and colleagues) and a regulatory anchor (the European Standards and Guidelines, or the equivalent national framework governing the unnamed system studied here) — neither appears, so the reader cannot situate what "quality culture" means in this paper against how the field's own producers of the term use it. Third, and more simply: the study's "single national system" is never named, which for a QA study is a substantive omission, since QA arrangements are jurisdiction-specific and the meaning of "external review" varies materially across regimes. **Fix**: engage the decoupling/audit-culture tradition explicitly (Reviewer 3 will have more to say on what it buys analytically); situate "quality culture" against the EUA-lineage usage; name the national system or state why it cannot be named and what that costs the reader.

5. **W5 — Disclosed exclusion of dissenting cases, read as a literature-and-contribution problem**: §4.4 states the study "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views," then in the next sentence discloses that participants with "more skeptical or dissenting views about the value of the entire QA enterprise" were "excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure." Reviewer 1 owns this as an analytic act; my concern is what was lost domain-side. Skepticism about whether the QA enterprise has value *at all* is not a residual case in this field — it is a recognised position in the critical QA literature and directly adjacent to the Delacroix argument the paper elsewhere cites. Excluding it means the paper's three-theme structure describes only administrators who have already accepted QA's premise, which narrows the contribution far more than "for space" implies. The apparent coherence of the three themes is therefore partly a product of the exclusion, not only of the data. **Fix**: reinstate the dissenting accounts and report what they held. If they will not fit the three-theme structure, that is a finding about the structure, not a reason to drop the cases.

## Review Body

The domain assessment turns on one finding and two structural consequences of it.

The finding is the Delacroix (2018) reversal. §2 of this manuscript states, in its own words, that Delacroix "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual, and argues *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of a healthy quality culture," and elaborates: "an institution can hold elaborate consultations and still lack any genuine ownership of standards; the consultation becomes one more artifact to be documented for the panel." The reference-list annotation says the same. §5 then writes: "our findings align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture. Consultation, on this reading, is the engine of internalization." No external lookup is required to establish the contradiction; the manuscript refutes itself across three of its own surfaces. Delacroix is the only citation supporting §5's operative recommendation, so the recommendation does not merely cite a source loosely — it is built on the source's negation. The substantive irony is sharp: §4.1's own data describe evidence portfolios that "nobody reads except the panel," which is precisely Delacroix's argument, and the paper's recommendation would push institutions toward producing more of exactly that.

The first structural consequence concerns the construct. A paper titled for quality culture, claiming to illuminate its formation, and reporting a numeric quality-culture score never says what would count as one. §1 gives an attributed paraphrase from Brennan & Osei (2020) and the manuscript never converts it into anything with observable purchase. This is why the referent can drift unnoticed from institutional property (§1) to perception score (§4.4) to negotiated process (§5): with no definition, nothing constrains the term. It is also why the paper cannot adjudicate its own central claim. "Quality culture is best understood not as a management output but as an ongoing negotiation" is a proposition about an object; without the object specified, the proposition cannot be tested against the alternative it rejects.

The second concerns the contribution. §2 declares sustained attention to administrators "missing from this body of work" in a paragraph directly following one that cites Pettersen (2022) on the quality office and Rahman (2020) on quality professionals' identity, and preceding a sentence citing Silva & Tan (2021) on administrators as intermediaries. §6 escalates this to "the first comprehensive account." The three findings correspond to these sources theme-for-theme. I want to be precise about what this does and does not mean: the correspondence is not evidence of bad faith, and confirming prior conceptual work with grounded first-person data is a legitimate contribution. But it is a different and smaller contribution than the one claimed, and the paper never states what it adds. The strongest available claim — that the three previously separate strands co-occur in the same actors, so that ritual compliance, distributed leadership, and identity work are not three populations but three faces of one position — is present in the material and currently unstated.

Two further domain matters. The manuscript's central explanatory vocabulary — ritual, theater, ceremony, the gap between documented and enacted practice — belongs to a developed tradition in organisational institutionalism and audit-culture scholarship which is nowhere cited; the paper reinvents it inductively and presents it as a finding. And the study's national system is never named, which for QA research is not a detail: the regulatory regime determines what "external review" is, and the paper's transferability cannot be judged without it. I record separately, as a verification lead rather than a finding, that all twelve DOIs occupy a single contiguous `10.5555/10420xx` block.

On the disclosed exclusion of dissenting participants, I take the domain half and leave the analytic half to Reviewer 1: administrators who doubt the value of the QA enterprise as a whole are not a residual category in this field but a recognised critical position — and one adjacent to the Delacroix argument the paper elsewhere cites correctly. Excluding them means the three themes describe only administrators who have accepted QA's premise. The themes' coherence is therefore partly an artifact of who remained.

The material here is worth publishing. The interviews are good, the vantage point is right, and §2 demonstrates that the authors read their literature accurately at the point of writing it. What has failed is the manuscript's discipline in carrying that reading forward into its own argument. That is recoverable, but the recovery is substantial: correct the Delacroix reading and rebuild §5's recommendation on it, define the construct, right-size the claim to what three institutions in one named system support, engage the tradition the paper is already using, and reinstate the dissenting cases.

- `what_to_look_for` — Whether the paper names its design and data source in a way an adjacent-field reader can evaluate: how many administrators, from how many and what kind of institutions, recruited how; whether instrument or interview protocol is described or appended; whether the analytic move from administrator accounts to claims about institutional quality culture is stated; whether the researcher's relationship to the studied institutions (insider/evaluator/consultant) is disclosed, since QA research is frequently done by people embedded in QA systems; whether limitations name the boundary of transferability rather than reciting generic caveats. I read these as *reportability and transferability* signals, deferring all statistical and design-validity adjudication to Reviewer 1.
- `what_triggers_block` — The paper presents empirical findings about administrators while giving essentially no recoverable account of who was studied or how the data were produced — no participant count, no institutional context, no data-collection or analysis description — such that an adjacent-field reader has no basis at all to judge whether the findings could transfer; OR the paper draws firm causal or prescriptive institutional conclusions ("X builds quality culture") from a design that cannot support them and offers no acknowledgement of that gap.
- `what_triggers_warn` — The design is identifiable and the basic parameters are stated, but one or more transferability-relevant elements are thin or absent: sampling logic unexplained, institutional type/national context unnamed, analytic procedure asserted rather than described, researcher positionality undisclosed, or limitations present but generic. Under-specification that a revision could repair is `warn`, not `block`.

### D2: domain_accuracy

- `what_to_look_for` — Whether "quality culture" and "quality assurance" are defined and attributed rather than assumed; whether the paper distinguishes quality culture from quality assurance compliance and holds that distinction consistently; whether QA regime features (accreditation cycles, external review requirements, institutional autonomy arrangements) are correctly scoped to their jurisdiction rather than stated as universal; whether cited constructs are used with their originating meaning intact; whether stated relationships in the QA literature are represented at the strength the evidence supports.
- `what_triggers_block` — A substantive factual or definitional error at the core of the argument: a central construct used in a sense contradicting its established meaning in the QA literature such that the paper's conclusions do not follow; a misattributed framework or misstated finding load-bearing for the thesis; or an accreditation/regulatory claim asserted as fact that is wrong for the context the paper operates in. I set this bar at *load-bearing* error, since detecting non-load-bearing inaccuracy at the margins is Reviewer 2's remit.
- `what_triggers_warn` — Core constructs used without explicit definition or attribution; "quality culture" and "quality assurance" blurred or used interchangeably where the distinction matters to the argument; jurisdiction-specific QA arrangements presented in universal language; prior findings characterized more strongly or more loosely than their sources support without visible overreach at the thesis level.

### D3: argumentative_coherence

- `what_to_look_for` — Whether the paper surfaces its implicit premises, particularly: that quality culture is unambiguously good and more of it is better; that administrator self-report indexes actual institutional practice; that compliance-to-culture is a maturity ladder rather than two distinct logics; that resistance or partial implementation is deficit rather than signal. Whether the inferential step from "what administrators perceive" to "what the institution's quality culture is" is named and defended. Whether conclusions and recommendations stay within the reach of the evidence presented, and whether disconfirming or divergent administrator accounts are reported alongside confirming ones.
- `what_triggers_block` — The paper's central conclusion rests on an unexamined premise that, once named, dissolves it — most concretely, institution-level or system-level prescriptive claims built entirely on one stakeholder group's self-report with no acknowledgement anywhere that this is a perspectival account rather than an institutional measurement; OR recommendations advanced with a confidence the presented evidence cannot bear and no limiting qualification anywhere in the paper.
- `what_triggers_warn` — Implicit assumptions are detectable but the paper partially hedges them; the perceptions-to-culture inferential gap is acknowledged in passing (a limitations line, a qualifying clause) without being worked through; conclusions mostly track the evidence but individual claims outrun it; only convergent accounts are reported with no engagement of variation or dissent among administrators.

### D4: cross_disciplinary_relevance

- `what_to_look_for` — Whether an adjacent-field reader (organizational studies, public management, implementation science, higher-education policy outside this QA tradition) can follow the framing without insider knowledge: acronyms and regime-specific terms glossed at first use, national/system context named early, the stake of the problem stated in terms that travel beyond accreditation practitioners. Whether stakeholders beyond administrators (faculty, students, quality officers, external reviewers) are acknowledged as holding materially different vantage points on the same implementation. Whether implications are drawn in a way another system or another sector could act on.
- `what_triggers_block` — The paper makes explicit interdisciplinary claims it does not substantiate — invoking organizational culture theory, institutional theory, or change-management frameworks as authority for its conclusions while engaging none of that work beyond a label — such that the theoretical framing is decorative rather than load-bearing; OR the framing is so bound to one unnamed accreditation regime's internal vocabulary that an adjacent-field reader cannot determine what the study is about or whether anything transfers. ### Summary Assessment

This is a decoupling study that never says so. "Ritual compliance," "theater," "feeding the machine," the gap between the documented and the enacted — §5 even uses the word *decouple* — is the Meyer & Rowan argument in its classic form, arrived at inductively from twelve interviews and reported as a finding. That is not a citation deficiency so much as a lost opportunity: without the frame, the paper cannot ask the questions the frame makes available, and its most interesting result (§4.3) gets the more comfortable of two available readings.

Two things must be fixed before this can be published in any form. First, §3.5 promises that "no individual could be identified," and §4.1 then quotes "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" saying professionally damaging things about their own institutions. Against §3.2's three-institution typology in one national system, those descriptors name people. Second, the paper's thesis is that quality culture is negotiated *among administrators, academics, and external bodies* — a three-party claim built on one party's testimony, with the dissenting members of even that one party removed from the corpus (§4.3, closing paragraph).

The underlying material is good and the study is recoverable at a right-sized claim.

### Strengths

1. **The interview corpus earns its keep**: "We produce beautiful evidence portfolios that nobody reads except the panel"; "a whole season of the year given over to feeding the machine"; "Half my job is convincing people this matters. The forms are the easy part." These are the kind of quotations organization-studies readers would cite. Whatever happens to the analysis, the data are worth the rework.
2. **Choosing the hinge actor is analytically right**: administrators occupy the position my own field studies as boundary or intermediary work, and public administration studies as street-level discretion. The choice is defensible on grounds well beyond higher education, and §1's framing of it ("neither the accreditors imposing standards nor the academics resisting them") is crisp.
3. **§4.3 is the paper's most original move**: reading the compliance/meaning gap not as a problem to be resolved but as "the space in which their professional identity was located" is a genuinely portable idea. It would travel to inspectorate staff, compliance officers, and clinical governance leads with almost no translation.
4. **The survey divergence is not smoothed away**: §5's closing paragraph reports that mid-level staff sentiment was *more* positive than the administrator accounts predicted and calls it a complication rather than folding it into the narrative. This is the one place the paper argues against its own interest, and it should be expanded rather than trimmed.

### Weaknesses

1. **An undisclosed theoretical debt costs the paper its best questions.** The three themes reconstruct, without naming, institutional theory's account of ceremonial conformity and loose coupling. Naming it would not weaken the contribution; it would let the paper ask what it currently cannot: *when* does decoupling stabilize rather than collapse, what makes ceremonial compliance rational rather than merely cynical, and — most consequentially — whether the identity work in §4.3 is a coping resource (the paper's reading) or the maintenance mechanism that keeps decoupling liveable for the people performing it. That second reading inverts the paper's optimism, and it is not considered anywhere. *Suggestion*: position the three themes against Meyer & Rowan (1977), Weick (1976), Power (1997), and Bromley & Powell (2012), and treat the alternative reading of §4.3 explicitly rather than leaving it to a reader from my field to supply.
2. **A live re-identification failure, not an abstract risk.** §3.5's assurance is falsified by §4.1's own descriptors. In one national system with the sample composition §3.2 describes, "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" are single individuals holding named posts, quoted saying their institution's QA is "theater, and everyone knows it's theater" and leaves "nothing behind that a teacher would recognize as helping them teach." These institutions have live audit relationships with a real regulator. I would also want to know whether the consent described in §3.5 contemplated attribution at post-and-institution granularity — in my experience it usually does not, and the assurance participants were given makes it unlikely here. *Suggestion*: strip role-plus-institution descriptors throughout, attribute at the level §4.2 already uses ("one registry head"), and member-check the identifiable quotations with those participants before resubmission. This is fixable in an afternoon and must be fixed regardless of the review outcome.
3. **A three-party thesis tested on one party — with that party pruned.** The abstract and §6 both claim quality culture is "an ongoing negotiation among administrators, academics, and external bodies." Academics were not interviewed; students, whose interests §4.3's participants invoke as their justification, were not consulted; external bodies were not approached. The survey does not close this gap — its respondents are mid-level *staff*, still one internal vantage. And the closing paragraph of §4.3 declares "balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views" one sentence before disclosing that participants who doubted "the value of the entire QA enterprise" were removed because they "fell outside the three-theme structure." Those are precisely the accounts that could have tested the thesis; their substance is now unrecoverable from the text. R1 owns what this does to the analysis methodologically. My concern is narrower and, I think, harder to repair: the paper's central claim is about a negotiation whose other parties were never asked, and the one party asked was edited to fit. *Suggestion*: either reinstate the dissenting cases and let them stress the theme structure, or drop the three-party framing and claim what the design supports — an administrator-side account of a negotiation.
4. **The operative recommendation may reproduce the pathology the paper diagnoses.** §5's practical conclusion is that "processes that maximize genuine participation should be favored," and its sole support is Delacroix (2018), described there as recommending "broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture." The manuscript's own §2 and its reference-list annotation say Delacroix argues the opposite — that consultation is frequently co-opted, becoming "one more artifact to be documented for the panel." R2 owns the citation-integrity audit; I am pointing at the practical consequence, which is severe. A quality director who acts on §5 as written will add consultation events to the evidence portfolio, which is exactly the mechanism §4.1 documents. The paper currently offers no conditions distinguishing participation that deepens ownership from participation that becomes an artifact — and that distinction is the only thing here a practitioner could actually use. *Suggestion*: restore Delacroix's actual position, then make the boundary conditions the contribution.
5. **Nothing in the paper lets an outside reader judge transferability.** The national system is never named (§3.2, "a single national system"), no QA framework or regulator is identified, and the survey's M=3.9 arrives without a stated range. Yet §5 asserts that "universities across the sector" behave this way and that three institutions "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture," with "administrators everywhere" facing the same tension. From outside the field this is the most conspicuous feature of the manuscript: the one descriptor concrete enough to locate the study ("the largest private university in the region") is the one that identifies a participant, while everything a comparative reader would need is withheld. *Suggestion*: name the system and its QA regime, state scope conditions, and scale §5–§6 to three institutions.

### Detailed Comments

#### Assumption Audit

- **Explicit assumptions**: The paper states that quality culture is a matter of internalization versus ritual (§1), and that participation cultivates it (§5). The first is defensible; the second is asserted, and asserted on a source that argues against it. §4.1 also states that the themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" and that this gives confidence the pattern is not a local artifact — from my vantage that inference does not follow, since a shared protocol producing shared themes is at least as consistent with the protocol shaping the accounts. R1 owns the analytic-procedure question; I raise it only because the confidence claim is doing work in the Findings.
- **Implicit assumptions**: Four go unexamined. *(a)* That more quality culture is straightforwardly better — never argued, though the paper's own participants suggest a plausible alternative in which ceremonial compliance is a rational settlement that protects teaching time from audit. *(b)* That administrator self-report indexes institutional practice; the paper moves from "how administrators experience QA" to what "universities across the sector" do without marking the step. *(c)* That compliance and culture sit on one continuum with culture as the mature end, rather than being two logics that coexist permanently — the survey/interview divergence in §5 is better explained by the second reading than the first. *(d)* That identity work is a coping resource. In the decoupling literature, the meaning-making that lets professionals live with a gap between the documented and the enacted is often what *prevents* the gap from becoming visible enough to force change. §4.3's data support both readings; the paper considers one.
- **Paradigmatic assumptions**: The paper treats sector-wide homogeneity as the natural inference from three sites ("administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension," §5) while simultaneously proposing institutional type as a moderator (§4.4). I flag this at the assumption level rather than as a consistency matter — the Devil's Advocate owns internal contradiction — because the underlying premise is the interesting part: a field that assumes QA regimes produce convergent institutional behavior will not look for the variation its own data hint at. Comparative and institutional-theory work would expect national regime, institutional autonomy, and funding dependence to condition all three themes.

#### Cross-Disciplinary Connections

- **Parallel research**: Regulated-sector research outside higher education has studied this exact object for four decades — hospital inspection, school inspectorates, financial compliance — and converges on the finding that formal structures adopted for legitimacy detach from operational practice. Espeland & Sauder's (2007) work on how rankings reshape law-school behavior is the closest parallel to Aoki's "steering at a distance" argument in §2 and would let the authors say something more specific about anticipatory reorganization than "the mere anticipation of external review reorganizes internal priorities."
- **Borrowing opportunities**: Three concepts would do immediate work here. *Ceremonial conformity* (Meyer & Rowan) reframes §4.1 from a deficit into a structural adaptation. *Loose coupling* (Weick) explains why the survey's mid-level staff can feel positively about a system administrators call theatre — the levels are not tightly linked, and that is the finding, not a complication. *Dirty-work identity defence* (Ashforth & Kreiner, 1999) is a close analogue to §4.3 and comes with an established caution: the identity strategies that make stigmatized or hollow work bearable also make it durable.
- **Methodological borrowing**: My field would ask for the other parties. A design that interviewed administrators, academics, and a regulator about the *same* review cycle would let the negotiation thesis be tested rather than asserted, and multi-site organizational research routinely manages this. Failing that, document analysis of the actual evidence portfolios against what administrators say happened would give an independent trace of the documented/enacted gap without adding participants. I recognize that single-stakeholder interpretive studies are normal in this field; my point is not that the design is wrong but that the *thesis* was sized for a different design.

#### Practical Impact

- **Real-world application**: A quality director reading this paper gets a vivid description of their own predicament and one instruction — maximize participation — that the paper's own literature review suggests may backfire. There is no guidance on the question they actually face: which consultations build ownership and which become artifacts. The paper is one revision away from answering that, since §4.2's material on persuasion and local buy-in ("It only works if the heads of department feel it's theirs") is exactly the evidence needed.
- **Implementation feasibility**: §6's recommendation that QA processes "create room for meaning-making rather than merely demanding documentation" is not implementable as stated, because documentation demands originate with the regulator and the institution cannot unilaterally relax them. The feasible version — which the paper's own data support — is about what institutions do *around* a fixed documentation burden. Naming the constraint would make the recommendation actionable rather than aspirational.
- **Stakeholders**: Academics appear only as the resisting party in others' accounts. Students are invoked by administrators as the justification for their vocation (§4.3) and are never heard from. Regulators are a structural presence with no voice. Casualized and part-time teaching staff, who absorb documentation burden with the least discretion to resist it, are absent from a paper whose thesis is about negotiation — and they are the party with the least power in it. Power asymmetry is not discussed anywhere in the manuscript.

#### Broader Implications

- **Ethical dimensions**: Beyond the re-identification failure in W2, the disclosed exclusion of dissenting participants (§4.3) is an ethical matter as well as a methodological one. Those participants gave time and candour on the understanding that their views would inform the account; their views were then removed for not fitting the structure, in a paragraph that simultaneously claims to capture "the full range." I would want the resubmission to state what those participants said, or to explain why it cannot.
- **Social impact**: If published as written, this paper tells regulators that ritual compliance is pervasive and that administrators everywhere experience it — a claim three institutions cannot support, and one a regulator could reasonably act on by tightening verification, producing more documentation rather than less. The paper should consider that its findings may be read by the party it is implicitly critiquing.
- **Future directions**: The single most valuable follow-up is not "a wider range of institutional types" (§6) but a study of the *same* review cycle from three vantages, which would convert the negotiation thesis from a framing into a testable claim. A close second is longitudinal: the questions that matter practically are when decoupling stabilizes into a durable settlement and what makes it collapse, and neither is answerable from one cross-sectional pass.

### Strengths (for fairness)

The interview material is genuinely good. The three themes — ritual compliance, distributed leadership, identity work — are plausible, well-illustrated, and the observation in §4.3 that administrators locate their professional identity *in* the unresolved gap between compliance and meaning is a real analytic move, not a restatement of quotations. ### Strongest Counter-Argument

Suppose I hold the opposite view: that this manuscript does not describe how quality culture forms, but demonstrates how a research design can manufacture a coherent finding out of an incoherent one. My case rests on the paper's own sentences.

§4.4 states that participants who dissented "about the value of the entire QA enterprise" were "excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis." Read the causal direction carefully: the structure did not emerge from the data and leave residue; the structure was applied, and the data that failed it were deleted. One sentence earlier the paper claims it "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views." Both sentences cannot be true. And the excluded view is not a marginal quibble — it is the rival hypothesis. If a subset of administrators believe QA has no value at all, the paper's central thesis (quality culture as "ongoing negotiation" in which administrators "work actively to recover meaning," §5) is refuted for that subset. The paper's account survives only because the disconfirming cases were removed from the account.

Now the parsimony test. The paper's explanation of its own data is that administrators construct meaning through identity work. A simpler rival: administrators occupying QA posts, recruited *through their own QA offices* (§3.2), talking to researchers about the value of QA, produce role-appropriate accounts — a self-presentational repertoire in which one first establishes credibility by naming the ritual ("it's theater, and everyone knows it's theater"), then demonstrates one's own exemption from it ("half my job is convincing people this matters"; "the paperwork is the price of admission, but it's not the point"). Every quotation in §4.1–4.3 fits this rival at least as well as it fits the authors' reading — and the rival additionally explains the survey anomaly the authors call a "note of complication," since non-QA staff have no such role to perform. The manuscript nowhere engages this. Third: the paper claims to study a "negotiation among administrators, academics, and external bodies" (§6) while interviewing only administrators. A negotiation described by one party is not a negotiation; it is one party's account of one.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths

1. **Role-appropriate self-presentation (strongest rival, see M1).** Administrators recruited through their own QA offices, interviewed about QA's worth, have a coherent account available: name the ritual to establish candour, then exempt one's own labour from it. ### Unexamined Premise

**That an account of QA given by the people whose职 position exists because of QA can be read as a description of QA.** Every administrator in this sample holds a post created by the quality regime. ## Review Body

This manuscript's defects are not distributed across it — they converge on a single pattern, and that pattern is what a devil's advocate is for. In four separate places the text makes a claim about itself that the adjacent text falsifies: the abstract's fourteen participants against Methods' twelve; §3.5's promise that no individual could be identified against §4.1's identification of two by post and institution; §4.3's "full range of administrator views" against the next sentence's disclosure that dissenting views were deleted; and §2's statement of what Delacroix argues against §5's statement of what Delacroix recommends. Individually these read as copy-editing. Together they are a reliability signal: the manuscript's assertions are not tethered to its own conduct, and a reader has no principled way to know which assertions are tethered and which are not. That is why I score three mandatory dimensions at `block` rather than accumulating warns.

Two of these are worse than reliability problems; they are argument-destroying. The Delacroix reversal (C2) matters because it is not a nuance drift but the source's position inverted, and because it is the only citation supporting the paper's sole practical recommendation. Correct it and the recommendation does not merely lose support — it acquires a cited opponent whose argument predicts that "maximize genuine participation" will reproduce the ritualism §4.1 documents. The recommendation, in other words, may be a live instance of the pathology the paper diagnoses, and the manuscript cannot see this because it has the source backwards.

The disclosed exclusion (C1) is the more serious of the two, and it is serious in a way that a limitations paragraph cannot repair. The sentence is explicit about causation: cases were removed *because* they fell outside the three-theme structure. A theme set that determines which data are admitted cannot be tested by the data it admits. This is not a small-sample complaint — twelve interviews can carry a great deal — it is that the analysis, as reported, contains no procedure capable of returning a negative result. And the excluded content is not incidental: administrators who reject the value of the QA enterprise are the standing refutation of a thesis built on administrators recovering meaning from it. Whether that refutation holds is unknowable from this manuscript, because the material is gone.

Against the three themes as reported, I press the rival the paper never names. Every participant was recruited through their own institution's QA office (§3.2); every one holds a post that exists because the quality regime exists; every one was asked by researchers to account for the worth of that regime. There is a well-worn conversational repertoire for exactly this position — establish candour by naming the ritual, then locate one's own labour outside it — and it generates all three themes from one mechanism rather than three. It also explains the finding the authors cannot explain: §4.4's survey shows mid-level staff *more* positive than the administrators' accounts implied, and the authors can only call this "a note of complication." Under the rival it is not a complication at all; non-QA staff have no such account to give. The manuscript's reading requires an unstated and untested premise — that these interviews describe institutional reality rather than professional narration — and it never marks the premise as one.

On scope, §5 ¶2 does not hedge, it demonstrates: "these findings demonstrate that universities across the sector treat QA as a negotiated accomplishment," extended to "administrators everywhere." Three institutions in a national system the paper does not name, recruited through the offices that agreed to be studied, cannot reach "everywhere." §6's "first comprehensive account" is refuted by §2's own bibliography, which cites three studies occupying the space the paper claims is empty and onto which its three themes map with uncomfortable precision. And the thesis in the abstract and §6 — quality culture as negotiation among administrators, academics, and external bodies — is asserted on one party's testimony. A negotiation described by one participant is that participant's account of it.

What survives all this is worth stating plainly, because the counter-argument is stronger when it concedes what it must. The interview material is real and the §4.3 formulation about identity located in the held-open gap is a genuine contribution. A defensible paper exists in this material — one that reads the interviews as data about how a profession legitimates itself under audit conditions, reinstates the dissenters as the analytic centre rather than the residue, right-sizes every claim to three institutions, and corrects Delacroix. That paper would be smaller in scope and considerably more interesting. It is not the paper submitted.

### Decision Rationale

Every mandatory dimension is scored `block` by at least one reviewer — in fact D1, D2 and D3 are each blocked by at least three of five — so F1 fires and the contract's action band is reject-or-major-revision. What settles the grade inside that band is not the count but the character of three repairs.

First, the thematic structure. All four non-DA reviewers and the DA agree the manuscript deleted the cases that would have tested its themes, and agree the deletion was caused by the structure the cases failed. Reinstating them produces a different analysis, not a corrected one; a revision round would be asked to approve a study it has never reviewed. Second, the quantitative strand: an inferential claim with no test behind it, on a scale with no range, against denominators that do not reconcile. Both reviewers who scored it (EIC conf 4, R1 conf 5) require withdrawal or full reconstruction; the other two deferred to that remit rather than dissenting. Third, the confidentiality breach. §3.5 promises that no individual could be identified; §4.1 identifies two, in posts, at institution types §3.2 has already enumerated, saying things that carry professional consequence in institutions with live regulatory relationships. Repairing it means going back to the consent basis and possibly to the participants — a clock no revision deadline governs.

I chose reject-and-resubmit over major revision against R2's and R3's reasoned dissent, and the arbitration is recorded above: both dissenters concede the excluded material must be restored from the authors' own data, which is the very step neither this panel nor a revision round can verify. I chose it over an unqualified rejection because the interview corpus is strong on all five cards and §4.3's reading of vocational framing as tension-holding rather than tension-resolving is, in the EIC's and the DA's words alike, an idea that would survive a complete rebuild of the claims.
