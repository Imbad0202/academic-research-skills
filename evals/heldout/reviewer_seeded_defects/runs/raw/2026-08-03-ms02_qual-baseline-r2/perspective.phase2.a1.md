## Review Body

I read this manuscript as an organizational sociologist of professional work who also runs institutional review for a national agency, so I approach it with two questions: whose account of quality does this paper reproduce, and what could a quality director actually do differently on Monday because of it. On the first question the paper has a structural problem it never acknowledges. On the second it has almost nothing to offer, and it does not seem to know that.

The vantage-point problem first. The paper centres administrators, which is a defensible and even overdue choice, and then treats their self-descriptions largely at face value. Administrators are not merely recipients of the audit regime; in every system I have worked in they are also its local enforcement arm. §4.3's image of administrators as "custodians of student interests against a tide of box-ticking" is exactly the account one would predict from actors with a stake in that account, and it is reported as a finding rather than analyzed as identity work in the sociological sense the section's own title invokes. The observation immediately following it — that administrators do not resolve the compliance/meaning tension but hold it open and locate their professional identity in the gap — is the best thing in the paper. It is also the most undertheorized. Street-level bureaucracy on discretion under conflicting mandates, the institutional-work and decoupling literatures on how actors sustain external legitimacy while buffering core practice, and the sociology of professional identity on coping strategies would each convert that observation from an anecdote into a contribution. None is cited. The paper claims in §1 that quality culture "remains under-theorized at the level of everyday administrative practice" and then declines to theorize it, borrowing the vocabulary of these traditions without their apparatus.

Meanwhile the people on whom QA actually acts are absent. Academics appear only as the objects of administrators' persuasion, students only as the beneficiaries administrators invoke to justify their own work, and the regulator only as an off-stage force. Yet §6 pronounces on how QA processes should be designed for all of them. A paper that sets out to "complicate the tidy managerial narrative" ends up telling that narrative from inside the quality office, in the quality office's own moral idiom. The silences are the finding here, and they are unexamined.

The sampling arrangement deepens this. Participants were recruited *through institutional QA offices* — the offices whose work is the object of study — and dissenting participants were then removed from the analysis because they "fell outside the three-theme structure." Structurally, the institutions curated the evidence about themselves and the residual criticism was filtered out at the analysis stage. Two sentences before disclosing that exclusion, §4.3 asserts that the study "achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views." Those two statements cannot both stand. I raise this as a political fact about the study and not only as a sampling technicality: the mechanism by which critical accounts disappeared from this paper is the same mechanism the paper describes institutions using on their own evidence portfolios, and the manuscript is not reflexive about the parallel.

On practice, §5 and §6 recommend processes that "maximize genuine participation" and "create room for meaning-making." I have sat in the chair these recommendations are addressed to. They tell me nothing about what to stop doing, what the substitution costs in staff time, which parts of my evidence base a regulator would have to agree to stop requiring, or how a panel would distinguish a meaningful process from a well-documented one. The paper diagnoses documentation burden across three sections without ever quantifying it — no hours, no headcount, no cycle length, no volume of artifacts. Worse, the single concrete prescription in §5 is attributed to the one cited author who argues against it: §2 and the reference annotation both record Delacroix (2018) as warning that consultation becomes another artifact for the panel, while §5 recruits Delacroix as recommending consultation as "the engine of internalization." An adjacent-field reader who follows that citation will import an inverted claim.

Finally, scope. Three institutions in one unnamed system, all apparently Anglophone and all with functioning, staffed QA offices, cannot license claims about "administrators everywhere." Institutions without a dedicated quality office, systems where QA is tied directly to funding or licensure, and non-Anglophone documentation regimes would plausibly generate quite different dynamics, and a reader in any of those settings currently cannot even tell how far their case sits from these three, because the system is never described. I note my own bias: my instinct is to ask for a larger, differently designed study. I have tried to keep the requests below to the version of this study that is fixable — rescope the claims to the evidence, name the system and its conditions, reintegrate the dissenters, describe the instrument, cite the literatures you are already using, and either operationalise the recommendations or drop them.

I flag for the panel that several findings below sit primarily in the methodology, domain-accuracy, and coherence seats' territory; I report them because they materially affect whether an outside reader can use this work, and I leave the dimension verdicts on those axes to their owners.

### S1: The "held it open" observation is a genuine contribution in waiting

§4.3 identifies administrators locating their professional identity in an unresolved tension rather than resolving it in either direction. That is a recognisable and non-obvious move for readers in organizational sociology and the study of professional work, and it is the observation on which a real theoretical contribution could be built.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "Rather than resolving the tension between compliance and meaning, they held it open"

### S2: The literature review stages a real theoretical tension rather than a consensus

§2 sets participatory-cultivation accounts against Delacroix's argument that participation itself becomes ritual, and uses that opposition to motivate the study. An adjacent-field reader can see immediately what is at stake and why the empirical question is worth asking.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §2 "between QA as participatory cultivation and QA as sophisticated performance"

### S3: The administrator/staff divergence is reported rather than smoothed over

§5 declines to harmonise the ritualism administrators described with the moderately positive staff sentiment, and instead names the level-of-institution divergence as the interesting puzzle. That is honest reporting of a disconfirming result and points at the right follow-up question.

**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "quality culture may be experienced quite differently at different levels of the institution"

### W1: Sector-wide generalisation asserted from three sites with no supporting evidence

The Discussion's second headline finding claims the study can be read as a general account of the higher education sector and that administrators everywhere face the same tension. No comparative, system-level, or cross-national evidence is presented anywhere in the manuscript, and the Conclusion compounds this by issuing sector-general design prescriptions. The fix is rescoping, not new data, but as written the paper's second of three claimed contributions is unsupported.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture" and "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension"
**Confidence**: 5 — routine judgement about claim scope versus evidence scope in institutional research.

### W2: The national system is never named or characterised, so transferability is unassessable

The study reports three institutions "in a single national system" and never identifies or describes it: not the regulator, not whether QA outcomes are tied to funding or licensure, not the documentation language, not the review cycle. Readers in other systems, and adjacent-field readers assessing whether these dynamics travel, have no basis for calibration. The Conclusion nonetheless invites cross-system application.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.2 and §6 — expected identification of the national QA system with its regulatory, funding, and documentation-language conditions; checked Abstract, §1, §2, §3.2, §5, §6, and References
**Confidence**: 5 — direct professional familiarity with how system conditions shape QA enactment.

### W3: Practical implications are non-operational and the documented burden is never quantified

The Abstract, §5, and §6 all promise implications for QA design, and deliver formulations at the level of "maximize genuine participation" and "create room for meaning-making." Nothing tells a quality director what to cease, what the substitution costs in staff time, what a regulator would have to relax to permit it, or how an external panel would distinguish a meaningful process from a well-documented one. The paper also diagnoses documentation burden repeatedly without a single quantity attached to it.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 and §6 "processes that maximize genuine participation should be favored" and "create room for meaning-making rather than merely demanding documentation"
**Confidence**: 5 — this is precisely the audience the recommendations address, and I cannot act on them.

### W4: The central recommendation is grounded in an inverted reading of its own source

§2 and the reference-list annotation both record Delacroix (2018) as arguing *against* treating stakeholder consultation as evidence of quality culture. §5 then aligns the paper's findings with Delacroix as recommending consultation as the central mechanism and "the engine of internalization." One of the two readings is wrong, the manuscript contains both, and the practical prescription rests on the version its own literature review contradicts. The domain-accuracy and coherence seats own the verdict; I record the cross-field consequence, which is that an outsider citing this paper will propagate the inversion.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §5 "who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture"
**Confidence**: 4 — the internal contradiction is unambiguous; which reading of Delacroix is correct I have not verified independently.

### W5: Dissenting participants were excluded while the paper claims full-range coverage

§4.3 asserts balanced representation and capture of the full range of administrator views, then discloses in the next sentence that sceptical or dissenting participants were excluded because they did not fit the three-theme structure. Disconfirming cases were removed *because* they were disconfirming, and the removal is paired with a false coverage claim. Participants were also recruited by the QA offices under study, so the sample was curated by the institutions being examined before any exclusion occurred. As written, the three-theme structure cannot be trusted as a description of what administrators said, and the coverage claim misrepresents the evidence base to readers. Reintegrating the dissenting accounts and deleting the coverage claim is the minimum remedy; the methodology and coherence seats own the dimension verdicts.

**Severity**: Critical
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "the study achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views" and "these were excluded for space"
**Confidence**: 5 — the contradiction is on the page, and selection-plus-exclusion effects on thematic structure are directly in my competence.

### W6: No academic or student data, yet the Conclusion prescribes QA processes for them

Academics and students are the populations on whom QA acts and on whose behalf administrators justify their work, and neither appears in the data. §6 nevertheless pronounces on what QA processes should look like to foster internalised commitment across the institution, and §5 generalises about how quality culture is experienced at different levels. Either the design must acknowledge that its prescriptions rest on administrator self-report alone, or the claims must be confined to administrative experience.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.2 and §4 — expected accounts from academics or students on whom QA acts, given the institution-wide prescriptions issued in §6; checked §3.2, §3.3, §4.1, §4.2, §4.3, §4.4, §5, and §6
**Confidence**: 5 — standard requirement that the population claimed about appears in the evidence base.

### W7: Borrowed adjacent-field vocabulary is used without its source literatures

The paper deploys "coping resource," "identity work," "distributed leadership," and a ritual/decoupling framing while citing none of the traditions that define them: street-level bureaucracy on discretion under conflicting mandates, institutional work and decoupling on legitimacy maintenance and buffering, and the professional-identity literature on coping strategies. An adjacent-field reader therefore cannot tell whether the paper's usage is compatible with those constructs or merely homonymous, and the paper's own claim to address under-theorisation goes unmet.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.3 "This vocational framing appeared to function as a coping resource"
**Confidence**: 5 — these are the literatures I publish in.

### W8: The survey construct is undefined, and a cross-level inference is drawn from it

The "institutional quality culture score" reported as M=3.9, SD=0.6 has no item wording, no response scale or anchors, no dimensionality, and no validity evidence, so no reader inside or outside the field can say what was measured. §5 then uses this single composite from 48 mid-level staff to license a claim about how quality culture is experienced "at different levels of the institution," and §4.4 offers a subgroup contrast as evidence that institutional type moderates quality culture. Both inferences move across populations and levels of analysis that the instrument was never shown to support. The methodology seat owns the rigour verdict; from my angle the undefined construct is what blocks outside use.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: absence: §3.3 and §3.4 — expected survey item wording, response scale anchors, and validity evidence for the quality culture score; checked §3.1, §3.3, §3.4, §4.4, and References
**Confidence**: 4 — construct description is squarely assessable; the statistical adequacy question belongs to another seat.

### W9: Quoted participants are identifiable, contradicting the anonymisation claim

§3.5 states that all data were fully anonymised so that no individual could be identified. §4.1 then attributes critical quotations to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university" in a three-institution sample containing exactly one private university and one research university. Those descriptors identify individuals holding named posts, and the quotations attributed to them are unflattering about their employers' practice. This is a straightforward pre-publication fix, but it must be made, and the ethics statement should be corrected to describe what was actually done.

**Severity**: Major
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §3.5 and §4.1 "all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis so that no individual could be identified" and "the quality director of the largest private university in the region"
**Confidence**: 5 — I administer participant-protection review as part of my institutional role.

### W10: The interview sample size is reported inconsistently

The Abstract reports fourteen interviewees; §3.2 reports twelve with an explicit n=12. A reader cannot establish the size of the evidence base, and the discrepancy also leaves open whether the two excluded dissenting participants account for the difference, which would materially change how the exclusion in §4.3 should be read.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: Abstract and §3.2 "Fourteen administrators were interviewed in depth" and "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)"
**Confidence**: 4 — the discrepancy is plain; its cause is not, and I may be over-reading a drafting error.

### W11: Most survey respondents are unaccounted for in the subgroup analysis

The reported comparison uses nine respondents from the research university and eleven from the teaching-focused university, twenty of forty-eight in total. The remaining twenty-eight are never allocated, and the private university, one of the paper's three sites, has no reported survey subgroup at all. Readers cannot tell whether the private institution was surveyed, under-responded, or was omitted from the analysis.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.4 "Comparing staff at the research university (n=9) with staff at the teaching-focused university (n=11)"
**Confidence**: 4 — arithmetic and reporting-completeness observation.

### W12: The systematicity warrant in §4.1 is a non-sequitur and misdescribes the protocol

§4.1 states that themes emerged systematically from "the structured protocol," giving confidence that the pattern was not an artifact of any single institution's circumstances. §3.1 describes semi-structured interviews and §3.4 describes iterative, emergent coding, so the protocol is misdescribed; and protocol structure cannot in any case establish cross-institutional non-artifactuality, which would require site-level comparison the paper does not report.

**Severity**: Minor
**Evidence Anchor**: text: §4.1 "These themes emerged systematically from the structured protocol"
**Confidence**: 4 — inference and description mismatch is clear on the page.
