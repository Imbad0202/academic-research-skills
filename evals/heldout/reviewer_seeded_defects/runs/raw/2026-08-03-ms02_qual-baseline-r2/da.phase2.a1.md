### D3: argumentative_coherence

score: block
trigger: "a conclusion generalised well beyond the described evidence base"
block_class: repairable

The Discussion converts an interpretive account of twelve administrators in three institutions of one national system into a claim about "universities across the sector" and "administrators everywhere," and the Conclusion adds a forward-looking claim about what QA design produces, for which the study contains no outcome evidence of any kind. Two further defects sit on the same load-bearing chain: the Discussion's design recommendation is warranted by a source the paper's own Literature Review describes as arguing the opposite, and the theme structure that carries the whole analysis is preserved by the acknowledged removal of participants who dissented from it. None of these requires new data to repair — retraction of scope, re-reading of Delacroix, and re-analysis of the existing transcripts including the excluded cases would suffice — so the defect is repairable rather than fatal, but the central argument as written does not reach its stated conclusion.

## Review Body

I read this manuscript looking for the shortest route by which a hostile reader could show that the paper's own text does not get from its premises to its conclusion. That route is short, and it does not require any external knowledge — every step of it is visible on the page.

The most damaging finding is that the paper's third and most consequential Discussion claim is supported by a citation reversed in meaning. Section 2 states plainly that Delacroix "cautions that participatory rhetoric can itself become a compliance ritual, and argues *against* treating stakeholder consultation as sufficient evidence of a healthy quality culture," and the reference-list annotation repeats that reading. Section 5 then asserts that the findings "align with Delacroix (2018), who recommends that institutions treat broad stakeholder consultation as the central mechanism for building a healthy quality culture," and concludes "Consultation, on this reading, is the engine of internalization." Both statements cannot be true. Worse, the reversed version is the one doing argumentative work: the paper's practical recommendation — favour processes that "maximize genuine participation" — is precisely the inference Delacroix is cited in Section 2 as warning against. Section 2 identifies the participation-versus-performance tension as the one that "animates the present study"; Section 5 then resolves it by simply asserting the participation side and attributing that resolution to the author who denies it. This is not a citation slip in a background paragraph. It is the warrant for the paper's design prescription, and it dissolves on inspection.

The second failure is scope. The study describes twelve (or fourteen — see below) administrators at three institutions in a single national system. Section 5 states that "these findings demonstrate that universities across the sector treat QA as a negotiated accomplishment," that the work "can be read as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture," and that "administrators everywhere face the same fundamental tension." "Demonstrate," "the sector as a whole," and "everywhere" are all quantifiers the design cannot license, and the paper knows this: its own Conclusion asks future research to "test these dynamics across a wider range of institutional types and national systems," which concedes that the present dynamics have not been established beyond the three sites. A paper cannot both claim generality and, one paragraph later, treat that generality as an open question. The Conclusion compounds this with a different species of overreach — a forward-looking claim that processes designed for meaning-making "are more likely to foster the internalized commitment that the language of quality culture promises." Nothing in the study measures internalized commitment, tracks any process redesign, or observes any outcome. This is a causal-predictive claim resting on interpretive interview data with no outcome variable.

The third failure is the most serious as a matter of analytic integrity, and I want to be precise about why. Section 4.3 asserts that "the study achieved balanced representation of perspectives, capturing the full range of administrator views on QA implementation," and in the very next sentence reports that participants with "more skeptical or dissenting views about the value of the entire QA enterprise" were "excluded for space, as they fell outside the three-theme structure that organized our analysis." The stated exclusion criterion is non-fit with the finding. Cases were dropped because they contradicted the structure those cases were supposed to help establish, and the paper then claims completeness of coverage on the strength of the remaining cases. Two claims elsewhere are directly falsified by this admission: that the analysis proceeded "until a stable structure was reached" through iteration between transcripts and themes (stability purchased by deletion is not stability), and that a distributed understanding of leadership "recurred across every interview" (a universal claim over a corpus from which non-conforming members were removed). Note that the excluded participants are precisely the ones whose accounts would bear hardest on the paper's central thesis that administrators recover meaning from ritual — the dissenters are the disconfirming case for exactly that claim.

Two rival explanations are never confronted. First, participants were "recruited through institutional QA offices" — the offices whose work is the object of study. Gatekeeper recruitment plausibly selects administrators invested in QA, which would generate the vocational and distributed-leadership themes as an artifact of sampling rather than as a finding about the population. Second, the paper's own third theme is that administrators construct professional identity through moral framing of QA work; an interview with an external researcher is exactly the setting in which such framing would be performed. The paper treats these accounts as windows onto experience without a sentence of reflexivity about that. Section 4.1's assertion that themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol, giving us confidence that the pattern was not an artifact of any single institution's local circumstances" does not repair this: it contradicts Section 3.1's "semi-structured interviews," it offers protocol structure as evidence against artifact when a fixed protocol would if anything increase the risk of imposing structure, and consistency across three institutions in one national system is fully compatible with a system-level artifact.

The survey component is presented as corroboration and is not. Section 4.4 opens with "The survey corroborated the qualitative picture" and then, two sentences later, concedes that "the interview accounts had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture." A result that disconfirms the anticipated pattern is being labelled confirmation. The honest reading, which the paper reaches only in passing, is that the survey diverges from the interviews. Separately, the subgroup contrast (n=9 versus n=11, drawn from an n=48 survey whose remaining 28 respondents are never located) reports "a statistically significant difference (p<.05)" without naming a test, reporting group means or dispersion, giving an effect size, or acknowledging that this is a comparison selected post hoc from several possible pairings; the instrument itself, its response scale, and its provenance are never described, so "M=3.9" is uninterpretable. On this basis the paper nonetheless nominates "institutional type as a possible moderator."

Finally, two integrity problems that an editor cannot pass over. The Abstract reports "Fourteen administrators were interviewed in depth"; Section 3.2 reports "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)." The reader cannot determine the size of the entire primary evidence base, and the discrepancy is uncomfortably close in size to the excluded dissenting group. And Section 3.5 guarantees that "all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis so that no individual could be identified in reported findings," while Section 4.1 attributes quotations to "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" and "the associate dean for quality at the public research university." With a three-institution sample and singular office titles, these descriptors identify individuals. The guarantee given to participants is broken by the manuscript that reports on them.

The paper's underlying observation — that administrators occupy an uncomfortable hinge and manufacture meaning to survive it — is worth reporting. Nothing in this review disputes that. What I dispute is that the manuscript as written establishes it, or that its stated conclusions are the conclusions its own evidence licenses.
