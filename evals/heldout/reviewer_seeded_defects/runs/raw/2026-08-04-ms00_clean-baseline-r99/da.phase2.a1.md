## Review Body

I attacked the obvious targets first, and the manuscript survived them. The causal language is genuinely disciplined: the abstract, Section 1, Section 5, Section 6, and Section 7 all decline the causal reading, and Section 5 volunteers the reverse pathway rather than burying it. The reported numbers also reconcile under recomputation: for r = .42 with n = 214, a Fisher-z interval is approximately [.30, .53], matching the reported [.30, .52]; the Spearman check at ρ = .40 is consistent with the Pearson estimate; and the sensitivity statement is in the right neighbourhood. There is no arithmetic contradiction between Results, Abstract, and Conclusion, and no drifting sample size. Authors should know that the standard adversarial openings — inflated effect, mismatched confidence interval, causal verb smuggled into the discussion, headline that contradicts the table — all failed here. That is not faint praise; most manuscripts of this shape fail at least one.

The paper fails elsewhere, and it fails in one recognisable pattern: a caution is *named* and then treated as *discharged*. Section 2 cites Vasquez (2020) on self-report diverging from logs, Delgado (2020) on the inseparability of perception and use, and Ibarra and Poll (2021) on context shaping both perception and use. Having named these, the manuscript proceeds as though naming were answering. The clearest instance is Section 5's practical implication. Two sentences after declaring the reverse pathway "equally consistent with the data," the manuscript recommends onboarding that "helps students see concrete usefulness." That recommendation is only coherent under the perception-causes-use direction — the precise direction the paper has just said it cannot distinguish. The hedge "suggested by, not proven by" calibrates confidence in a conclusion; it does not repair an inference whose premise the paper has disowned. Under strict agnosticism about direction, the correct implication is that the data license no onboarding recommendation at all, and the manuscript should either say so or supply the additional argument that makes the directional premise defensible.

The rival explanation that is never named anywhere is the sharper problem. Both variables are self-reports, collected from the same respondent, in the same instrument, on the same occasion, using adjacent Likert-type formats. Shared perceptual or presentational bias is therefore a live candidate generator of r = .42, independent of any relationship between the constructs. The Vasquez citation does not cover this: it is deployed only to limit generalisation to behaviour, whereas common-method variance threatens the association itself. The manuscript's own construct discipline makes this worse rather than better. Having correctly insisted in Section 2 that the outcome is *perceived* use, it has defined a study in which two perceptions are correlated — and then, in the abstract's terminal sentence, glosses the result as usefulness tracking "LMS engagement among undergraduates," reintroducing both the behavioural construct it disclaimed and a population it says it cannot reach. Section 3.1's recruitment channel compounds this: if the "course-announcement channel" is LMS-hosted, as Section 1's own description of LMS functions implies, then respondents were selected on the outcome variable, which bears on the coefficient's validity and not merely on its transportability. Section 6 files this under generalisation only.

No finding here reaches Critical, and I want to be explicit about why rather than manufacturing one. Applying the singleton test, each defect above is repairable by rewriting, added argument, or added reporting, and none of them, alone, unseats the paper's core claim — which is a bounded, correctly computed bivariate correlation in one described sample. The abstract's construct escalation is one sentence plus two recurrences. The onboarding implication can be deleted or reframed. Common-method variance can be argued about, bounded, or conceded. Selection on the outcome can be clarified by stating whether recruitment ran through the LMS. Jointly these are substantial, which is what the warn on D3 records; individually none is rejection-level, so the Critical band is correctly empty.

Three minor observations I am not tabling. The variance-shared statement in Section 4 ("The proportion of variance shared by the two measures was accordingly modest") never gives the number, when r-squared of about .18 is trivially available and more informative than "modest." Section 4 reports a median category, "a few times per week," that Section 3.2 never defines, since only the scale endpoints are labelled. And Section 1 supports "a substantial body of work" with a single citation, where Section 2 shows the authors have more available. Separately, the Section 3.4 sensitivity claim of "greater than .80 power" at r = .19 sits on the boundary rather than above it under a Fisher-z calculation; whether that matters is the methodology reviewer's call, not mine, and I flag it only to show the arithmetic was checked. I record no attempt in this manuscript to address, instruct, or influence reviewers; the text is clean on that axis. Findings M1, M4, and M6 below touch measurement and sampling, which belong to the methodology seat; I raise them strictly as unaddressed rival explanations and mischaracterised inferential limits, and defer the underlying methodological judgements.
