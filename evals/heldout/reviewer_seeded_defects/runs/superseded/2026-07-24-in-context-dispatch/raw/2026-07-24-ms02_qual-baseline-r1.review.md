### Overall Recommendation

### Confidence Score
5 — This is squarely within my editorial remit (quality culture, administrator perspectives, the performativity debate).

### Summary Assessment
The manuscript asks a genuinely useful question — how administrators, the actors caught between accreditors and academics, experience and enact QA — and organizes its interview material into three legible themes (ritual compliance, distributed leadership, identity work) that would interest this journal's readership. The topic fits *Quality in Higher Education* well and the writing is fluent. But as an editor I cannot send this out as-is, and not because the topic is weak: the paper repeatedly contradicts itself on facts that a reader must trust. The abstract interviews "fourteen administrators"; the Methods interviews twelve (§3.2). The Methods describe "semi-structured" interviews (§3.1) and "iterative" coding (§3.4); the Findings say themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" (§4.1). The Ethics section guarantees full anonymization "so that no individual could be identified" (§3.5); the Findings then identify a participant as "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" (§4.1). The Findings claim "balanced representation... the full range" and then say dissenters "were excluded for space" (§4). And the Discussion cites Delacroix (2018) as *recommending* consultation (§5) when §2 and the reference annotation say Delacroix argues *against* it. Individually each is fixable; together they undermine the paper's credibility. My decision is Major Revision because the underlying study is salvageable and valuable, but every one of these contradictions must be resolved before external readers can trust the account.

### Strengths (3-5 items)
1. **Timely, well-scoped question**: Centering the administrator "hinge" position (§2, final paragraph; §1) is a real gap — the paper is right that most QA work studies accreditors or resisting academics, not the reconcilers. This is publishable framing.
2. **Legible thematic structure**: The three themes (§4.1–4.3) are distinct, well-labeled, and each is anchored to quoted interview material ("It's theater, and everyone knows it's theater," §4.1; "You can't do quality *to* a department," §4.2).
3. **Engages the right debate**: The paper positions itself inside the participation-vs-performance tension (§2, ¶2) that animates current quality-culture scholarship — a debate this journal's readers care about.
4. **Fluent, reader-friendly prose**: Organization Title→Abstract→Intro→Methods→Findings→Discussion→Conclusion is complete and the writing needs no line-editing to be readable.

### Weaknesses (3-5 items)
1. **Cross-cutting internal contradictions (fatal to trust as written)**: The five contradictions listed in the Summary each break the Title→Abstract→Findings→Conclusion consistency chain. *Why it matters*: an editor cannot certify a paper that states two different participant counts or that both anonymizes and names the same person. *Suggestion*: reconcile every pair against the source data — decide the true N, the true interview structure, whether participants are anonymized (and if so, remove identifying role+institution descriptors), and whether dissenters are in or out of scope.
2. **Scope over-reach in the Discussion**: §5 (Second) reads the 3-institution study "as a general account of how the higher education sector as a whole constructs quality culture." *Why it matters*: this is exactly the over-promising an EIC screens for; the sample cannot license a sector-wide claim. *Suggestion*: rewrite to sample-bounded language ("across these three institutions").
3. **A reversed citation**: §5 (Third) has Delacroix (2018) recommending consultation as "the central mechanism"; §2 and the reference list say the opposite. *Why it matters*: misrepresenting a source is a domain-accuracy failure that a knowledgeable reader (or Delacroix) will catch. *Suggestion*: correct the attribution and rebuild the Discussion's design implication on a source that actually supports it, or reframe against Delacroix.
4. **Novelty overclaim**: "the first comprehensive account" (§6) and "balanced representation... the full range" (§4) are unbounded claims the paper does not substantiate. *Suggestion*: replace with bounded contribution language.

### Detailed Comments

#### Journal Fit
Strong fit for *Quality in Higher Education* in topic and readership. Fit is not the obstacle; internal reliability is.

#### Originality
The administrator-hinge framing is a genuine, if incremental, contribution. It is not "the first comprehensive account" (§6) — Pettersen (2022) and Rahman (2020), which the paper itself cites (§2), already study the quality office and QA-professional identity. The claim must be bounded.

#### Significance
If the contradictions are resolved and scope is honest, the paper offers a useful, quotable account of how administrators recover meaning from ritualized QA — of moderate but real significance to the field and to practitioners designing QA processes.

#### Structural Coherence
This is where the paper fails today. The five contradiction pairs (participant N; structured/semi-structured; anonymized/named; balanced/dissenters-excluded; Delacroix for/against) each violate coherence between sections. The survey's "corroborated the qualitative picture" (§4.4) is also loose: a positive M=3.9 that "coexists" with described ritualism is a tension, not a corroboration, and the paper half-admits this ("had led us to anticipate a more skeptical picture").

#### Title & Abstract
Title is accurate to the intended study. The abstract's "Fourteen administrators" is the first contradiction a reader meets — fix at source.

#### Conclusion
"the first comprehensive account" over-reaches; otherwise the conclusion reasonably restates the negotiated-accomplishment thesis. Ensure the final claims are bounded to the evidence.

2. Were interviews semi-structured (§3.1) or structured (§4.1)? The two imply different rigor claims.
3. Given §3.5's full-anonymization guarantee, how do you reconcile naming "the quality director of the largest private university in the region" (§4.1)? Should these descriptors be removed?

### Phase 2 — Paper-visible Methodology Review Report

## Methodology Review Report (Peer Reviewer 1)

### Reviewer Identity
Qualitative and mixed-methods methodologist in education research; thematic-analysis rigor and small-N survey inference.

### Overall Recommendation
Major Revision

### Confidence Score
5 — Methodology and statistical reporting are my primary expertise.

### Summary Assessment
This is a qualitative-dominant mixed-methods study: an interview strand analyzed thematically, plus a 48-respondent perceptions survey. The design is a reasonable choice for the research question, and the qualitative material is rich. But the methods reporting has two defects that are not polish items. First, the study contradicts itself on basic facts a replicating researcher needs: the participant count is 14 (abstract) versus 12 (§3.2), and the interview protocol is "semi-structured" (§3.1) but later "the structured protocol" (§4.1). A reader cannot reproduce, or even correctly describe, a study whose N and instrument type are ambiguous. Second, §4.4 reports a subgroup contrast — research-university staff (n=9) versus teaching-focused (n=11) — as "a statistically significant difference (p<.05)," but §3.4 says survey responses were "summarized descriptively," and no test is named, no test statistic or df is given, no effect size accompanies the p-value, and n=9 vs n=11 is severely underpowered for the claim. This is an unsupported inferential claim of exactly the kind statistical review exists to catch. Beyond these, the qualitative reporting omits the standard trustworthiness apparatus (coding reliability, saturation, reflexivity, member checking) and the survey strand omits its instrument (scale, items, reliability). I recommend Major Revision: the study can be rescued, but the inferential claim must be either properly supported or withdrawn, and the factual contradictions resolved at source.

### Strengths (3-5 items)
1. **Appropriate design for the question**: A qualitative-dominant interview study (§3.1) is the right instrument for "how administrators experience and enact" QA; the survey is correctly positioned as supplementary/contextual, not primary.
2. **Adequate qualitative depth signals**: 45–70-minute audio-recorded, transcribed interviews (§3.3) with senior QA post-holders is a defensible data source; the quoted material is substantive.
3. **Iterative coding described in principle**: §3.4 ("moving between transcripts and an emerging set of themes until a stable structure was reached") gestures at a recognizable thematic-analysis process.
4. **Ethics process stated**: §3.5 reports institutional approval and informed consent (though see anonymization contradiction, which is R2/DA territory on the integrity side).

### Weaknesses (3-5 items)
1. **Reproducibility-defeating factual contradictions**
   - **Problem**: Participant count is "Fourteen administrators" (Abstract) vs "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)" (§3.2). Interview type is "semi-structured interviews" (§3.1) vs "the structured protocol" (§4.1).
   - **Why it matters**: N and instrument type are the two facts a reader most needs to interpret and reproduce a qualitative study; contradicting them makes the method uninterpretable and casts doubt on how carefully the analysis was tracked.
   - **Suggestion**: Reconcile against the actual study log. State one N and one instrument type; if both structured and semi-structured elements existed, describe them explicitly rather than using the labels interchangeably.
   - **Severity**: Critical
2. **Unsupported inferential claim in the survey strand**
   - **Problem**: §4.4 asserts "a statistically significant difference (p<.05)" between research-university staff (n=9) and teaching-focused staff (n=11), while §3.4 says the survey was "summarized descriptively." No test is named, no test statistic/df is reported, no effect size or CI is given, and the subgroup Ns are very small.
   - **Why it matters**: A bare "p<.05" on n=9 vs n=11 with no test, no effect size, and no power consideration is uninterpretable and is a textbook statistical red flag (missing effect size; possible post-hoc subgroup on a descriptively-planned analysis). It cannot support the "institutional type as a possible moderator" claim built on it.
   - **Suggestion**: Either (a) report the analysis fully — name the test, give the statistic, df, exact p, an effect size with 95% CI, and address the small-N power limitation — and reconcile it with the "descriptive only" statement in §3.4; or (b) withdraw the significance claim and present the contrast descriptively as hypothesis-generating.
   - **Severity**: Critical
3. **Missing qualitative trustworthiness apparatus**
   - **Problem**: No coding reliability / second-coder check, no statement of thematic saturation, no researcher reflexivity, no member checking (§3.4 is one paragraph).
   - **Why it matters**: These are the standard evidence that a thematic analysis is disciplined rather than impressionistic; their absence weakens every theme's warrant. The §4.1 phrase "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" is not a substitute — it asserts rigor rather than documenting it (and invokes the contradicted "structured" label).
   - **Suggestion**: Add a paragraph documenting coding procedure (who coded, how disagreements resolved), the basis for saturation, and reflexivity on the researchers' relationship to the QA field.
   - **Severity**: Major
4. **Survey instrument undescribed**
   - **Problem**: §3.3 says the survey "captured respondents' perceptions of institutional quality culture" but never describes the scale — number of items, response format, the source/validation of the instrument, or its reliability (e.g., Cronbach's α). M=3.9, SD=0.6 (§4.4) is reported with no scale anchor.
   - **Why it matters**: Without the instrument, M=3.9 is uninterpretable (3.9 on what?), and the survey cannot be reproduced or trusted.
   - **Suggestion**: Report the scale, its provenance, item count, response anchors, and internal-consistency reliability; state the anchor for M=3.9.
   - **Severity**: Major
5. **Subgroup N accounting does not close**
   - **Problem**: Total survey N=48 (§3.2), but §4.4 reports only research-university (n=9) + teaching-focused (n=11) = 20; the private university's staff and the remaining 28 respondents are unaccounted in the subgroup analysis.
   - **Why it matters**: Unexplained attrition between the total sample and the analyzed subgroups is a reporting gap that further undermines the inferential claim.
   - **Suggestion**: Report the full role/institution breakdown of the 48 and explain which respondents entered the subgroup contrast and why.
   - **Severity**: Major

### Summary Assessment
Substantively, the paper is in live conversation with the right literature: it invokes the governance-technology strand (Harlow; Aoki; Delacroix), the felt-burden strand (Montero; Brennan & Osei), and the quality-culture strand (Ferreira & Lund; Nakamura), and it correctly identifies the administrator "intermediary" position (Silva & Tan, §2) as under-examined. That is the opposite of Delacroix's position, and it is load-bearing: the paper's central design implication ("consultation… is the engine of internalization") is built on the reversal. A knowledgeable reader — or Delacroix — will catch it immediately, and it discredits the Discussion. Additionally, "quality culture" is invoked more than it is operationalized, the literature is cited but rarely critically synthesized, and the contribution is overclaimed ("the first comprehensive account," §6) against the paper's own citations. ## Decision Rationale *

The decision is **Major Revision**, and it is not close. Under the sprint contract, F1 fires (a `block` on a mandatory dimension appears in three of the five seats — DA and EIC block on domain_accuracy and argumentative_coherence, Methodology and DA block on methodology_rigor), which alone forecloses Accept and Minor Revision; F2 (a majority-and-then-some of seats at warn-or-worse on ≥2 mandatory dimensions) fires as well. The Devil's Advocate returned four CRITICAL findings, so by Iron Rule #4 the decision cannot be Accept. Yet every seat independently recommended Major Revision, and no seat recommended Reject — because the manuscript's failures, though severe, are the *correctable* kind. Reject, per our standards, is reserved for fundamental unfixable flaws; here the flaws are internal contradictions (R1 and DA on the 14-vs-12 count and the structured/semi-structured label, EIC concurring), a reversed citation the authors are positioned to fix quickly since their own §2 states the correct reading (R2, EIC, DA on Delacroix), an anonymization breach that can be repaired by removing identifying descriptors (EIC, DA), and an inferential claim that can be either properly reported or withdrawn (R1, DA). The underlying study — administrator accounts of recovering meaning from ritualized QA, with a genuinely strong "identity work" theme (affirmed by EIC, R3, and even DA as a non-defect) — is worth salvaging. We chose Major over Minor because the defects touch the paper's factual spine and its central design recommendation, not merely its polish; we chose Major over Reject because none of the CRITICAL items is a design flaw that revision cannot cure. The revised manuscript will be sent for another round of review, with particular attention to whether the contradictions are resolved at source rather than papered over.
