### Summary Assessment
This manuscript takes a genuinely worthwhile angle — the administrator as the "hinge" between external QA requirement and internal practice — and writes it with unusual fluency. The three themes (ritual compliance, distributed leadership, identity work) are recognizable and the "theater" and "season of feeding the machine" quotes are vivid. As an editor I would want this paper's *argument* in my journal. But I cannot advance it toward acceptance in its current state, because the manuscript contradicts itself on facts that a reader checks in the first five minutes: the abstract reports "Fourteen administrators" while §3.2 reports "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)"; the survey is "48 mid-level staff" in the abstract and §3.2, yet §4.4's only inferential comparison uses n=9 + n=11 = 20 with 28 respondents unaccounted for. ### Strengths (3-5 items)
1. **Timely, well-motivated gap**: The Introduction (§1) and the closing of §2 frame the administrator's intermediary position crisply — "neither the accreditors imposing standards nor the academics resisting them" — and Silva & Tan (2021) is used well to set up the gap.

### Weaknesses (3-5 items)
1. **Abstract-to-body factual contradictions**: "Fourteen administrators" (Abstract) vs "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)" (§3.2); survey "48" (Abstract, §3.2) vs the 20 respondents actually used in §4.4. *Why it matters*: an editor cannot send to production a paper whose headline numbers disagree with its methods. *Direction*: reconcile every N across Abstract, §3.2, §4.4 and report the full survey breakdown.
2. **A reversed citation**: Delacroix (2018) is "against" consultation in §2 and "recommends" consultation in §5. *Why it matters*: it is a domain-accuracy failure and it makes the paper's central design implication (§5, "consultation is the engine of internalization") rest on a misreading of its own source. *Direction*: correct §5 to match §2, and re-derive the implication from a source that actually supports it.

## Methodology Review Report (Peer Reviewer 1)

### Reviewer Identity
Mixed-methods education researcher; interpretive thematic analysis + applied social statistics.

### Summary Assessment
The design — QUAL-dominant interviews contextualized by a supplementary perception survey — is appropriate to the research question, and the qualitative material is rich. But the methods reporting has defects serious enough to block acceptance on rigor grounds, and they are recoverable only by re-reporting (and in the survey's case, possibly re-analyzing). The sample size contradicts itself: the Abstract says "Fourteen administrators", §3.2 says "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)." The survey is reported as 48 (§3.2) but the only inferential result (§4.4) compares n=9 vs n=11 — 20 respondents — with no account of the missing 28, and the "statistically significant difference (p<.05)" is reported with no test statistic, no degrees of freedom, no effect size, and no confidence interval. On a subgroup of 9 vs 11 this is precisely the setting where an effect size and CI are indispensable and where a bare p-value is uninterpretable. Qualitatively, the paper claims the themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol" (§4.1) although §3.1/§3.3 describe *semi-structured* interviews and §3.4 describes iterative thematic coding — there is no "structured protocol," and no account of saturation, reflexivity, member checking, or coder agreement. These are fixable, but they are not minor.

### Strengths (3-5 items)
1. **Design-question fit**: A QUAL-primary mixed design (§3.1, "The qualitative component was primary; the survey served to contextualize") is the right choice for an experience-and-meaning question.
2. **Purposive recruitment through QA offices** (§3.2): sensible access strategy for this population, and the three-institution spread (research / teaching-focused / private) gives useful variation.
3. **Adequate interview dosage** (§3.3): 45–70 minutes, recorded and transcribed, is a defensible interview footprint for this kind of study.

### Weaknesses (3-5 items)
1. **Self-contradicting sample size**
   **Problem**: Abstract "Fourteen administrators" vs §3.2 "Twelve senior administrators (n=12)."
   **Why it matters**: a reader cannot tell what the study's N is; every downstream claim inherits the ambiguity.
   **Suggestion**: state one number, reconcile the abstract, and confirm the interview count in §3.3.
   **Severity**: Critical
2. **Survey inference is un-reportable as written**
   **Problem**: §4.4 reports "a statistically significant difference (p<.05)" for n=9 vs n=11 with no test named, no statistic, no df, no effect size, no CI; and 48 − 20 = 28 respondents are unaccounted for.
   **Why it matters**: a bare p-value on a 9-vs-11 subgroup is uninterpretable and unreproducible; the missing denominator raises a selective-reporting question.
   **Suggestion**: report the full respondent breakdown by institution, name the test, report the statistic + df + effect size + 95% CI, and address multiple-comparison risk if more than one subgroup contrast was run.
   **Severity**: Critical
3. **"Structured protocol" mislabels a semi-structured design**
   **Problem**: §4.1 says themes "emerged systematically from the structured protocol"; §3.1/§3.3 describe semi-structured interviews and §3.4 iterative coding.
   **Why it matters**: the phrase is used to license a claim of objectivity/systematicity the design does not provide.
   **Suggestion**: describe the actual analytic procedure; add saturation, reflexivity, and (if done) member-checking/coder-agreement detail; drop "structured protocol."
   **Severity**: Major
4. **No trustworthiness apparatus**
   **Problem**: §3.4 gives coding as "iterative... until a stable structure was reached" with no saturation criterion, no reflexivity statement, no member checking, no second coder.
   **Why it matters**: without these, the three themes cannot be assessed for dependability.
   **Suggestion**: add a trustworthiness paragraph; state how many coders and how disagreements were resolved.
   **Severity**: Major

The RQ (§1) is clear and answerable by the qualitative design.

#### Research Design
Convergent-ish mixed design with QUAL priority is appropriate; the integration point (survey "contextualizes" interviews) is stated but thin — see Results.
